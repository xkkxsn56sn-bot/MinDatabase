## Introduction

Few names in the history of medieval art carry the mythic charge of *Gislebertus.* Carved in bold Roman capitals along the lower edge of the mandorla that frames the enthroned Christ of the Last Judgment tympanum at the cathedral of Saint-Lazare in Autun, the two words *GISLEBERTVS HOC FECIT* — "Gislebertus made this" — have for more than a century been read as the proud signature of a lone genius, a Romanesque master who supposedly stepped out of the anonymity that shrouds nearly all twelfth-century craftsmen to claim his masterpiece. On this reading, Gislebertus becomes the exception that proves the rule: the one Burgundian sculptor whose name we know, and to whom, by extension, an entire cathedral's worth of carving — tympanum, lintel, and dozens of historiated capitals — can be attributed.

This essay sets out to examine that legend critically. It treats "Gislebertus" less as a settled biographical fact than as a problem — epigraphic, historiographic, and art-historical — that opens onto the whole world of Saint-Lazare: the Gallo-Roman city that framed it, the cult of Lazarus that occasioned it, the Cluniac architectural culture that shaped it, the extraordinary carvings that adorn it, and the modern scholarship that reconstructed, mythologised, and then contested the figure to whom those carvings are ascribed. Throughout, the aim is to keep three registers of knowledge distinct: what is documented or physically certain; what constitutes broad scholarly consensus; and what remains hypothesis or live debate. Where popular accounts and even some scholarly ones have repeated errors — the uncritical assumption that the inscription is an artist's signature, the romantic invention of a biography for Gislebertus, or the misdating of individual elements — these will be flagged rather than reproduced. The three scholars whose work most directly structures the modern understanding of Gislebertus — [Denis Grivot](/scholars.html#grivot-denis) and [George Zarnecki](/scholars.html#zarnecki-george), whose 1960 monograph created the canonical portrait of the sculptor, and [Linda Seidel](/scholars.html#seidel-linda), whose 1999 study dismantled it — recur throughout as the principal interlocutors.

## Augustodunum: The Historical Context of Autun and the Cult of Lazarus

To understand Saint-Lazare, one must first understand that Autun was, in the twelfth century, a city living amid the monuments of a far grander Roman past. Founded under the first emperor as *Augustodunum* — the name yokes Augustus to the Celtic *dunum*, "hillfort" — the city was established in the late first century BCE as the Romanised capital of the Aedui, the Gaulish people who had been allies of Rome, replacing their older hilltop *oppidum* at Bibracte. Augustodunum was a planned foundation of the first rank, ringed by some six kilometres of ramparts and endowed with monumental gates, two of which — the Porte d'Arroux and the Porte Saint-André — still stand, together with the remains of an immense theatre. At its height the Roman city was far larger than the modest town that succeeded it, with a population that has been estimated at anywhere from 30,000 to as many as 100,000, against the roughly 13,000–15,000 of the modern town. This is not incidental background. The visible antiquity of Autun conditioned the imagination of those who built and decorated the cathedral, and a significant strand of recent scholarship, above all Seidel's, has argued that Saint-Lazare was designed to assert both continuity with and dominion over this pagan and imperial inheritance, incorporating antique architectural vocabulary and even, in some readings, alluding to specific Roman monuments.

Autun had been a bishopric since late antiquity, and its first cathedral, dedicated eventually to Saint Nazarius (Saint-Nazaire), stood as the diocesan seat. The impetus for a second great church came from relics. In the second half of the tenth century — the traditional date given is 970, with the solemn translation dated to 1 September 972 — Autun acquired from Marseille relics believed to be those of Lazarus, the friend of Christ whom the Gospel of John describes as raised from the dead at Bethany. Here lies one of the foundational confusions of the whole enterprise, and it must be stated plainly as a matter of scholarly consensus rather than legend: the relics venerated at Autun were in fact those of a different Lazarus, Lazarus of Aix (sometimes called Lazarus of Marseille), a figure traditionally identified as a fifth-century bishop in Provence. The medieval cult conflated this Provençal Lazarus with the Lazarus of Bethany of the Gospels, brother of Martha and of Mary — the latter herself conflated in the Western tradition with Mary Magdalene, whose relics were claimed by the great Cluniac abbey of Vézelay not far to the north. The papal bull *Qui Christi Domini* issued by Pius VII in 1801 still enshrined the dedication to "Saint Lazarus raised by Christ" (*S. Lazari a Christo suscitati*), a testament to the durability of the identification.

The Lazarus cult at Autun thus existed in a web of relationships. It was bound to the Magdalene cult at Vézelay by the fiction of sibling kinship, so that a pilgrim might venerate the brother at Autun and the sister at Vézelay. It was bound also to the theme of resurrection and, through a second Gospel Lazarus — the sore-covered beggar of the parable in Luke 16 — to the care of lepers, so that Saint-Lazare drew both general pilgrims and the sick. And it was bound to the ambitions of the bishops and dukes of Burgundy, who saw in a flourishing pilgrimage both spiritual prestige and material advantage. The rivalry with Vézelay over the bones of the holy family was, as Seidel and others have emphasised, a competition over sacred capital, and the decision around 1120 to raise an entirely new church for relics already in the city's possession — a genuinely unusual step in the medieval period — must be read against that competitive backdrop.

## The Cathedral of Saint-Lazare and Its Construction

The new church rose on a site to the north of the old cathedral of Saint-Nazaire, on the high ground within the Roman walls, on land that the documentary tradition associates with a donation by the ducal house of Burgundy. The presence of Pope Callixtus II at Autun in 1119 is often connected with the availability of this land, the pope being kin to the Burgundian ducal family; the duke Hugh II, the pope's nephew, is named in the tradition as the donor. The driving force, by consensus, was the bishop Étienne de Bâgé, whose episcopate ran from 1112 to 1139 and who was closely tied to Cluny — a bond expressed literally in the fact that he retired to Cluny in 1139 and was buried in the choir of its abbey church. Construction began around 1120.

The building's chronology is anchored by a small number of relatively secure dates, though these should be handled with care, since the sources transmitting them are late and occasionally inconsistent. The church was consecrated by Pope Innocent II, who had been crowned at Autun at Christmas and consecrated the new church on 28 December — the year given as 1130 in some accounts and 1132 in others, a discrepancy that reflects the state of the documentation rather than any certainty. The building was substantially complete by 1146, when, on 20 October, the relics of Lazarus were solemnly translated into it. The great northern porch was added later, from around 1178. Saint-Lazare was raised to the rank of co-cathedral toward the end of the twelfth century, in 1195, functioning for a time as the "summer cathedral" from Easter to All Saints' alongside the older Saint-Nazaire, before eventually becoming the sole cathedral of Autun after the demolition of its predecessor.

Architecturally, Saint-Lazare belongs unmistakably to the Cluniac orbit. Its design retains the formulae developed for the *maior ecclesia* — the vast third abbey church of Cluny (Cluny III), begun in 1088 — transmitted through intermediary buildings, above all the priory church of Paray-le-Monial, which the scholarly tradition identifies as the most immediate architectural model. The plan is a Latin cross with an aisled nave, a transept, and a choir terminating in a semicircular apse. The elevation is articulated in three storeys, with a pointed barrel vault over the nave — the broken, slightly pointed arch being one of the features Burgundian builders drew from the Cluniac experiment and which would feed into the emergence of Gothic. The most conspicuous debt to antiquity lies in the fluted pilasters and the treatment of the wall articulation, which draw directly on the Roman remains of Augustodunum, the gates of which offered ready models. It is a commonplace of the literature, traceable to the guide written by one of our three scholars, Denis Grivot, that the interior of Saint-Lazare, despite its later Gothic accretions, is not Gothic in structure: it lacks the crossed diagonal ribs of true Gothic vaulting, employing instead the pointed barrel vault of the Cluniac tradition.

The building did not survive the centuries unchanged. A rapid initial construction obliged later reinforcement in the thirteenth and again in the fifteenth century, with flying buttresses added from 1294 onward; a fire in 1469 led to the wholesale rebuilding of the upper parts of the chevet and the erection of the tall Flamboyant Gothic spire under Cardinal Jean Rolin. Side chapels were inserted in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The west towers as they now appear are largely a nineteenth-century reconstruction, modelled on the Romanesque forms of Paray-le-Monial. The result is a monument that, in the frequently repeated phrase, "hesitates between Romanesque and Gothic" in its external silhouette, while its sculptural heart remains firmly of the twelfth century.

## The Last Judgment Tympanum and Its Iconography

The west tympanum of Saint-Lazare is one of the supreme achievements of Romanesque monumental sculpture, and its physical scale is part of its argument: the carved field measures precisely 4.62 metres in height by 6.53 metres across the base, and — as Fernand Auberjonois recorded in the documentation surrounding the Grivot–Zarnecki campaign — it was assembled from twenty-nine separate blocks fitted together, so large that it required a double lintel and a central supporting column, or trumeau. The subject is the Last Judgment, organised around the towering central figure of Christ enthroned within a mandorla borne aloft by four elongated angels. Christ's frontal immobility — arms extended, the whole figure rigidly symmetrical — is deliberately set against the agitated, attenuated, flame-like figures that surround him, a contrast between the impassivity of the divine judge and the frantic motion of the judged that is among the tympanum's most studied effects.

The composition divides, as the theme demands, into the saved on Christ's right and the damned on his left, the division reinforced by the very geometry of the field. To the left (the viewer's left, Christ's right), the apostles and the elect occupy a zone of relative order; an enthroned female figure, generally identified as the Virgin, presides above, and the blessed ascend toward the heavenly Jerusalem, some hoisted by angels, their heads appearing under an arcade of the celestial city, with Saint Peter overseeing the ascent.

Among the saved are pilgrims whose pouches bear identifying badges — one marked with a cross, another with the cockle-shell of Santiago de Compostela — a detail that roots the eschatological vision in the lived practice of pilgrimage that the church was built to serve. On the right unfolds the register of the damned: gaunt, sticklike demons, the torments of hell, and, at the centre of the lower zone, the weighing of souls, in which the archangel Michael and a devil contend over the scales while demons attempt to cheat the balance.

A frequently cited iconographic observation is that the scales at Autun hang from a small cloud rather than being held by the officiating angel, a configuration paralleled in Byzantine rather than Western imagery of the period — a detail that has been used to argue for the breadth of the sources on which the Autun designer drew.

The tympanum is a rare instance of a Romanesque monument that speaks in words as well as images, for it carries a remarkable series of Latin verse inscriptions. Around the mandorla runs the couplet, verbatim as transmitted by French heritage documentation,

> OMNIA DISPONO SOLVS MERITOSQVE CORONO / QVOS SCELVS EXERCET ME IVDICE POENA COERCET

"I alone dispose of all things and crown the just; those whom crime drives, I, as judge, restrain with punishment."

It is not accidental that the clause of reward falls on the side of the elect and the clause of punishment on the side of the damned.

The lintel below carries two further hexameter couplets. Over the resurrection of the elect:

> VISQVE RESVRGET ITA QVEM NON TRAHIT IMPIA VITA / ET LVCEBIT EI SINE FINE LVCERNA DIEI

"Thus shall rise again everyone whom an impious life does not drag down, and for him the lamp of day shall shine without end."

Over the damned:

> TERREAT HIC TERROR QVOS TERREVS ALLIGAT ERROR / NAM FORE SIC VERVM NOTAT HIC HORROR SPECIERVM

"Let this terror terrify those whom earthly error binds, for the horror of these images denotes truly what shall be."

The verb *disponere* in the mandorla couplet has attracted scholarly attention precisely because it is a legalistic, administrative term not applied to Christ in Scripture or the standard glosses, contributing to the sense that the Autun program was shaped by a sophisticated clerical intelligence.

This raises the important and often neglected question of who actually saw the tympanum, and how. It is easy to imagine every pilgrim passing beneath it, terrified into penitence; but the west portals of great churches were, for much of the liturgical year, kept closed, opened only on major feasts and for ceremonial occasions. For most pilgrims the ordinary point of entry was in fact the north portal, and it was there — the portal that carried the now-destroyed Resurrection of Lazarus and the surviving Eve lintel — that the everyday traffic of the cult flowed. The intricacy of the west tympanum's multi-figure composition and its versified inscriptions would in any case have been difficult to absorb from below at a steep angle.

This has led to the argument, advanced in the more recent literature, that the west portal functioned less as a routine entrance than as a solemn, ceremonial, and quasi-juridical space — a setting for the kinds of public acts, including forms of ordeal and judgment, that were staged before church portals. The eschatological program, on this view, addressed the community on its most charged occasions rather than serving as a daily catechism.

The dating of the tympanum remains genuinely unsettled, and honesty requires that the range be stated rather than a single date asserted. Estimates in the scholarly and museum literature cluster around two poles: an earlier bracket of roughly 1120–1130 and a later one of roughly 1130–1145. The Grove/Oxford tradition gives circa 1130–1140. The disagreement follows from the uncertainty over the consecration date and over the relative sequence of the eastern parts of the church and the west front. It is safest to say that the tympanum was most probably carved in the second quarter of the twelfth century, and to resist the false precision that so often attaches to it.

## The Inscription "Gislebertus hoc fecit" and the Debate over Identity

Everything that the modern imagination has built around the name of Gislebertus rests on a single epigraphic fact: beneath the feet of the judging Christ, on the tympanum, are carved the words

> GISLEBERTVS HOC FECIT.

Before anything is inferred from them, one common error should be corrected, because it bears directly on the interpretation. The inscription reads *hoc fecit* — "made this" — and not, as a surprising number of secondary sources and even parts of standard reference works have it, *me fecit*, "made me." The distinction is not pedantic; it sits at the very centre of the debate over what the inscription means.

The traditional reading, canonised in the mid-twentieth century, takes the words as an artist's signature and Gislebertus as the sculptor of the tympanum and, by stylistic extension, of most of the church's carving. This is the interpretation enshrined in the 1960 monograph by Denis Grivot, then choirmaster of Autun cathedral, and George Zarnecki, the London-based authority on Romanesque sculpture, whose book *Gislebertus, sculpteur d'Autun* — published in French in 1960 and in English as *Gislebertus, Sculptor of Autun* in 1961, with a second, expanded French edition in 1965 — created the modern portrait of the master.

Grivot and Zarnecki did not merely assert the signature; they built from it an entire artistic personality, attributing to Gislebertus's own hand the tympanum, the Eve lintel, and effectively the whole series of nave and choir capitals — a corpus the reference literature numbers at some sixty capitals in the interior and doorways — and they reconstructed a plausible career for him. They also anticipated the objection that signatures were unknown in the period, noting that inscriptions of this kind were not in themselves unusual in Romanesque France; what was extraordinary, in their account, was the prominence and position of this one, set beneath Christ rather than tucked away at the base of a column or on a capital, as earlier examples had been. That prominence they took as a measure of the esteem in which the artist was held.

The prestige of the Grivot–Zarnecki reconstruction was immense and immediate. The book was prefaced by Paul Deschamps, the leading French authority on medieval sculpture, and its photographs — by Gérard Franceschi — circulated internationally. By 1961 the popular press had seized on the figure; the culture minister André Malraux is reported to have pronounced Gislebertus "a Romanesque Cézanne," a phrase *Time* magazine took as the banner headline of its feature of 3 March 1961, which called the tympanum "the greatest achievement in Romanesque art by a single man." Gislebertus had become, in effect, the named genius that the modern cult of the individual artist required medieval art to produce.

It is precisely this modern manufacture that Linda Seidel set out to anatomise in *Legends in Limestone: Lazarus, Gislebertus, and the Cathedral of Autun* (University of Chicago Press, 1999). Seidel's book is, in part, a critical historiography: she demonstrated that the elevation of "Gislebertus, sculptor" to canonical status was a relatively recent event, that his identification as the sculptor dates only from the 1830s, and that it gathered force in the decade after the Second World War, in a climate — she argued — of French patriotic investment in the national artistic patrimony. For an earlier generation of iconographers concerned with commonly understood meaning rather than individual authorship, the question of who Gislebertus was had held little interest.

Seidel's more provocative thesis concerns the inscription itself. She proposed that "Gislebertus" names not the sculptor but a patron or benefactor, and specifically that the inscription should be understood in relation to the ducal house of Burgundy — the same tradition that names Gilbert, Duke of Burgundy, as a possible candidate. As reconstructed from her own summary and from the detailed review literature, the argument identifies two figures: a shadowy tenth-century count — corresponding to the historical Gilbert (Giselbert) of Chalon, count of Chalon, Autun, and other territories and de facto duke of Burgundy until his death in 956, a figure connected in her account with the original acquisition of the relics — and a much more obscure twelfth-century Gislebertus who witnessed a charter relating to a claim of the bishop of Autun.

On this reading, the inscription is a kind of "charter in stone," in which a well-placed individual authorises himself and the building by inscribing his name, and by invoking a real or claimed ancestor associated with securing Lazarus's bones, in the most prominent position the church afforded. Seidel supported this by embedding the inscription in the medieval culture of memory and lineage, and by reading the church as a whole as a "structure for engendering memory," in which even the antique borrowings served to recall a constructed local past.

Two supporting arguments have circulated around the patron thesis, and both must be handled with care because they cut in more than one direction. The first is philological. It has been argued that artists claiming authorship tended to use *me fecit* ("made me"), while *hoc fecit* ("made this") was more characteristic of donors and patrons — a linguistic hint, on this view, that Gislebertus was a benefactor.

Against this stands the equally serious objection that the standard and unambiguous formula for a patron who commissioned rather than executed a work was the causative *fecit fieri* ("caused this to be made"), of which Italian donor inscriptions offer numerous examples; the bare *fecit* is, if anything, more naturally read as the act of the maker. The philology, in short, is genuinely ambiguous and cannot by itself settle the question. The second argument concerns the very prominence of the inscription that Grivot and Zarnecki had read as a mark of artistic esteem: Seidel inverts it, taking the placement beneath Christ as fitting for a patron's commemoration rather than a craftsman's boast.

How has the scholarship responded?

The reception has been respectful but markedly sceptical of the patron thesis specifically, even where the book's historiographic and methodological contributions were praised. The most substantial specialist review, by [William Diebold](/scholars.html#diebold-william) in *The Medieval Review* (2000), granted that, given medieval conventions, it is entirely plausible that the Gislebertus of the inscription was a patron rather than an artist, and that the displacement of the artist by the patron was in any case old news to medievalists rather than the scandal some lay readers felt it to be.

But Diebold judged Seidel's two candidate patrons to be, in his exact words, "such ghost-like figures that they can't entirely dislodge even the slight weight of the deconstructed artist Gislebertus," and he faulted the argument for being unable to explain why this particular twelfth-century Gislebertus would have wished to memorialise that particular tenth-century one in so conspicuous a place. The wider methodological complaint — that the book erects and demolishes straw men of an older iconographic art history — recurs across the reviews, including [Christine Verzar](/scholars.html#verzar-christine)'s notice in *Speculum* (2001). It is fair to conclude, as a matter of the current state of the question, that Seidel succeeded in destabilising the confident biography of "Gislebertus the sculptor" without establishing "Gislebertus the patron" as its secure replacement.

What, then, can responsibly be said?

At the level of documented certainty, we have only the inscription itself and its wording; the name Gislebertus (Latin for Gilbert) is attached to the tympanum, and nothing in any contemporary document independently identifies the person it names. At the level of consensus, it is agreed that the traditional confidence in a fully reconstructed artistic personality was overbuilt, and that the romantic biography of Gislebertus — his training, his supposed movements, his temperament — is a modern construction rather than a medieval datum. At the level of live hypothesis stand both the surviving form of the sculptor reading and Seidel's patron reading, neither of which commands the field. The intellectually honest position is that the identity of Gislebertus is unknown, that "sculptor" remains the traditional and still widely held interpretation, and that the patron hypothesis is a serious, influential, but unproven challenge to it.

## The Capitals of the Nave and Choir and Their Iconographic Program

If the west tympanum is the monument's rhetorical climax, its intellectual and narrative richness lies as much in the extraordinary series of historiated capitals that run through the nave, the crossing, and the choir. The cathedral preserves a large ensemble of Romanesque capitals — the reference literature counts on the order of sixty carved for the interior and doorways — executed with an inventiveness and dramatic economy that rank them among the finest of the period. Owing to structural interventions in the nineteenth century, a number of the most celebrated capitals were removed from their original positions high on the piers and are now displayed at eye level in the chapter house (salle capitulaire), with replicas set in their former places; this accident of conservation has, ironically, made some of the greatest small-scale Romanesque sculpture unusually accessible to close study.

The subjects range across the Old and New Testaments, hagiography, moralised allegory, and the bestiary of the marvellous. On either side of the nave, scenes from the Hebrew Scriptures and the Gospels are set in dialogue, staging the recurrent Romanesque theme of the victory of good over evil. Among the most famous is the Suicide of Judas, in which the traitor hangs between two demons who haul on the rope, the tendrils of the capital's foliage themselves seeming to become the instrument of his death; the interpretation transmitted in the later scholarship connects his hanging to a "girdle of wickedness."

Equally celebrated is the group carrying the Infancy narrative: the Adoration of the Magi, the Dream of the Magi — in which an angel gently touches the hand of one of the sleeping kings, who lie together under a single coverlet, to wake them and point them to the star — and the Flight into Egypt, with the Virgin and Child mounted on the ass. The Flight capital is often singled out for the grave frontal serenity of the Virgin's face, and it is worth noting that the connoisseurial tradition, from [Denise Jalabert](/scholars.html#jalabert-denise) onward, observed a close kinship between that face and the face of Eve on the lintel, an observation that has been used both to support single-hand attribution and to explore the theological pairing of Eve and the Virgin.

Beyond the biblical narratives, the capitals include moralised allegory of the kind Cluniac art had pioneered — the combat of Virtues and Vices, drawn from the tradition of Prudentius's *Psychomachia*, with Charity and Patience enthroned in triumph over the crushed figures of Avarice and Wrath — as well as scenes of temptation, the questioning of Cain, the parable of Dives and the beggar Lazarus, the Widow of Nain, and a repertory of exotic and monstrous imagery reflecting the medieval "geography of the world."

The overarching character of the program is not a single linear narrative but a distributed one, in which meanings are made and remade as the visitor moves through the building. This is a point Seidel pressed against the older, textually-modelled iconography associated with the tradition of [Émile Mâle](/scholars.html#male-emile): rather than a fixed pictorial "book" to be read in sequence, the capitals generate associations through juxtaposition and through the bodily itinerary of the pilgrim, who might, for instance, be led to link the groupings of Holy Land place-names — Bethany, Bethel, Bethlehem — as a kind of virtual pilgrimage.

Even sympathetic critics have cautioned that some of the more elaborate readings — for example, the claim that the image of a fighting cock would summon, for a twelfth-century viewer, the Roman name of Gaul and the attributes of Gallo-Roman Mercury — presuppose an antiquarian sophistication more typical of the modern art historian than of the medieval faithful. Here again the epistemic layering matters: the subjects of the capitals are, for the most part, securely identifiable; their precise programmatic logic and their reception by contemporary viewers remain matters of interpretation and debate.

The question of attribution attaches to the capitals as acutely as to the tympanum. Grivot and Zarnecki assigned essentially the entire series of nave and choir capitals to Gislebertus himself, allowing him only a small number of assistants for the simpler ornamental work. This maximalism did not go unchallenged even in its own day: a French reviewer of the second edition objected in the mid-1960s that to attribute nearly all the capitals of Saint-Lazare to a single hand was to oversimplify a more complex reality of workshop collaboration, and the objection has largely been vindicated by later scholarship, which distinguishes an earlier campaign on the eastern parts of the church from the work of the atelier associated with the name Gislebertus. The stylistic coherence of the best capitals is real, but the inference from coherence to a single carving hand is an interpretive leap, not a documented fact.

## The Eve of Autun

Among all the sculptures of Saint-Lazare, none has exerted a stronger modern fascination than the fragment known as the Temptation of Eve, now the signal treasure of the Musée Rolin in Autun. Carved in the local limestone, the surviving relief — preserved in two blocks, together roughly 72 centimetres high and originally about 1.3 metres across the lintel — shows Eve nude and reclining, her body extended horizontally, her legs and lower body in profile while her torso turns toward the viewer, her head resting pensively on one hand as, with the other reaching behind her back, she plucks the fruit from a branch bent toward her by the clawed hand of the tempter at the right. Amid the sinuous foliage, a single leaf modestly veils her. The figure is celebrated as the first large-scale nude in monumental European sculpture since antiquity; its deep carving — projecting, in contemporary description, more than five inches from the ground, so that it is almost a figure in the round — and its serpentine grace invited comparison, in the effusions of twentieth-century critics, with El Greco and Gauguin (a parallel drawn by [Arthur Kingsley Porter](/scholars.html#porter-arthur-kingsley)) and with Cézanne (the comparison behind Malraux's "Romanesque Cézanne").

The Eve is a fragment of a lost whole, and its reconstruction depends on documents as much as on stones. It belonged to the lintel of the north (or lateral) portal of the cathedral — the portal that served as the principal everyday entrance for the faithful — whose tympanum depicted the Resurrection of Lazarus, the patron's defining miracle. A crucial piece of evidence is a text of 24 June 1482, discovered in the diocesan archives in the nineteenth century, describing that portal: in the tympanum, the story of the resurrection of Saint Lazarus in large stone images; below it, images of Adam and Eve; and on the pillar dividing the portal, a small mitred figure representing Lazarus as bishop, with further images "in the old style" beneath.

From this and from the surviving fragment it is generally reconstructed that Eve was originally balanced on the lintel by a figure of Adam, with the tempter between or beside them — though, strikingly, Adam has never been recovered, and Eve is exceptional in Romanesque art in appearing, as she now does, effectively alone. Grivot and Zarnecki proposed a fuller reconstruction of the portal, associating several fragments in the Musée Rolin with it and comparing its likely form to the subdivided tympanum at Cahors; their specific identifications, however, have been disputed, not least because some of the fragments they assigned to the tympanum do not obviously accord with the 1482 description.

The history of the Eve's survival is a small drama in its own right. In 1766 the canons of Autun, remodelling the church in the taste of their age and indifferent to what they regarded as barbarous medieval work, dismantled the north portal along with the tomb of Lazarus and the choir's medieval fittings. The central portion of the lintel was sold off as building stone; the block bearing Eve, broken in two, was built into the wall of a house on the Champ de Mars in Autun.

There it remained, hidden, for a century. In 1866, when the house was demolished, the relief came to light and passed to the architect Jean Roidot-Houdaille; on his death his heirs sold it, and after a series of transfers — including a period in the hands of the Autun choirmaster and antiquarian Abbé Terret — and some litigation over its ownership, it came to rest in the Musée Rolin, where it was materially accessioned in the twentieth century. It was classified a *monument historique* in 1935. In 2017 the museum devoted a major exhibition to the figure, occasioned by its cleaning and restoration and mounted in collaboration with the Louvre — an event that restored the warm colour of the stone and confirmed the exceptional preservation of the modelling.

The interpretation of the Eve has become a locus classicus of Romanesque iconographic debate, and it is here that some of the most influential readings of the whole monument have been developed — readings that, in keeping with the constraints of this essay, can be summarised without multiplying attributions. The older explanation of Eve's recumbent posture was simply pragmatic: the narrow horizontal field of a lintel dictated a reclining figure. A landmark study of 1972, however, argued that the posture was semantically charged, matching the humiliating prone position of penitents at the rite of public penance on Ash Wednesday, when notorious sinners presented themselves barefoot and prostrate before the bishop; on this view the north portal, with its themes of sin, resurrection, and forgiveness, framed an actual penitential ritual, and Eve's crawling body mirrored that of the penitent even as her sensually rendered nudity embodied the *concupiscentia* that theology named as the root of the Fall.

A further and much-discussed strand of interpretation, developed by Seidel among others, reads Eve's posture and expression — her calm, thoughtful face, in some descriptions marked by a tear — as deliberately evoking the penitent Mary Magdalene, and even the Virgin, so that the single figure gathers into itself the medieval triad of sinful, penitent, and redemptive womanhood, and points across the portal toward the resurrection of Mary's brother Lazarus. These readings are illuminating and widely cited; they are also, it must be said, interpretations built on a fragment and on analogy, and they remain contested in their particulars.

## Narrative in Stone: The Historiated Capitals of Saint-Lazare

### God's Reproach of Adam

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-08.jpg" alt="God's Reproach of Adam">
  <figcaption>God's Reproach of Adam, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Musée Rolin, Autun.</figcaption>
</figure>

The capital retains the Corinthian-derived architecture typical of the Autun workshop: large acanthus volutes unfurl at the upper corners, while slender fluted stems rise from the base to frame the figural scene, so that the sacred narrative appears to grow organically out of the vegetal order of the capital itself. At the left stands the Creator, identified by a cruciform nimbus whose segments are enlivened with delicately incised ornament — a decorative refinement characteristic of the finest Autun carving.

Draped in long, close-pleated robes whose parallel folds fall in the nervous linear rhythms of Burgundian Romanesque, he leans slightly forward and extends his right hand toward the newly formed man in a gesture at once of command and of animation. Adam, nude to the waist, stands at the right in a subtly contrapposto-like sway, his head inclined toward his Maker, one hand drawn to his body in a gesture of nascent self-awareness. Between the two figures rises a slim tree crowned by a cluster of fruit — an anticipation, perhaps, of the Tree of Knowledge and of the drama to come. The dialogue of gazes and hands across the intervening stem gives the scene its quiet dramatic tension, condensing the mystery of creation into two figures and a tree.

### The Death of Cain

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-09.jpg" alt="The Death of Cain">
  <figcaption>The Death of Cain: Lamech Slaying Cain, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun.</figcaption>
</figure>

The capital condenses one of the rarest narratives of the Romanesque repertoire — the apocryphal death of Cain at the hands of his descendant Lamech — into a single, densely woven field in which figures and foliage are inseparable actors in the drama. The composition is organised around a central vegetal axis: a sinuous stem rises through the middle of the basket, unfurling into the broad, deeply undercut acanthus leaves that are the hallmark of the Autun workshop, and it is within this thicket that the fatal misrecognition takes place.

At the right, Lamech draws his great bow, the weapon's curve echoing and amplifying the swing of his own body; the sculptor has caught him at the instant of maximum tension, the arms spread across the full width of the capital's face, the head bent along the line of the arrow's flight. According to the exegetical legend elaborated from Lamech's obscure boast in Genesis 4:23 — "I have slain a man for wounding me" — the aged archer was blind, guided to what he believed was a beast stirring in the undergrowth; the luxuriant foliage that engulfs the left half of the capital is thus no mere ornament but the very cover in which the supposed quarry crouches.

That quarry is Cain. Hunched at the left, his body folded upon itself among the leaves, the first murderer has already received the shaft: the arrow is shown transfixing his head — an unflinching literalism characteristic of the Autun carvers, who elsewhere hang Judas between his demons with the same narrative bluntness. The juxtaposition of the two moments — the bow still drawn, the arrow already lodged — belongs to the compressed, simultaneous time of Romanesque narration, in which cause and consequence share a single visual field.

The moral architecture of the scene would not have been lost on a twelfth-century viewer: the slayer of his brother is himself slain, blindly, by his own blood, so that the capital completes the arc of divine justice begun in the Cain and Abel narrative and rhymes, across the church, with the great theme of judgment enthroned on the west portal. The elongated proportions of the figures, the fine parallel striations of the drapery, and the nervous, flickering energy of the surfaces place the capital firmly within the stylistic orbit of the finest Autun carving.

### The Dream of the Magi

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-07.jpg" alt="The Dream of the Magi">
  <figcaption>The Dream of the Magi, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun (salle capitulaire).</figcaption>
</figure>

The Dream of the Magi is justly the most celebrated of the Autun capitals, and among the most tender inventions of Romanesque art. The problem the sculptor set himself was one of narrative economy: how to show three kings, asleep in one bed, at the very instant of angelic visitation, within the compass of a single capital. The solution is of luminous simplicity. The three Magi lie together beneath a single coverlet, only their crowned heads emerging in a gently rising diagonal, stacked one above the other so that the eye reads them at a glance as one body of sleep interrupted. The coverlet itself is a tour de force of carving: its fine parallel striations spread across the whole face of the capital in a great fan, at once bed-linen, compositional field, and rhythmic ground against which the drama unfolds — the sculptor has even distinguished the patterned border and the texture of the cloth, refinements that, in the capital's original position high on its pier, would have been all but invisible from the pavement below.

Above the sleepers, the angel descends on a single outspread wing, its feathers rendered scale by scale. With one hand it points upward to the star — carved as a great rayed blossom at the upper corner — while with the other it does not shake or summon but merely touches, with one extended finger, the hand of the uppermost king. That fingertip contact is the hinge of the whole composition: the touched Magus alone has opened his eyes, caught between sleep and waking, while his companions dream on. Rarely has monumental sculpture found so delicate a gesture for the intrusion of the divine into human rest — a warning delivered not as thunder but as the lightest imaginable pressure. The scene renders Matthew 2:12, the dream in which the kings are warned not to return to Herod; within the Infancy sequence of the Autun capitals it stands between the Adoration and the Flight into Egypt, and its quiet nocturne belongs to the same sensibility that gave the Flight its grave, frontal Virgin. Below the figural field, the capital retains its full Corinthian-derived apparatus of fluted stems and budding acanthus, the sacred dream literally blossoming from the architectural order.

### Adoration of the Magi

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-06.jpg" alt="Adoration of the Magi">
  <figcaption>Adoration of the Magi, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun (salle capitulaire).</figcaption>
</figure>

The Adoration unfolds across the face of the capital as a procession of gifts moving toward the enthroned Virgin, who occupies the place of honour at the right beneath a canopy of carved architecture — a turreted, arched structure whose crisply pierced masonry stands both for the house at Bethlehem and, in the visual shorthand of the period, for the dignity of the new dwelling of God among men.

The great rayed star is set immediately beside the arch, pinned to the architecture itself, so that the goal of the Magi's journey and the sign that guided them are fused in a single image. The Virgin, her head ringed by a broad plain nimbus, turns slightly outward, grave and monumental in the manner of her counterpart on the Flight into Egypt capital; the Child, seated across her lap, leans forward with both arms extended to grasp the offered vessel — not a passive recipient of homage but an eager participant, a touch of narrative naturalism entirely characteristic of the Autun carvers.

The three kings are disposed in a rising sequence that turns the constraint of the capital's trapezoidal field into an expressive device. The foremost Magus kneels — or rather sinks into a deep, springing genuflection — extending in both hands a great spherical vessel toward the Child; behind him the second, still standing, raises one hand toward the star even as he carries his rounded flask, his upturned face registering the astonishment of the journey's end; the third, at the far left, waits crowned and upright with his casket held before him.

The sequence of postures — kneeling, turning, standing — reads almost cinematically as three stages of a single act of homage, and the differentiated gifts (sphere, flask, casket) preserve the traditional triad of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. The drapery throughout is carved in the fine, close-set parallel folds of the workshop's finest manner, clinging to the bent leg of the kneeling king and breaking into agitated hems at the borders. As on its companion capitals, the scene was designed to be legible at a glance from the pavement far below, yet rewards close scrutiny — now possible in the salle capitulaire — with details such as the delicately patterned crowns and the little arcaded window of the canopy that no medieval viewer at floor level could ever have discerned.

### The Flight into Egypt

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-03.jpg" alt="Flight into Egypt">
  <figcaption>The Flight into Egypt, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun (salle capitulaire).</figcaption>
</figure>

Of all the Autun capitals, the Flight into Egypt is the one in which the workshop's union of narrative charm and hieratic gravity is most complete. The ass moves in steady profile from left to right, its legs caught mid-stride, its long ears pricked forward, the harness and the fringed saddle-cloth described with the carver's habitual delight in texture. Upon it, in radical contrast to the animal's lateral motion, the Virgin sits in absolute frontality: haloed by a great double-ringed nimbus, her veiled head slightly inclined, she gazes out of the scene and directly at the beholder with the grave, wide-eyed serenity that connoisseurs since Denise Jalabert have compared to the face of the Eve of Autun. She does not clutch the Child in maternal anxiety; rather she steadies him with one arm as he stands — himself frontal, himself haloed — upright before her, one small hand raised. In her other hand she holds a sphere, so that the group, in the very midst of its flight from Herod, composes itself into an image of the Virgin as throne of Wisdom, the *Sedes Sapientiae*: the fugitives are simultaneously an enthroned icon, and the journey to Egypt becomes a triumphal progress of the true King.

At the right, Joseph leads the way, his staff over his shoulder, hauling on the halter with the bent-kneed effort of a man walking hard — the one figure in the group given over entirely to the labour of the journey, his practical exertion setting off the miraculous calm of his charges. Foliage springing at the left corner marks the country through which they pass. Most curious of all is the ground beneath: the entire cortège advances not on rocky terrain but upon a row of large, delicately drilled rosettes — disc-like forms that have prompted long discussion, read variously as ornamental ground, as symbolic flowers marking the sanctified path, or even as wheels, an echo, on one hypothesis, of the wheeled processional images of the Virgin used in liturgical drama. Whatever their source, they give the scene its strange, gliding buoyancy, as though the Holy Family rolled through the world without touching it. The composition was among the most admired of the series even before the capitals' descent from the piers, and its pairing of frontal icon and profile narrative remains one of the most sophisticated solutions in Romanesque sculpture to the problem of showing, at once, an event in time and a truth outside it.

### The Suicide of Judas (The Hanging of Judas)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-04.jpg" alt="Suicide of Judas">
  <figcaption>The Suicide of Judas, historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130, Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun (salle capitulaire).</figcaption>
</figure>

Among the moralising capitals of Saint-Lazare, none is more pitiless than the Hanging of Judas. The traitor hangs at the centre of the field, naked, his body a limp vertical of dead weight: the head has fallen heavily to one side, the mouth gapes, the arms dangle useless along the flanks, the feet trail without purchase on the ground. He is suspended from the fork of a slender tree that rises behind him and breaks into foliage at the top of the capital — but the sculptor has made the mechanics of the death emphatically diabolical. At either side crouches a winged demon, shaggy-bodied and grimacing, and each grips one of the taut cords from which the corpse swings, hauling with the braced, squatting effort of workmen at a windlass.

The invention is theologically pointed: Matthew 27:5 says only that Judas "went and hanged himself," but the carver makes visible the doctrine beneath the verse — that despair is itself a demonic work, and that the suicide's rope is pulled, in truth, by hell. The demons' masks are studies in complementary malice, one turned outward toward the viewer with hollowed eyes and open maw, the other in profile, intent upon the labour; their membranous wings rise like blades against the corners of the basket.

The vegetal apparatus of the capital participates fully in the judgment. Great acanthus fronds curl in from the upper corners, each terminating in the drilled, cone-like rosettes familiar from the workshop's finest carving, and the hanging-tree itself sprouts leaves and seed-clusters directly above the traitor's fallen head — the living order of creation framing, with terrible indifference, the self-destruction at its centre. Within the church's distributed program the capital reads as the dark inversion of the penitential themes gathered at the north portal: Judas is precisely the sinner whose remorse curdled into despair rather than turning toward mercy, the counter-example to the crawling Eve and the risen Lazarus.

Set against the Weighing of Souls on the west tympanum, where demons cheat at the scales for each single soul, the scene shows the same infernal appetite operating within the Gospel narrative itself. The stylistic kinship with the best of the series — the deep undercutting, the wiry musculature of the demons, the dramatic economy that tells the whole story in three figures and a rope — placed the capital, for the older connoisseurship, at the heart of the "Gislebertus" corpus; it remains one of the most reproduced and discussed of all Romanesque capitals.

### The Triumph of the Virtues over the Vices

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gislebertus/gislebertus-05.jpg" alt="The Triumph of the Virtues over the Vices">
  <figcaption>The Triumph of the Virtues over the Vices (Charity over Avarice, Patience over Wrath), historiated capital in Autun limestone, 1125–1130. Cathedral of Saint-Lazare, Autun (salle capitulaire).</figcaption>
</figure>

Where the narrative capitals of Saint-Lazare compress events into drama, this capital compresses doctrine into diagram. A central tree — its trunk rising the full height of the basket before breaking into the workshop's familiar drilled cones and feathered acanthus — divides the field into two symmetrical halves, and upon this vegetal axis the sculptor has hung a fourfold moral architecture: four figures at the four corners, the Vices crouched below, the Virtues erect above, each of the standing figures resting its weight upon the head of the sin it has mastered. The scheme descends from the tradition of Prudentius's Psychomachia, the late-antique allegorical poem of the soul's battle, but in the form that Cluniac art had given it: not the melee of combat, but its aftermath — the Virtue as victor standing upon the vanquished, in the immemorial posture of triumph that Romanesque carvers knew equally from images of Christ treading the beasts.

The left half is the more explicit, and its identification is secured by the diocesan documentation: below, Avarice squats with gaping mouth, reduced almost to a mask of appetite, and clutches a swollen, tightly bound purse — the money-bag that is her canonical attribute across Romanesque art, and that reappears, tied to the traitor's rope, in the Judas capital of the same series. Above her, Charity — draped in tunic and mantle where the vices are bestial and exposed — leans upon the sinner's head, her quiet, almost negligent poise making the point that virtue's victory is not effortful but constitutional. On the right half the same formula is repeated with variations: a youthful, upright figure stands full-length, feet planted on the skull of the creature beneath — a hairy, winged, open-jawed demon-beast crouched on all fours, sin rendered as pure animality. The traditional reading completes the quartet as Patience triumphing over Wrath, the choleric vice fittingly imagined as a snarling quadruped; it should be said, however, that while the Charity–Avarice pair is explicitly named in the official documentation, the identification of the right-hand pair rests on the older interpretative tradition and merits verification against the catalogue.

The capital's intelligence lies in how completely the moral scheme is naturalised into the architecture. The Vices crouch where the capital bears its load, at the springing of the basket; the Virtues rise where it flowers; the central tree, laden with its cone-like fruit, stands between the halves like the axis of moral choice itself. Within the church's distributed program the capital functions as a hinge between narrative and judgment: it abstracts into emblem what the Judas capital tells as story (avarice ending in the noose) and what the west tympanum stages as cosmic drama — the vertical sorting of the saved above and the damned below, here rehearsed in miniature on a single block of stone.

## Style, Workshop Practice, and Artistic Formation

The question of where the Autun style came from is inseparable from the question of who Gislebertus was, and it is beset by the same tension between a compelling stylistic narrative and a thin documentary base. The traditional account, again crystallised by Grivot and Zarnecki and repeated in the reference literature, situates the Autun master within the great Cluniac artistic tradition of Burgundy. On this account he was trained at Cluny, in the workshop responsible for the sculpture of the third abbey church, the most influential building of the Romanesque age; from Cluny he is supposed to have moved to Vézelay, where the mutilated remains of an early tympanum have been thought to bear the marks of his hand; and from Vézelay he came, with a mature style, to Autun, working first on the eastern parts of the church and then on the west tympanum. Some accounts render this with a false precision — placing him at Cluny by 1115 as a chief assistant to the "Master of Cluny," at Vézelay in the early 1120s, and at Autun from 1125 — but these dates are inferential scaffolding rather than documented biography, and they should be treated as the reasoned hypothesis of the mid-twentieth-century scholarship, not as fact.

What can be said with more confidence concerns the stylistic milieu rather than the individual. The carving of Saint-Lazare is unmistakably Burgundian and unmistakably indebted to the Cluniac experiment: the pointed arches, the Corinthianesque and figured capitals, the very idea of an ambitious allegorical and narrative capital program all descend from Cluny III and its dependencies, of which Vézelay was among the greatest. The relationship to Vézelay is genuinely close in the handling of drapery, the elongation of figures, and the nervous linear energy of the surfaces, and the two monuments are routinely discussed together as the twin peaks of Burgundian Romanesque sculpture — even though it must be noted that not all scholars accepted the single-artist link between them. An older tradition of pilgrimage-road scholarship, for instance, preferred to see Cluny, Vézelay, and Autun as the products of three distinct ateliers rather than the itinerary of one man. The debt to antiquity is a second, well-documented dimension of the style: the sculptors of Autun worked amid Roman remains and drew on them directly, adapting Gallo-Roman capitals, pilasters, and figural conventions, so that the classicising strain in the Autun capitals is not a vague influence but a concrete borrowing from the visible past of Augustodunum.

On workshop practice, the material evidence of the tympanum itself is instructive. By the early twelfth century, large-scale carving was no longer done in the quarry but on the building site; the master would have worked from a shed near the church, assembling and fitting the twenty-nine blocks of the tympanum on a bed of sand, with assistants preparing stone and executing the simpler ornamental patterns. Whatever the truth about the name Gislebertus, the design of the ensemble — its compositional unity, the consistency of its figural language — plausibly reflects the controlling vision of a single master mind, even if many hands executed it. This is the defensible residue of the older attributionist enthusiasm: one need not believe that a single chisel cut every capital in the church to accept that a single dominant designer shaped the tympanum and set the terms of the whole.

## Destruction, Concealment, and Rediscovery

The tympanum that visitors admire today survived by a series of accidents that are, in their way, as remarkable as the sculpture itself, and the story is worth telling precisely because it corrects a sentimental narrative of continuous veneration. Far from being cherished across the centuries, the Romanesque carving of Autun was, by the eighteenth century, held in contempt by the very canons who administered it. Steeped in classical taste and regarding the medieval work as crude and barbarous, the chapter undertook a thoroughgoing modernisation, deciding in 1766 to reorder the choir. They dismantled the north portal — consigning the Eve lintel and its companions to the rubble — destroyed the medieval tomb-shrine of Lazarus, and encased the Romanesque apse in a skin of marble and gilding that would hide it until 1937, damaging capitals in the process. The archivolts above the west tympanum, which had carried a procession of elders of the Apocalypse and the labours of the months and signs of the zodiac, were partly chiselled away — an irreparable loss, though fragments of the elders survive in the Musée Rolin.

The west tympanum itself was, around the same time, covered over with plaster to produce a smooth, decorous surface in keeping with the new aesthetic. To achieve an even plane, the projecting head of Christ was cut off. This act of eighteenth-century vandalism proved, by a bitter irony that the sources never tire of noting, to be the tympanum's salvation: concealed behind plaster, it was invisible and therefore uninteresting to the anticlerical iconoclasts of the Revolutionary years, who smashed the exposed north portal and the tomb of Lazarus but left the hidden Last Judgment untouched — a fate very different from that of Cluny, which was largely demolished in the early nineteenth century. The great abbey's near-total destruction makes the survival of Autun's tympanum all the more precious.

The rediscovery came in 1837, when a canon of the cathedral — the tradition names the Abbé Devoucoux, who had consulted a fifteenth-century description of the church — probed and removed the plaster to reveal the tympanum almost perfectly preserved beneath. The head of Christ, however, was missing, having been separated in 1766, and it remained lost for over a century. In an extraordinary coincidence, the head had in fact been preserved in the Musée Rolin itself, unrecognised; only in 1948, when someone thought to test it against the tympanum, was the match confirmed, and the head restored to its place, where the break at the neck remains visible. The tympanum was restored in the mid-nineteenth century in the era of Viollet-le-Duc, whose circle also promoted the making of the monumental plaster casts through which the Autun Last Judgment became known in museums abroad. A further careful restoration in 2009, accompanied by the repositioning of eight fragments, permitted a renewed reading of the program and is the basis for much of the most recent French scholarship.

## Historiography and Critical Fortune

The story of how Gislebertus and Saint-Lazare entered the canon of art history is itself one of the most instructive aspects of the whole subject, and it is largely to Seidel that we owe its articulation. Until the nineteenth century, the Romanesque carving of Autun was, as we have seen, either despised or literally hidden. Its rehabilitation belongs to the broader nineteenth-century revaluation of the Middle Ages and, more specifically, to the French national project of the 1830s and 1840s, when Romanesque monuments — long overshadowed by the Gothic — were rediscovered as antecedents of a national artistic patrimony. The cathedral was classified among the historic monuments of France in 1840. The identification of Gislebertus specifically as the sculptor of Autun dates, on Seidel's account, from this same period of the 1830s, and it was consolidated, she argued, in the aftermath of the Second World War, when the desire to affirm French cultural greatness found a congenial hero in a named medieval genius.

The decisive act of canonisation was the Grivot–Zarnecki monograph of 1960–61. It is difficult to overstate its influence: with its authoritative text, its distinguished preface, and its superb photographic campaign, it fixed the image of Gislebertus as one of the supreme sculptors of Western civilisation and made Autun a fixed point in every survey of Romanesque art. Yet even at its moment of triumph the book drew serious scholarly criticism on precisely the point of attribution, with a French reviewer of the second edition insisting, against the authors, that the wholesale ascription of the capitals to a single hand simplified the evidence unacceptably. The intervening decades saw important interventions on individual problems — most influentially on the Eve lintel and its penitential context — that enriched the iconographic understanding of the monument without necessarily disturbing the figure of the master.

Seidel's *Legends in Limestone* of 1999 marks the great turn. Its achievement, acknowledged even by reviewers unpersuaded by its central thesis, was to make the invention of Gislebertus visible as an invention — to show that the confident biography rested on a single ambiguous inscription and on a set of modern assumptions about individual artistic genius that the twelfth century did not share. Whether or not one accepts her positive proposal that Gislebertus was a ducal patron, her negative demonstration has reshaped the terms of discussion: it is no longer possible to write about "Gislebertus the sculptor" as an unproblematic fact.

At the same time, the sceptical reception of her patron hypothesis — the judgment that her candidate patrons are too shadowy to carry the weight placed on them, and that the book sometimes battles an art-historical orthodoxy already dead — has meant that the field has been left in a productive suspension rather than resettled around a new certainty. Recent French scholarship, informed by archival discoveries such as a ducal inquiry of 11 April 1443, ordered by Philip the Good into the authenticity of the relics and found at the bishopric of Autun in 1999, and by the 2009 restoration, has tended to concentrate on the liturgical and pilgrimage function of the two portals and on the sophistication of the sculptural program, rather than on adjudicating the identity of Gislebertus — a shift of attention that is itself a consequence of the debate Seidel opened.

## Legacy and Significance in Romanesque Art

The significance of Saint-Lazare and its carving does not, in the end, depend on the resolution of the Gislebertus question, and it is worth stating positively what is not in doubt. The Autun tympanum is one of the two or three greatest surviving achievements of Romanesque monumental sculpture, a work in which the abstract, hieratic language of twelfth-century art is bent to purposes of extraordinary dramatic and psychological intensity — the terror of the damned, the serenity of the elect, the impassive majesty of the judge — and in which image and inscribed word are integrated with a rare deliberateness. The Eve is a landmark in the history of the human figure in Western art, the first monumental nude since antiquity and one of the most sensuously and intelligently conceived. The capitals constitute one of the richest surviving programs of narrative and allegorical carving of their century. These facts are independent of any name.

The style of Autun, moreover, stands at a genuine hinge in the history of European art. The Burgundian Cluniac tradition to which it belongs — with its pointed arches, its tall proportions, its ambitious sculptural portals — was among the seedbeds from which the Gothic emerged; the rich carved portals of the Île-de-France, beginning at Saint-Denis, drew on the achievement of Burgundian sculpture, and the elongated, expressive figures of Autun and Vézelay point forward, in their way, to the column-statues of the early Gothic. To place Autun in this lineage is not to diminish its Romanesque particularity but to recognise its generative power.

The final significance of the Gislebertus problem is perhaps methodological, and it is here that the case has resonance well beyond a single Burgundian church. The name beneath the feet of Christ has functioned, across nearly two centuries, as a screen onto which successive generations have projected their assumptions about art and the artist: the Romantic and post-Romantic cult of individual genius that made Gislebertus a hero; the connoisseurial confidence that could reconstruct a whole personality from a stylistic signature; and, latterly, the critical suspicion that reads the same inscription as a document of patronage, memory, and power. What the monument teaches, in the end, is the discipline of distinguishing what we know from what we have supposed. We know the church, the relics and their misidentification, the broad chronology of construction, the inscriptions, and the sculptures themselves. We have reasonable consensus about the Cluniac context and the second-quarter dating. We do not know who Gislebertus was. To hold those three registers apart — and to resist the perennial temptation to convert a name into a biography — is the truest homage that scholarship can pay to the anonymous or half-named makers of one of the medieval world's most eloquent monuments.

## Conclusion

Gislebertus remains, and will very likely always remain, a name in search of a person. The traditional identification of him as the sculptor of Autun, given its classic form by Grivot and Zarnecki in 1960, produced a compelling and enormously influential portrait that lifted a Romanesque master out of anonymity; Seidel's counter-thesis of 1999, identifying him instead as a patron bound to the ducal house of Burgundy and its memory of the relics, exposed that portrait as a modern construction and reframed the inscription as an act of commemoration rather than authorship. Neither reading has prevailed decisively, and the responsible conclusion is one of calibrated uncertainty: the inscription and the monument are certain; the Cluniac milieu and the twelfth-century date are consensual; the identity, formation, and even the professional role of Gislebertus are genuinely unknown and actively debated. What is beyond dispute is the quality and historical weight of the work gathered under that single, enigmatic name — the towering Last Judgment with its judging Christ and its verses of reward and terror, the reclining Eve with her fatal fruit, the crowded capitals with their sleeping Magi and their hanged traitor — and the fact that, whoever Gislebertus was, the church of Saint-Lazare at Autun stands among the indispensable monuments of the Romanesque age.

It helps, in holding that uncertainty steady, to remember that Autun is not an isolated case but the most contested member of a small and revealing class. The twelfth century produced a scatter of inscribed names on churches from Emilia to Galicia — Wiligelmo on the façade of Modena, praised in his inscription as one whose sculpture proclaims how much honour is his due; Nicholaus, who put his name on portals at Ferrara, Verona and Piacenza; Gilabertus at Toulouse, who declared himself no uncertain man; Master Mateo, named on the lintel of the Pórtico de la Gloria at Compostela in 1188 as the director of the works. Set beside these, the Autun formula is conspicuously reticent: it gives a name and a verb and nothing else, no epithet of craft, no claim of skill, no statement of office. The comparative epigraphy therefore cuts both ways. It establishes beyond argument that named self-identification was available to twelfth-century carvers and was sometimes used with real assertiveness; it establishes equally that *hoc fecit* was a capacious formula, applied across the period to those who cut the stone, those who directed the work, and those who paid for it. What the comparison finally suggests is that the question has been badly framed. The productive enquiry is not whether the Autun inscription is a signature but what naming was for — what it accomplished, liturgically and commemoratively, to fix a name in stone beneath the feet of the judging Christ.

If the epigraphic and stylistic arguments have reached something like exhaustion, the material evidence has not, and it is here that the most promising avenue now lies. The tympanum is an assembly of twenty-nine separate blocks; its figures were cut with tools that leave recoverable traces; its surfaces, and those of the capitals, carried a polychromy of which enough survives in protected interstices to repay systematic analysis. The 2009 campaign, which repositioned eight fragments and permitted a renewed reading of the program, demonstrated how much a careful physical intervention can still yield. A rigorous technical study — tool-mark analysis across the tympanum, the Eve and the capital series, petrographic comparison of the blocks, and the mapping of pigment residues — would test the single-hand hypothesis on evidence independent of the eye, and might do so decisively in a way that six decades of stylistic argument have not. The same methods applied comparatively, against the surviving Cluny ambulatory capitals and the mutilated Vézelay fragments, could put the traditional Cluny–Vézelay–Autun itinerary on a footing other than inference. That such work remains largely undone is the most concrete deficiency in the present state of the question.

There is a further caution, less often stated, about the object such enquiry would address. The Saint-Lazare that a visitor encounters is not a twelfth-century monument that has merely aged; it is a heavily edited one, and every judgement about the hand or hands at work in it is a judgement about a reassembled thing. The north portal was demolished in 1766 and its lintel sold for building stone; Eve spent a century inside the wall of a house on the Champ de Mars and re-entered the record only in 1866, passing through private hands and litigation before reaching the Musée Rolin. The head of Christ was struck off to accommodate the plaster and rejoined only in 1948, its break still legible at the neck. The archivolts of elders and labours were chiselled back; the apse lay encased in marble and gilding until 1937; a substantial part of the capital series has been withdrawn from the building and displayed in the salle capitulaire, severed from the architectural positions that gave the program its sequence. To this must be added the nineteenth-century restoration in the era of Viollet-le-Duc and the plaster casts through which the tympanum became internationally known. Some of the coherence that the mid-century scholarship discerned may belong to this modern act of curation as much as to the twelfth century.

Whatever the name meant, it was almost certainly not what the monument was for. The tympanum addresses its beholder directly and in the second person of admonition, in verses cut where they cannot be missed: TERREAT HIC TERROR QVOS TERREVS ALLIGAT ERROR — let this terror terrify those whom earthly error binds, for the horror of these images denotes truly what shall be. That inscription anticipates a viewer, not a connoisseur; it presumes an audience arriving on foot, largely unlettered, moving through a sequence of portals and capitals whose meanings accumulate by juxtaposition rather than by attribution. Against the vertical drama of judgment and the distributed moral architecture of the nave, the two words beneath Christ's feet are typographically minor and, on any reading, functionally marginal. The modern history of the monument has consisted very largely in inverting that hierarchy — in making the smallest inscription on the building carry the greatest interpretative weight — and it is worth registering how peculiar an inversion that is.

None of which is to say that the invention of Gislebertus was without consequence, or that its exposure as an invention cancels its effects. The name did real cultural work. It gave a body of art that the eighteenth century had despised, and that the nineteenth had rehabilitated chiefly as national patrimony, the one thing a public trained on Vasari could readily grasp: an artist. Through the casts, the survey textbooks, the monograph of 1960 and the modernist analogies of critics from Porter's El Greco and Gauguin to Malraux's Romanesque Cézanne, Gislebertus became the figure through which Romanesque sculpture entered general aesthetic consciousness as something other than a primitive prelude to the Gothic. That the figure was largely constructed does not make the reappraisal it enabled illusory, and the protection, publication and restoration that followed were tangible goods. The historiographic critique explains this history; it does not undo it.

What remains, then, is a well-posed question rather than a settled answer, and there is a case for regarding that as the more valuable possession. The usual condition of medieval art history is an abundance of names attached to a poverty of surviving objects; at Autun the relation is inverted, and we are confronted with one of the most complete and eloquent sculptural ensembles of its century attached to a name that will support almost nothing. The temptation, felt in every generation, is to close the gap by supposition — to make the inscription yield a biography, the biography a career, and the career an oeuvre. The discipline that the monument requires is the harder one of leaving the gap open: of describing with precision what the stones show, of holding the twelfth-century evidence apart from the modern arrangement of it, and of recognising that the anonymity against which Gislebertus has been made to stand is not a defect in the record but the ordinary condition of the men who built and carved Saint-Lazare. The church survives them, and says what they meant it to say, without needing to be signed.

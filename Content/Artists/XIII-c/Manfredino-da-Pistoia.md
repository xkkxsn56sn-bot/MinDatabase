## Introduction: a painter between two cities

Manfredino d'Alberto, called *Manfredino da Pistoia*, is one of those late thirteenth-century painters whose historical weight is out of all proportion to the number of objects that can be securely connected with his name. Two detached fresco fragments in the Museo di Sant'Agostino in Genoa, a handful of archival notices spread across Pistoia and Genoa, and a signature that was read more fully in the middle of the nineteenth century than it can be read today: this is the irreducible core upon which more than a century of scholarship has built a personality, a career, a catalogue and, at times, a legend. Around that core has grown a ring of attributions of very different solidity, ranging from apse paintings in Pistoia to a panel in a parish church outside Florence. Since the restoration campaigns of the first decade of the present century it has also come to include a substantial Carmelite cycle in Genoa which, if it is indeed his, would considerably enlarge his monumental work.

The interest of the figure lies precisely in that position between two cities and two artistic systems. Pistoia in the 1270s and 1280s was a Tuscan commune in which the presence of Coppo di Marcovaldo and his son Salerno had planted, a generation earlier, a vigorous Florentine idiom, and where the new language of Cimabue was rapidly absorbed. Genoa, by contrast, was a maritime power whose figurative culture had been shaped by long contact with Byzantium and the Latin East. As the reference literature on Genoese painting has emphasised, painting of a fully Tuscan and specifically Cimabuesque kind appears there only with Manfredino's arrival. To study him is therefore to study one of the earliest documented moments in which the reforming painting of central Italy, the painting of Assisi and of Cimabue, was transplanted to the Tyrrhenian coast.

What can be known about him depends on distinguishing at every stage between documentary fact, inscription, consensus attribution, contested attribution and hypothesis. Three scholars mark the decisive turns in the literature:

- **<a href="/scholars.html#donati-pier-paolo">Pier Paolo Donati</a>**, whose article of 1972 in the *Bollettino d'Arte* provided the first comprehensive critical reconstruction of the painter;
- **<a href="/scholars.html#neri-lusanna-enrica">Enrica Neri Lusanna</a>**, whose work on the Pistoiese context and on the apse of Santa Maria a Ripalta has repeatedly reopened the question of Manfredino's beginnings;
- **Clario Di Fabio**, whose studies of Genoese painting culminated in the 2011 publication that attributed to Manfredino the Carmelite cycle of Nostra Signora del Carmine and a lunette at San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella.

## The documentary record

The archival evidence for Manfredino is meagre but, within its limits, precise. The earliest notice, as summarised in the 2007 entry of the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*, dates to 30 August 1280. On that day Manfredino appears in Pistoia as a witness to a payment made to the Opera di San Jacopo by the heirs of a certain Vincenzo di Jacopo. On 15 October of the same year he received forty lire for a fresco, now lost, placed in the cathedral of San Zeno above the altar dedicated to Saint Proculus; the payment was entered in an expense register of the Opera di San Jacopo.

These two documents were published by a Pistoiese archival historian at the end of the nineteenth century, first in an article of 1897 in the journal *Arte e storia* and then in a volume of Tuscan documents for the history of art printed in Florence in 1910. They remain the only certain evidence of Manfredino's activity in his native city. The sum is not negligible for a single altar painting, and the commission came from the cathedral's own administrative body, which suggests that by 1280 Manfredino already enjoyed some standing in Pistoia. Beyond this, the documents say nothing about his training, his age or his family.

The next notice has been reconstructed only indirectly. The most recent study of the Madonna of Mosciano, published in 2024–2025, states that the apse painting of San Bartolomeo in Pantano was executed before September 1283. The reason given is that by that date Manfredino had left Pistoia "per problemi economici e giudiziari" (for economic and judicial problems), never, as far as is known, to return. The author attributes this chronological indication to a survey of Tuscan mural painting published in 2013. The underlying document, its archival location and the exact nature of the difficulties it records have not been published, so the point remains a datum reported in the secondary literature. It is nevertheless important for two reasons: it offers a plausible motive for the painter's departure from Tuscany, and it bears directly on the chronology of his Pistoiese works.

The *Dizionario Biografico* states that Manfredino was "still present in Pistoia in 1291" and active in Genoa the following year. It cites no document for the 1291 presence, and the same entry describes him, consistently with the rest of the literature, as documented between 1280 and 1293. The claim appears to descend from a Pistoiese biographical dictionary of 1883. That book is also the source of the tradition that Manfredino "putatively" painted in 1291 for a since-destroyed chapel in Pistoia Cathedral. The only cathedral commission actually documented is that of October 1280 for the altar of Saint Proculus, so the "1291" tradition is probably a garbled recollection of the 1280 payment, and it has no documentary basis.

The Genoese document is the most informative of all. It is a notarial act of 5 November 1293, published in 1870 by the nineteenth-century historian of Ligurian artists. Manfredino, then living in Genoa in a house owned by Vivaldino Vivaldi in the contrada of San Francesco, placed his son Obertino as an apprentice in the workshop of the armourer (*corazzaio*) Ton da Firenze for eight years. The text is significant in several respects:

- It confirms Manfredino's residence in Genoa in 1293 and places him near the great Franciscan church of San Francesco di Castelletto, then nearing completion.
- It shows him as the father of a son old enough to begin an apprenticeship, which implies that he was no longer a young man.
- By committing Obertino to an eight-year contract with a Florentine craftsman settled in Genoa, it suggests an intention to stay in the city for a considerable time.
- It illustrates, almost incidentally, the dense network of Tuscan immigrants in late thirteenth-century Genoa, within which a Pistoiese painter could find lodging, patrons and a placement for his son.

Beyond November 1293 the documents fall silent. The extension of Manfredino's career to 1305, now frequently encountered, rests not on an archival notice but on an attribution. The work in question is an unsigned lunette at San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella, a Cistercian foundation whose marble foundation inscription bears the date September 1305; it is discussed below. No document records Manfredino's death, and nothing is known of Obertino's later career.

## The Fassolo frescoes: discovery, inscriptions and survival

The church of San Michele di Fassolo, in the western suburb of medieval Genoa, no longer exists. It was rebuilt in the mid-seventeenth century and demolished in the nineteenth to make room for the western railway station, the present Genova Piazza Principe. Its medieval apses, however, had preserved under later whitewash a painted decoration whose rediscovery is recorded in an unusually detailed eyewitness account.

That account was published in 1875 in the *Giornale ligustico* by the Genoese painter and antiquarian Tammar Luxoro. He recalled how, while making drawings of the ruinous building, he noticed the outlines of figures under the whitewash of the apses. He alerted the painter Cristoforo Gandolfi, who uncovered the paintings with his son Francesco. Luxoro dates the event to "the ill-fated spring of 1849", when troops were taking positions for the assaults on the Arsenale dello Spirito Santo during the Genoese insurrection, and he relates that he had the scaffolding dismantled to protect the paintings from the fighting.

Luxoro's text is also the earliest verbatim transcription of the signature that has reached us. "Pictures by an artist until then unknown came to light," he writes, "whose name is read beneath the middle fresco (for every apse had one) in the well-known inscription: MAGISTER MANFREDINUS PISTORIENSIS ME PINXIT MCCLXXXXII IN MENSE MADII." Two of the three "medallions", as he calls them, were later detached and transported to the Accademia Ligustica, with the municipality paying and the Gandolfi doing the work; the third was in too poor a condition to be saved. The current museum record attributes the detachment to Luxoro himself, whereas his own account assigns the work to the Gandolfi. The two surviving fragments were long in the custody of the Accademia and for a time deposited at the Galleria di Palazzo Bianco, as the photographic records of the Fondazione Zeri attest. They are now displayed in the Museo di Sant'Agostino.

The surviving fragments are a figure of Saint Michael and a scene of the supper at which a woman anoints Christ. The literature calls the latter variously the *Cena in casa di Simone il fariseo*, the *Convito di Betania* or the *Supper at Bethany*.

- **The supper.** According to the *Dizionario Biografico*, which follows the nineteenth-century reports, it came from the central zone of the main apse. Along its lower edge it bore the inscription with the painter's name ("Magister Manfredinus Pistoriensis") and the date May 1292; that inscription is now almost entirely illegible.
- **The Saint Michael.** This panel was probably placed originally to the right of a single-light window and formed part of a more complex scene, a *psychostasis* or weighing of souls<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-psychostasis" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>. The words "Magister Manfredinus" are still legible at lower right, as the 1868 catalogue of the Genoese artistic and archaeological exhibition already noted.

The sources diverge slightly on which fragment carried the full dated inscription. Luxoro speaks of the inscription under "the middle fresco" without naming its subject; the later literature unanimously identifies that fresco with the supper, and on the Saint Michael only the shorter form of the signature survives. The third, lost painting is described by the *Dizionario Biografico*, following the early twentieth-century archival publication, as a monumental representation of Saint Michael. Whatever its subject, the essential point is secure: Fassolo is the only cycle in Manfredino's catalogue that bears his name, and it is therefore the touchstone against which every attribution must be measured.

The condition of the fragments must be borne in mind in any stylistic discussion. Detached in the mid-nineteenth century by the methods then available, both paintings have lost much of their original surface. The scholarly literature describes the supper in particular as impoverished and weakened by the transfer, whereas the Saint Michael is comparatively little repainted. Any judgement about Manfredino's colour, which is central to the critical image of him, must be made with caution on the basis of these fragments alone.

## The question of the date: 1292 or the 1280s?

The date of the Fassolo frescoes has become the pivot of Manfredino's whole chronology, and of a larger debate about the diffusion of Cimabue's Assisi paintings. On the face of it the question should not arise. The signature was read, at the moment of discovery, as giving May 1292, and Manfredino's presence in Genoa in November 1293 is independently documented. The difficulty arises from style.

In his 1972 article, Pier Paolo Donati argued that the Genoese fragments fitted more comfortably in the 1280s, close to the San Bartolomeo in Pantano apse. He did not exclude that Manfredino might have made more than one journey to Liguria from 1280 onwards. The reference literature records that several scholars followed him, proposing a date between 1282 and 1287 and therefore postulating an earlier Genoese stay. The most recent monographic contribution, published in 2024, maintains that the Genoese frescoes belong to the second half of the 1280s. It argues that the later, Duccio-influenced Madonna of Mosciano makes a 1292 date for Fassolo unlikely.

The opposing position is held just as firmly. The Genoa entry of the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale*, co-written by Clario Di Fabio, observed that the retro-dating requires one to postulate, "not entirely convincingly", a Genoese stay five or ten years earlier than the one attested by the sources. It also pointed out that the debate over Manfredino's chronology mirrors the debate over Cimabue's own work at Assisi, placed by some before 1279 and by others between 1288 and 1292. In the 2011 study of the Carmine frescoes, the same scholar reaffirmed the 1292 date for San Michele.

Three arguments favour the retention of the documentary date:

- **The quality of the witness.** The inscription was transcribed in full by a witness of the discovery who was himself a painter and antiquarian, and his transcription is specific in both month and year. A damaged Roman numeral could have been misread, but the burden of proof lies on whoever proposes the misreading, and no physical argument for such an error has been advanced.
- **The nature of the opposing case.** The case for the 1280s is entirely stylistic, and it depends in turn on a relative chronology of other works, none of which is itself documented.
- **The 1293 document.** Its long-term apprenticeship contract fits a recent settlement in the city much better than a return after a long interval.

The stylistic case is not negligible. If the Mosciano Madonna is by Manfredino and reflects knowledge of Duccio's Rucellai Madonna of 1285, a painter capable of that refinement might be expected to show it in a work of 1292. Yet the argument presupposes a linear development in which every later work must be more "advanced" than every earlier one. The variety of functions, techniques and patrons in late thirteenth-century painting does not justify that assumption, and the poor condition of the Fassolo fragments weakens it further.

The evidence may be summarised as follows: the frescoes are dated May 1292 by their inscription as recorded in 1875; that reading cannot now be checked on the original; and a minority of scholars prefers, on stylistic grounds, a date in the 1280s.

## The Pistoiese works: San Bartolomeo in Pantano, Ripalta and the cathedral sinopia

No surviving painting in Pistoia bears Manfredino's name. His Pistoiese catalogue is entirely a construction of connoisseurship, built backwards from Fassolo, and it is in this area that attributions are most fluid.

The most widely accepted is the apse painting in the Benedictine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedictine-order" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> abbey church of San Bartolomeo in Pantano: a great image of Christ in glory, or Christ Pantocrator, flanked by saints and angels in the semi-dome. The reference works do not agree on the identity of the flanking saints. The *Dizionario Biografico* names Saint John the Baptist and Saint John the Evangelist; the Pistoia entry of the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* names Saint John the Baptist and Saint Bartholomew, the church's titular saint; and a caption in the most recent Mosciano study even calls the composition an Ascension. The painting itself, in its present state, does not settle the question.

The attribution itself has a complex history:

- A scholar writing in 1971 proposed Vigoroso da Siena as the author.
- The same name had been advanced earlier, in unpublished university lectures, by a leading mid-century connoisseur. Significantly, he also recognised that this apse and the Mosciano Madonna were by the same hand.
- The attribution to Manfredino became established through Donati's 1972 reconstruction. As the *Dizionario Biografico* notes, it has been repeated since "with some caution", and the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* treats Manfredino as the "probable" author.

It is, in short, a consensus attribution rather than a certainty. Its date depends on the reported 1283 departure: if Manfredino left Pistoia by September 1283 and did not return, the apse must precede that date.

The *Ascension with apostles* in the apse of Santa Maria a Ripalta is a more problematic case. Recovered during restoration and first published by Enrica Neri Lusanna in 1984, it carries an inscription with the date 1274. The *Dizionario Biografico* reports that this date would, conditionally, allow it to be placed in the earliest phase of Manfredino's activity. The question of authorship, however, is genuinely open. The Pistoia entry of the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* lists the Ripalta Ascension, with a cautious "perhaps", among the works of Salerno di Coppo, citing Neri Lusanna's 1992 monograph on the church. The two reference works thus appear to report divergent conclusions from the same body of research. Neri Lusanna's most recent essay, published in 2021 as "Oltre il dipinto e tra i margini: alle origini di Manfredino da Pistoia", is devoted precisely to the painter's origins.

Whatever the final verdict, the Ripalta Ascension is best described as a dated work (1274) of contested attribution from the circle of the Marcovaldi, which some scholars have connected with Manfredino's beginnings. It is not a dated work by Manfredino. The statement that he was "active from 1274", which appears on a museum label, turns an attribution into a biographical fact.

A third Pistoiese item is the sinopia of the *Virgin and Child between Saints James and Zeno*, which emerged in 1959 in the lunette of the cathedral's southern door. Donati placed it close to the Acton dossal and thus in Manfredino's early phase. The difficulties in this case are even greater. The work appears to be connected with a document of 1260; some scholars have referred it to a Lapo da Firenze, while others have admitted it only cautiously into Manfredino's small catalogue, and the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* simply ascribes it to the Lapo named in the 1260 document. A painter documented only from 1280 is an unlikely author for a work bound to a document of 1260. The sinopia is best regarded as part of the Pistoiese background from which Manfredino emerged, rather than part of his work.

Behind these questions stands the broader issue of Manfredino's formation. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* describes him as a product of the Marcovaldi's Pistoiese circle. It is documented that Coppo di Marcovaldo and Salerno worked intensively in Pistoia between 1265 and 1276, leaving among other things the Crucifix in the cathedral and the panel of Saint Francis from Santa Maria al Prato. That Manfredino grew up in contact with this milieu is a reasonable inference from geography and chronology. That he was formally a pupil of the Marcovaldi is not documented.

## Panels: the Acton dossal, the lost Villa polyptych and the Madonna of Mosciano

Two surviving panels are attributed to Manfredino, and a third, now lost, is reported to have been signed by him.

**The Acton dossal.** This polyptych in the Acton collection at Villa La Pietra in Florence, now owned by New York University, shows the Virgin and Child flanked by Saints Michael, John the Baptist, Dionysius and Nicholas. <a href="/scholars.html#longhi-roberto">Roberto Longhi</a> first connected it with Manfredino in his 1948 survey of Duecento painting. Donati placed it no later than 1280, a dating accepted by later scholarship. It is thus the cornerstone of the hypothesis that Manfredino was already fully Cimabuesque by 1280. The attribution has been widely repeated, but it remains an attribution, and the circumstances of the work's origin are unknown.

**The lost Villa polyptych.** The question of the Acton dossal is entangled with that of a lost signed work. The *Dizionario Biografico* records that the Genoese collection of G. B. Villa contained a signed polyptych by Manfredino, which disappeared from view at the end of the nineteenth century; the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* notes that it was in Genoa in 1899. A scholar writing in 1986 proposed that the Acton dossal and the Villa polyptych were the same work, which would place the dossal in Manfredino's Genoese period. The identification is considered uncertain, above all because the Acton panel lacks the signature that the sources attribute to the Villa polyptych. The lost polyptych nonetheless matters in principle: if the reports are accurate, Manfredino signed at least one panel as well as his frescoes, and a signed work by him was on the Genoese market in the late nineteenth century.

**The Madonna of Mosciano.** The *Madonna and Child with Two Angels* in the church of Sant'Andrea a Mosciano, near Scandicci, has the longest critical history of any work attributed to him:

- It was published in 1916.
- In the 1920s and 1930s it was discussed as the work of an artist at the meeting point of Florentine and Duccesque characteristics.
- It was restored in 1935 for the 1937 Giotto exhibition.
- In 1954 it was associated with Vigoroso da Siena.
- In 1972 Donati attributed it to Manfredino's late phase, with the concurring opinion of another leading specialist of Cimabue.

Much of subsequent scholarship has accepted the attribution, including a major supplement to the *Corpus of Florentine Painting* published in 2021. As the restoration report published in 2024–2025 notes, the dissenting voices include Neri Lusanna (1992 and 2021) and a Genoese specialist writing in 2011.

The restoration completed in 2022, published with a technical report in 2024–2025, has clarified several aspects of the panel's fabric. The support consists of two conifer planks joined vertically, covered with linen, gesso and red bole under the gold ground; the paint is a fat tempera laid in very fine strokes. Beneath the Virgin's left hand the restorers found traces of gilding, which suggest that an object, perhaps a golden orb, was planned and then abandoned during execution. A dove was later added in that place, which led popular devotion to treat the panel as a Madonna del Carmine; it was removed in 1935. The historical study published with the restoration also records that the panel was carried in procession until the Soprintendenza prohibited the practice after the 1935 restoration.

The case for attributing the Mosciano panel to Manfredino rests on comparison with San Bartolomeo in Pantano and with Fassolo, and on Donati's formula for his style: a "cimabuismo riformato a forza di colore", a Cimabuesque idiom reformed by the force of colour. The latest study emphasises two features: a floral punched motif associated with Cimabue in the Virgin's halo, and a softness of palette that it reads as direct knowledge of Duccio. It proposes a date around 1290–1295, after the Rucellai Madonna of 1285. If both the attribution and this dating are accepted, the panel would be Manfredino's latest known work, showing him at the end of his career absorbing the Sienese refinement then visible in Florence.

Two problems remain. The first is that the same study uses the panel to argue for the retro-dating of Fassolo, a circular argument discussed above. The second is geographical: if Manfredino left Pistoia by 1283 and was settled in Genoa in 1292–1293, a commission in the early 1290s for a collegiate church near Florence needs an explanation that is not yet available. The Mosciano Madonna is therefore best described as a high-quality panel of the Cimabuesque-Duccesque circle. Most specialists attribute it to Manfredino, but not all, and no document fixes its date.

## The Genoese catalogue enlarged: the Carmine, the Olivella, Sant'Andrea della Porta and Andora

The most significant change in Manfredino studies in recent decades has come from Genoa. During the restoration of the church of Nostra Signora del Carmine between 2006 and 2009, the removal of later plaster revealed a painted cycle in the square main apse. The results were published in 2011 in a three-part study in the *Bollettino d'Arte*:

- an account of the architectural restoration;
- an account of the rediscovery and conservation of the paintings;
- an art-historical essay by Clario Di Fabio, which analysed the iconography, attributed the cycle to Manfredino, discussed its chronology and the circumstances of its commission, and assigned to the painter a previously unpublished fresco at San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella.

The abstract of that study describes the cycle as the earliest known painted programme with Carmelite subjects.

Di Fabio described the iconography of the Carmine apse in a 2009 article in *L'Osservatore Romano* and in the 2011 study:

- At the top of the end wall is a great, fragmentary Annunciation, celebrating the Virgin as patron and titular of the order.
- Below it stand two pairs of large figures. On one side is the prophet Elijah, regarded by the Carmelites as their founder and wearing the order's habit, beneath Saint John the Baptist, the last of the prophets and a frequenter of Mount Carmel. On the other side is Saint Bartholomew, apostle and protector of Armenia, above Saint Margaret of Antioch, dressed, in Di Fabio's phrase, like a Byzantine *basilissa*.
- The cycle is completed by Carmelite saints, identifiable by their haloes and by the white habit the order adopted in the late 1280s, and by roundels with saints on the side walls, among them Lawrence, Paul and Peter.

Di Fabio's 2009 article places the adoption of the white mantle in 1289; other accounts give 1287. The date bears on the earliest possible date of the cycle.

Di Fabio's historical reading of the cycle is ambitious. The Carmelites of Genoa, he argues, were a community that had been forced to leave the East under Muslim pressure, and the programme alludes to their origins as well as celebrating the order's characteristic devotions. He identifies the real patron as Opizzo Fieschi: a relative of Popes Innocent IV<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-innocent-iv" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> and Adrian V, Latin patriarch of Antioch, papal diplomat and, in Di Fabio's words, the organiser of the resettlement of such fugitive Eastern communities in several European countries. The *Dizionario Biografico* confirms that Nicholas IV<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-nicholas-iv" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> appointed Opizzo administrator of the diocese of Genoa on 4 June 1288, after the see had been vacant for two years. Di Fabio further suggests that Opizzo "probably" brought Manfredino to Genoa, and that the painter had met Cimabue in Rome in 1272.

Each of these propositions carries the caution Di Fabio himself applied:

- The identification of Opizzo as patron rests on an iconographic and historical argument, not on a document.
- The claim that Opizzo summoned Manfredino is a hypothesis.
- The Roman meeting of 1272 is inferred from Cimabue's documented presence in Rome that year, not from any record of Manfredino.

A further difficulty is chronological. Opizzo's administration of Genoa ended around 1291–1292. Yet the abstract of the 2011 study states that the Olivella lunette of 1305 provides a *terminus ante quem* for the Carmine cycle, which implies that the cycle could postdate Opizzo's tenure. The relation between the two limits remains to be clarified.

The second addition is the lunette over the portal of San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella, the small church of a Cistercian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-cistercian-order" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> monastery founded in 1305 by the banker Bonagiunta Valente. It shows the Virgin and Child between Saint Bartholomew, identified by his knife, and a saint usually identified as Bernard of Clairvaux<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-bernard-of-clairvaux" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>, appropriate to a Cistercian foundation. Di Fabio's abstract calls it an "affresco datato" and on that basis fixes the latest date in the painter's biography at 1305. The date, however, does not appear to be painted on the fresco. It derives from the monastery's marble foundation inscription, which records the foundation in September 1305 and the name of Bonagiunta Valente. The lunette is therefore unsigned, datable to about 1305 by association with the foundation, and attributed to Manfredino on stylistic grounds, including the raised, radially incised haloes that the literature calls "Assisian".

The reception of these two additions has been mixed. Their proponent presented the Carmine attribution as having found "wide consensus", and parish publications and the city museums' website now take it for granted. More recent scholarship, however, has expressed explicit doubts. The 2024–2025 Mosciano study states that both the Carmine and the Olivella frescoes should be removed from Manfredino's catalogue. In its view they lack the quality of his secure works and show cultural references entirely foreign to the Duccesque renewal; it cites in support a Genoese specialist writing in 2011 and a 2015 study that considered the Carmine paintings workshop productions.

The issue is thus open. The attribution of the Carmine cycle to Manfredino or his immediate workshop is a serious hypothesis. It is supported by the coherent Assisian technical features described by the restorers and by the painter's documented presence in Genoa in the early 1290s. But it does not have the status of the signed Fassolo fragments, and the Olivella attribution is weaker still.

Two further items complete the Genoese picture.

The first is a group of frescoes with stories of the Infancy of Saint John the Baptist, detached from the destroyed monastery of Sant'Andrea della Porta and now in the Museo di Sant'Agostino. They are dated to the end of the thirteenth or the beginning of the fourteenth century, and their anonymous author has been named the Master of the Infancy of the Baptist. Di Fabio proposed, in 1998 and 2005, to identify this master with Manfredino; the museum's record notes particular connections with Manfredino's work and describes the style as a blend of the older Eastern manner with the novelties of Tuscan painting. The identification is a hypothesis. But a second fresco cycle with Tuscan features in Genoa, in the years around Fassolo, is itself evidence, either of Manfredino's impact or of a broader influx of Tuscan and Assisian painting of which he was the most visible representative. According to its abstract, the 2011 study adds that other contemporary discoveries show several hands bringing an Assisian figurative culture to Genoa.

The second item is lost. In the same 1875 article in which he recorded the Fassolo discovery, Luxoro reported frescoes in a hall of the castle of Andora, in western Liguria. They carried a fragmentary inscription, "A ... MANFREDINUS", in characters he said were identical to those on the Saint Michael medallion. The *Dizionario Biografico* notes that these paintings were already regarded as lost at the beginning of the twentieth century. If Luxoro's reading was correct, Manfredino worked beyond the city in the domains of the Ligurian aristocracy, but nothing survives to verify it.

## Style and sources: Cimabue, Byzantium and colour

Every critical account of Manfredino begins with Cimabue, and rightly so. As the entry in the *Dictionary of Art* put it, the Fassolo fragments show a keen and intelligent response to Cimabue: the Saint Michael in its dynamic pose and physical solidity, the supper scene in its modelling and facial construction. The broad, heavy, angular drapery, the strong chiaroscuro of the faces, the large hands and the emphatic glances belong to the language Cimabue had developed in the upper church at Assisi and in his panels. The question is where and when Manfredino learned it.

There are several hypotheses:

- **The Assisi transept.** The earliest critics, followed by many later scholars, assumed that Manfredino had worked under Cimabue in the transept of the upper church of San Francesco at Assisi. One scholar of the 1960s went so far as to recognise his hand in the Apocalyptic Christ of the left transept, a proposal rejected by the *Dizionario Biografico* entry on Cimabue.
- **Florence.** Others, including Donati, preferred to locate Manfredino's absorption of the new language in Florence.
- **The Assisi nave.** Di Fabio's 2009 article proposed a more nuanced version: Manfredino would have worked not in Cimabue's transept but in the upper parts of the nave, alongside Roman painters and local workshops, and, it may be imagined, near the young Giotto.

No document supports any of these hypotheses. The strongest arguments in their favour are the Assisian features of the Genoese paintings: the haloes, the punched motifs, the treatment of architecture and, in the supper, the city backdrop. The *Dictionary of Art* entry noted the "remarkably ambitious and detailed backdrop of a city scene" behind the supper, and Di Fabio has read this view of buildings behind the seated figures as one of the details that certify Manfredino's experience of Assisi. What can be said with confidence is that Manfredino knew Cimabue's painting intimately, and that Cimabue's decisive works were then at Assisi and in Florence. Whether he was ever on the scaffolding of the upper church cannot be known on present evidence. The common description of him as "Cimabue's collaborator at Assisi", found in museum labels, therefore remains a hypothesis.

Donati's formula of a Cimabuesque idiom "reformed by the force of colour" captures what is distinctive in Manfredino's painting, as far as the surviving works allow one to judge. Where Cimabue tends towards tragic intensity, Manfredino's figures appear softer and his colour more nuanced, especially in the Pistoiese apse and the Mosciano panel. In the Mosciano panel, translucent rose and pale blue garments and filament-like brushstrokes modelling the flesh suggest a painter attentive to the qualities of pigment and to the delicate gradations Duccio was bringing to Florence in the mid-1280s. This assessment of his colour, however, depends on the attribution of the Mosciano panel and on the condition of the Fassolo fragments. It would be imprudent to build too fine a profile from such uneven evidence.

The Byzantine component of Manfredino's art is better described as a matter of iconography, and of the shared heritage of Italian Duecento painting, than as a direct relation with Byzantine models. The *Dictionary of Art* noted that in the supper scene Manfredino adds to the traditional Byzantine iconography a sympathetic and psychologically alert sense of the dramatic undertones. The same may be said of the Saint Michael: the weighing of souls has a long history in both Byzantine and Western art, and the archangel's imperial dress and outstretched wings belong to a type common to both traditions.

In the Carmine, if the cycle is his, the Byzantine element becomes more explicit: Saint Margaret is dressed as an empress, and the programme is rooted in the Carmelites' Eastern origins and in the career of a patriarch of Antioch. Genoa in the 1290s was a city in which Byzantine objects, artists and patrons were present; a decade later, as Di Fabio has recalled, a Constantinopolitan painter would fresco the cathedral. Manfredino's painting is thus best understood not as a fusion of Byzantine and Tuscan styles but as a Tuscan idiom already permeated by the *maniera greca*, which Cimabue had transformed from within, practised in a city whose patrons were particularly receptive to Eastern iconography.

## Between Tuscany and Liguria: patrons, routes and the Mediterranean horizon

Manfredino's move from Pistoia to Genoa, whatever its precise date, shows how artistic exchange between Tuscany and Liguria worked at the end of the thirteenth century. The two regions were linked by sea, by the coastal road and by the movements of merchants, bankers and craftsmen. The 1293 document shows a Pistoiese painter lodging with a Genoese family, apprenticing his son to a Florentine armourer, and living near a great Franciscan church whose façade, as the architectural literature has noted, alluded to the mother church at Assisi. A Tuscan painter in Genoa was not an isolated figure but part of a community.

If confirmed, the reported economic and judicial troubles that drove Manfredino from Pistoia would supply an individual motive for this move. The structural reason was the demand of the Genoese church, enriched by the city's maritime success and by the building campaigns of the mendicant orders. Genoa in these years had a remarkable concentration of ecclesiastical patronage connected with the Fieschi, from Innocent IV and Adrian V to Opizzo, whose career linked Genoa to Antioch, Cyprus, Trani and the papal curia. This context lends Di Fabio's hypothesis of Opizzo's patronage some plausibility. An ecclesiastic who knew the Latin East, the Eastern communities displaced by the Mamluk conquests and the papal court would have been well placed to recognise the prestige of the new Tuscan painting, and to commission it for the Carmelites, a community whose origins lay in the East.

Who commissioned the Fassolo frescoes is unknown. San Michele di Fassolo was a parish church in the western suburb, and the iconography of the surviving fragments, the titular archangel and a supper with the anointing of Christ, does not by itself point to a particular patron. The documents are silent, and no family or institution can be named.

Manfredino's impact on Genoese painting is visible in the Sant'Andrea della Porta frescoes and, more broadly, in the "Assisian" culture that the 2011 study identified in other works of the same years. The Genoa entry of the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* speaks of monumental painting turning towards Cimabue after Manfredino's Genoese activity, and the Genoese museum system describes him as the painter who introduced the Tuscan innovations of the late thirteenth century to the city. Whether one regards him as the sole conduit or as the best documented of several, his signed Fassolo frescoes of 1292 are the earliest dated evidence of this transplanting.

## Errors, legends and misreadings

Several errors about Manfredino circulate widely, and some appear in sources that otherwise deserve respect.

**The Carmine and 1305.** The English text of the city museums' record of the Fassolo Saint Michael states that Manfredino was "active in Pistoia and Genoa between 1274 and 1305 (the year in which he signed the frescoes in the Church of Carmine)". This compresses three different things into one:

- 1274 is the inscribed date of the Ripalta Ascension, whose attribution to Manfredino is contested;
- 1305 is the foundation date of the Olivella monastery, not of the Carmine;
- neither the Carmine cycle nor the Olivella lunette is signed. The only signed works are the Fassolo fragments.

**The Carmine and 1292.** A date of 1292 for the Carmine paintings, which occasionally circulates, confuses the newly discovered cycle with the Fassolo frescoes. No scholar has dated the Carmine cycle to 1292.

**The subject of the main Fassolo fragment.** Reference works old and new call it the *Magdalen at the feet of the Redeemer*, the *Supper in the house of Simon the Pharisee*, the *Supper at Bethany*, the *Anointing at Bethany* or the *Supper at Simon's House*. These labels conflate two Gospel episodes:

- the anointing in the house of Simon the Pharisee, in Luke 7;
- the anointing at Bethany in the house of Simon the leper, in Matthew 26 and Mark 14, which John 12 places at a supper in Bethany with Martha, Mary and Lazarus.

Medieval iconography frequently fused the two, and the Magdalen was identified with the anointing woman in both. The variety of titles is not an error in itself, but it has caused confusion in catalogues, where the same fresco appears under different names as if it were two works. The neutral description "supper with the anointing of Christ" avoids prejudging the identification.

**The circumstances of discovery.** It is sometimes stated that the frescoes were found during the demolition of the church for the railway station. Luxoro's own account makes clear that he noticed them under the whitewash while drawing the building, that the Gandolfi uncovered them in the spring of 1849, before the demolition, and that their detachment was a separate operation paid for by the municipality.

**The Cimabue connection.** The description of Manfredino as a "pupil" or "collaborator" of Cimabue at Assisi, presented as fact in museum texts, is a hypothesis. The statement that he met Cimabue in Rome in 1272 is likewise an inference.

**The location of the frescoes.** Even the short entry in the Treccani online encyclopedia, which places the Fassolo frescoes in the "Accademia liguistica" (a misprint for Ligustica), is out of date: the frescoes have long since passed to the Museo di Sant'Agostino.

## Critical fortune

The critical history of Manfredino falls into four phases.

**1. Antiquarian discovery (mid-nineteenth century).** This phase comprises the uncovering and detachment of the Fassolo frescoes in 1849, the 1868 exhibition catalogue, the 1870 publication of the Genoese document and Luxoro's 1875 account. Manfredino emerged as "an artist until then unknown", a Pistoiese name read on a Genoese wall.

**2. First placement in the history of Italian painting (turn of the century to 1937).** The Pistoiese documents were published in 1897 and 1910. Manfredino entered a German survey of Genoese art in 1906 and <a href="/scholars.html#toesca-pietro">Pietro Toesca</a>'s history of medieval Italian art of 1927, which formulated the Assisi hypothesis. A first monographic article appeared in *L'Arte* in 1937.

**3. A catalogue built by connoisseurship (1948 to the 1990s).** The Acton dossal was attributed in 1948, followed by debates over San Bartolomeo in Pantano and the Mosciano Madonna. Donati's 1972 synthesis gathered the scattered proposals, set out a chronology running from the Acton dossal before 1280 to the Mosciano Madonna in the 1290s, and proposed the retro-dating of Fassolo; it remains the foundation of all later discussion. The 1980s and 1990s added the Ripalta Ascension, the debate on Fassolo's date in the major surveys of Italian and Genoese painting, the reflections of specialists of Cimabue and of Florentine Duecento painting, and the Genoa entry in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale*, which defended the 1292 date.

**4. The Carmine discovery and its aftermath (2011 to the present).** The Carmine discovery and the 2011 publication made Genoa once again the centre of Manfredino studies. The reactions continue:

- the 2015 study that attributed the Carmine paintings to a workshop;
- the 2021 Pistoia exhibition, which placed Manfredino in the context of the city's medieval art;
- the 2021 essay on his origins;
- the 2024 article on his Genoese maturity between Cimabue and Duccio, which, together with the 2024–2025 Mosciano restoration report, has reopened both the date of Fassolo and the extent of the Genoese catalogue.

The current state of the question is one of genuine disagreement. Future research may help to resolve it, above all the publication of the 1283 document and a technical study of the Fassolo fragments.

## Conclusion

Manfredino d'Alberto is documented in Pistoia in 1280 and in Genoa in 1293, and he signed a fresco cycle in the church of San Michele di Fassolo whose inscription was read in 1849 as dated May 1292. These few facts establish him as one of the earliest painters of the Cimabuesque tradition whose name and works can be linked, and as the first documented agent of that tradition's transfer to Liguria.

Around this core, the attributions fall into three groups:

- **Consensus attributions:** the apse of San Bartolomeo in Pantano, the Acton dossal and, with some dissent, the Mosciano Madonna.
- **Contested attributions or hypotheses:** the Ripalta Ascension, the cathedral sinopia, the Carmine cycle, the Olivella lunette and the Sant'Andrea della Porta frescoes.
- **Known only from nineteenth-century reports:** the Andora frescoes and the Villa polyptych.

The figure who emerges is a painter of considerable quality, formed in the Pistoia of the Marcovaldi and shaped by an intimate knowledge of Cimabue's painting. He took his art to a Mediterranean city with a different figurative tradition and found patrons there willing to commission ambitious programmes. It remains to be determined whether he spent the last decade of his life painting the earliest known Carmelite cycle, or whether that cycle is the work of followers formed in his shadow. What is certain is that the study of Manfredino is not a closed chapter but an open problem, in which the precise reading of documents and inscriptions remains, as it was in 1849, the indispensable foundation of any attribution.

The Saint Michael is one of the two surviving fragments of the paintings that decorated the apses of San Michele di Fassolo, a church in the western suburb of medieval Genoa, demolished in the nineteenth century for the building of the railway station of Piazza Principe. The paintings were uncovered in 1849 and the two fragments were detached and transferred to the Accademia Ligustica; they are now in the Museo di Sant'Agostino (MSA 3641). The fragment measures 272 by 138 centimetres and is mounted on a modern support of glass resin.

Along the lower edge runs a yellow band with an inscription in dark Gothic capitals, preceded by a cross. It is partly abraded, but the beginning of the words "Magister Manfredinus" can still be made out. The rest of the line is lost.

The archangel strides towards the right on a band of ground painted in yellow and red, his body turned in a strong diagonal. He is shown as a young figure with reddish hair, a broad face and large eyes, under a gold halo outlined in white. His wings spread widely behind him, their feathers painted in green and white and edged in red, and they fill the upper part of the panel with a sweeping movement.

He wears a long robe of pink and violet, its folds marked by sharp white highlights in a fan-like pattern, and a jewelled collar with a band set with gems down the front of the chest. The sleeve of his left arm, lined in green, flutters outwards, and the hem of the robe swings back at the left, giving the figure a sense of rapid movement.

With his raised right hand Michael holds a long staff, which crosses his body diagonally. From its lower end, at the right, hangs a balance with a round pan, in which a small naked soul kneels with its arms raised in supplication. The subject is the *psychostasis*, the weighing of souls at the Last Judgement, in which the archangel decides the fate of each soul. Behind the pan the dark feathers of a wing can still be seen, probably belonging to the devil who in such scenes tries to tip the balance; the rest of the figure is lost.

At the left edge a band of black and white stripes runs along a curved frame, the remains of the architectural setting of the apse, beside a single-light window that the fresco originally flanked. The figure formed part of a larger scene, whose other parts were destroyed with the church.

The figure shows the qualities that the literature recognises in Manfredino's art: a Cimabuesque monumentality joined to bright, light colour and to a lively, animated line. The broad face with its large eyes, the dynamic pose, the fluttering drapery with its white highlights and the strong contrast of pink, green and blue recall Cimabue's frescoes at Assisi, which Manfredino probably knew at first hand, while the clarity of the colour appears to be his own.

The fragment has suffered from its detachment by the methods available in the nineteenth century. The surface is crossed by cracks and abraded, the upper right part is largely lost, and the inscription is worn. It is nonetheless less repainted than the companion fragment of the supper, and it preserves a clear record of the colour and the drawing of the signed Fassolo cycle.

The second surviving fragment of the Fassolo apse paintings is a scene of a supper at which a woman anoints the feet of Christ. It measures 226 by 205 centimetres and, like the Saint Michael, is mounted on a modern support of glass resin (MSA 3640). Its exact position in the church is reported differently: the nineteenth-century accounts place it in the central zone of the main apse, beneath which the dated signature was read, while the current museum record describes it as taken from a side wall of the choir. The lower border preserves only faint traces of lettering.

The composition is organised along a long table set parallel to the picture plane. Christ sits at the left end, slightly apart from the others, on a high seat whose footstool is decorated with small Gothic arches. Behind the table four men are aligned in a single row, each turned slightly towards Christ. Their heads stand out against a green curtain hung from a rod, with a red border embroidered with ornamental motifs, which closes the space of the meal.

Christ, with a gold halo, has long hair and a forked beard, and raises his right hand to his chin in a gesture of reflection or of speech; the left hand rests on his knee. His figure is larger than those of the guests and occupies the full height of the scene, from the halo to the footstool, so that he is distinguished both by scale and by position.

The guests are differentiated by age and type. At the left an old man with a hood and a long wavy beard raises his hand; in the centre a beardless youth with red hair holds a knife; next to him an apostle with a white beard and a gold halo, probably Peter, holds a piece of bread; at the right a second hooded old man holds a knife and reaches towards the table. The two hooded figures may be read as the host and another guest, though the identification depends on which Gospel account is followed.

Under the table the woman lies stretched out on the ground in a red mantle, her face turned towards the viewer and pressed against Christ's feet in the act of anointing them. She has a faint halo. Her long, horizontal figure, set below the line of the tablecloth, forms a counterpoint to the vertical figure of Christ and draws the eye from the guests down to the act of devotion that is the subject of the scene.

The table is laid with care: footed bowls with fish, glass goblets, a gilded two-handled jug, round loaves, knives and a lidded flask. The white cloth falls in regular folds and is decorated at the bottom with yellow bands and fringes. The objects are painted with light, transparent strokes, and the glass vessels in particular show an attention to the material of things that recurs in Assisian painting of the late thirteenth century.

Above the curtain rises a city of buildings with pink and red walls, tiled roofs, domes, a tower and, at the right, a column with a capital. At the left a tall building with a pediment and an arch opens like a gateway. The buildings are set at different angles and suggest depth through the oblique placing of their sides and roofs, a solution that the literature connects with the architectural backdrops of the upper church at Assisi.

The fresco is in poorer condition than the Saint Michael. The surface is crossed by cracks and widely faded, especially on Christ's robe and in the lower left, where the seat and the footstool are partly lost. The green of the curtain and the sky is mottled, and many details of the faces have been weakened by the nineteenth-century detachment, so that the original modelling can now be judged only in the better preserved heads of the guests.

San Bartolomeo in Pantano is a Romanesque abbey church in the centre of Pistoia, built for a Benedictine community. Its painted apse is the work most widely connected with Manfredino's Pistoiese years. It bears no inscription, and its attribution rests on comparison with the signed fragments from Fassolo, above all with the Saint Michael. If Manfredino left Pistoia in 1283, as the documents seem to indicate, the painting would date from the years before his departure.

The conch is painted on the curved surface of the semi-dome, above the three windows of the apse, and framed at the top by red bands that follow the curve of the arch. The composition is centred on Christ, with angels and saints arranged symmetrically around him against a ground of deep blue. The upper half is relatively well preserved; the lower half has lost most of its paint, so that the original extent of the scene below the rainbow can no longer be judged.

Christ sits frontally on a rainbow striped in red, yellow and green, inside a mandorla of pale grey-blue that rises to the top of the conch. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds a closed red book on his left knee. His face is long, with large eyes, a straight nose and a beard divided at the chin, framed by long hair falling on the shoulders, under a gold halo with a red border. He wears a red tunic and a dark brown mantle wrapped around his legs in broad folds.

Six angels surround the mandorla, two above it and two at each side, with large wings of brown, red and white that fill the blue ground. The two upper angels lean over the top of the mandorla; the others turn towards Christ, in poses that vary from figure to figure. The wings, with their long feathers marked by white strokes, recall in their form and colour the wings of the Fassolo Saint Michael.

At the edges of the conch two saints bend towards the centre, their bodies following the curve of the vault. The saint at the left, bearded and with dark hair, wears a garment of pink and green; the saint at the right, with long hair and a long beard, wears a brown mantle and stretches out an open hand towards Christ. The gesture and the brown garment might suit Saint John the Baptist, but the identification of both figures varies in the literature and cannot be settled from the painting in its present state.

The figure of Christ shows the characteristics that link the apse to Manfredino. The heavy mantle with its broad folds, the long face with large eyes, the strong modelling of the features and the frontal, monumental presence correspond to the Cimabuesque language of the Fassolo fragments. The faces of the angels, round and softly modelled, are close to those of the guests in the Fassolo supper.

The iconography of Christ in glory on a rainbow, surrounded by angels, belongs to a long tradition of apse decoration that goes back to Early Christian and Romanesque models. In this apse it is treated with a new sense of volume and movement: the bending saints and the angels in different poses give the composition a dynamic quality within a symmetrical scheme.

The losses in the lower half of the conch have exposed the plaster and, in places, traces of red underdrawing, which offer a direct view of the painter's preliminary design. The surviving paint surface is faded and darkened in places, particularly on Christ's mantle and on the blue ground.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Two Angels (Scandicci, Sant'Andrea a Mosciano)

  <img src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-04.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-04-zoom.jpg" alt="Tall rectangular panel with a gold ground. The Virgin sits on a wide wooden throne with two red cushions, its sides decorated with carved foliage and inlaid rows of red, black and white stones. She wears a dark blue mantle drawn over her head, with a small gold star on the right shoulder, over a long pink gown that falls in heavy folds to a light blue footstool seen from above, with arched openings in its front. Her head is tilted to her right and she looks out towards the viewer. On her right knee sits the Child in a pale grey-white tunic, with a gold halo; he turns his head towards the viewer and stretches both arms towards his mother's left hand, which is open, palm upwards, in front of her body. Behind the throne, at the upper corners, two angels in pink garments with large raised wings of brown and white look down; the lower parts of their bodies fade into the gold ground. The Virgin's halo is drawn with incised concentric circles.">
  <figcaption>Attributed to Manfredino da Pistoia, <em>Madonna and Child Enthroned with Two Angels</em>, c. 1290–95(?). Tempera and gold on panel. Scandicci, church of Sant'Andrea a Mosciano. Photo: Saul Rinaldi, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons (cropped).</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel hangs in the church of Sant'Andrea a Mosciano, in the hills south-west of Florence, inside a stone frame set into the wall. It is a tall rectangular panel without gables or side compartments, a format common to the large Marian images of the second half of the thirteenth century. The attribution to Manfredino is accepted by much of the literature but not by all, and the date proposed in the most recent study, around 1290–1295, depends on that attribution.

The Virgin occupies almost the whole height of the panel, from the halo near the top edge to the footstool at the bottom. She sits on a wide wooden throne seen slightly from the side, so that the left side of the seat and its decoration are visible. The throne carries two large red cushions, and its uprights are decorated with carved foliage and rows of inlaid red, black and white stones, framed by narrow bands of white dots.

She wears a dark blue mantle drawn over her head and shoulders, with a small gold star on the right shoulder, over a long pink gown. The mantle falls in broad, soft folds over her arms and knees, while the gown, lit by lighter strokes, descends in long vertical folds to her feet. The two fabrics are modelled with gradual transitions of tone rather than with the sharp gold striations of earlier Byzantine-inspired Madonnas.

Her face is long and oval, with large almond-shaped eyes, a long straight nose and a small mouth, and her head is tilted to her right. The gaze is directed towards the viewer, with an expression of gravity. The halo is marked by incised concentric circles on the gold ground; its decoration has been connected in the literature with motifs used by Cimabue.

The Child sits on the Virgin's right knee, supported by her right hand. He wears a pale tunic of grey-white tones, modelled with fine strokes, and has a large head with curly hair and a full face. He turns his head towards the viewer while stretching both arms towards his mother's left hand, which is open, palm upwards, in front of her body. The empty, receptive gesture of the hand is the visual centre of the panel.

Behind the throne, at the upper corners, two angels in pink garments look down towards the Virgin. Their large wings, raised vertically, are painted in brown and white feathers. The lower parts of their bodies dissolve into the gold ground, in a pale, transparent paint that may be the result of wear rather than of an original intention.

The footstool, painted light blue, is seen from above and projects towards the viewer, with arched openings in its front and a border of inlaid stones. Its oblique placement, like that of the throne, creates a defined space for the figure. The Virgin's feet, in dark shoes decorated with gold, rest on it beneath the hem of her gown.

The painting is in good condition after the restoration of 2022. The gold ground is worn in places, revealing the bole beneath, and the wood of the support is visible along the edges. The colour, with its contrast of deep blue and pink and its light, soft modelling, is the feature most often cited in favour of the attribution to Manfredino and of the panel's link with the painting of Duccio in Florence.

### Stories of Saint John the Baptist, from Sant'Andrea della Porta (Genoa, Museo di Sant'Agostino)

  <img src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-05.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-05-zoom.jpg" alt="A long horizontal fresco fragment divided into three scenes by dark vertical bands, set in a rocky landscape of ochre, brown and green under a dark blue-grey sky. In the centre the adult John, haloed, thin and bearded, dressed in a shaggy garment of animal skin, holds a tall staff ending in a cross and a short scroll with large letters; he raises his right hand towards a small bearded head that appears in the sky at the top, beside a tree and the dark opening of a cave. At the right two men, one young and bearded in a red mantle, the other old with a white beard and a striped head-cloth, stand before John, who points at them with one hand and holds an unrolled white scroll in the other, beside a second cave.   <figcaption>Master of the Infancy of the Baptist (Manfredino da Pistoia?), <em>Stories of Saint John the Baptist</em>, late thirteenth or early fourteenth century, fragment from Sant'Andrea della Porta, Genoa. </figure>

The fragment comes from the church of Sant'Andrea della Porta, the church of a convent of Benedictine nuns beside the Porta Soprana of medieval Genoa. The church was demolished at the beginning of the twentieth century, and during the work three scenes from a cycle of the life of Saint John the Baptist came to light and were recovered. They were detached, transferred to canvas and are now in the Museo di Sant'Agostino (MSA 3722). The cycle was certainly larger, but no other part of it is known.

The three scenes are set side by side, separated by narrow dark bands, in a continuous landscape of rocky hills under a dark blue-grey sky. The museum record identifies them, from left to right, as the archangel Uriel leading the young John into the desert, the voice of God manifesting itself to John, and John questioned by the Pharisees. The sequence follows the Baptist's life from his childhood in the wilderness to the beginning of his preaching.

In the first scene the angel, with large brown wings, a pale tunic and a pink mantle, walks towards the right while turning back towards the child, whom he leads by the hand. The child John, haloed, wears a short tunic with stripes and carries a small dark bag, the provision for his life in the desert. The episode of the angel guiding the infant Baptist into the wilderness derives from apocryphal and Byzantine tradition rather than from the Gospels, and it is less frequent in Italian painting of the period than the scenes of the Baptist's preaching and death.

In the central scene John, now adult, stands before a rocky slope, dressed in a shaggy garment of animal skin that leaves his thin arms and legs bare. He holds a tall staff ending in a small cross and a short scroll with large letters, only partly legible. He raises his right hand towards the sky, where a small bearded head appears at the top of the scene: the voice of God, rendered as a divine bust emerging from the darkness. A tree and the dark opening of a cave complete the setting.

In the third scene two men stand before John: a younger bearded man in a red mantle over a pale tunic, and an older man with a white beard and a striped head-cloth, in a pink mantle. They are the priests and Levites sent from Jerusalem to ask John who he is (John 1:19–23). John, at the right beside a second cave, points at them with one hand and holds in the other an unrolled white scroll, the sign of his prophetic message.

The landscape is built of steep, faceted rocks painted in ochre, brown, green and white, with broad, rapid strokes that give the slopes a flickering surface. The trees, with slender trunks and tufted crowns, are of a type common in Byzantine and Italo-Byzantine painting. The dark sky, now probably darker than originally intended, unifies the three scenes into a single band.

The museum record describes the style as nervous, combining an older Eastern manner with the novelties of Tuscan painting, and notes particular connections with the work of Manfredino. The literature has named the anonymous author the Master of the Infancy of the Baptist, and Clario Di Fabio has proposed to identify him with Manfredino himself. The long, thin figure of John, the heavy drapery of the Pharisees and the angel's face may be compared with the Fassolo fragments, but the more summary execution and the Byzantine landscape leave the identification open.

The paint surface is worn, with large areas of loss, particularly on the figure of John in the third scene and in the lower part of the central scene. The transfer to canvas has flattened the surface, and the outlines of some figures are now blurred. The fragment nonetheless preserves the narrative structure of the cycle and its unusual iconography of the Baptist's youth.

### Virgin and Child between Saint Bartholomew and a Cistercian Saint (Genoa, San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella)

  <figcaption>Attributed to Manfredino da Pistoia, <em>Virgin and Child between Saint Bartholomew and a Cistercian Saint (Bernard of Clairvaux?)</em>, c. 1305. Fresco. Genoa, San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella, lunette of the portal. Photo: Superchilum, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/">CC BY-SA 3.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>

San Bartolomeo dell'Olivella is the small church of a Cistercian monastery founded in 1305 on the hill above the old centre of Genoa. The painted lunette over its portal is unsigned and undated; its approximate date derives from the foundation of the monastery. It was attributed to Manfredino by Clario Di Fabio and, if the attribution is accepted, would be the latest work connected with the painter.

The lunette fills the tympanum of a round-arched portal framed by voussoirs of alternating grey and white stone, outlined by a red band and set under a low gable. The two-coloured masonry, typical of Genoese religious architecture of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, frames the painting like a second, architectural border. A narrow painted frieze with a running ornament follows the inner curve of the arch.

The composition is arranged in three parts, with the Virgin and Child at the centre and a saint on each side, shown at half length against a grey-green ground. The figures are cut off at the bottom by the lintel and by the loss of the lower edge of the painting, so that their original extent cannot be judged.

The saint at the left is Bartholomew, the titular saint of the church, identified by the knife of his martyrdom, which he holds in his right hand. He has dark curly hair, a full beard and large eyes under a gold halo, and wears a garment of red and ochre. His face, the best preserved in the lunette, is modelled with strong shadows and a firm outline.

The central group is badly damaged. The head of the Virgin is lost, and only a fragment of her halo survives at the top, displaced to the right of the axis. Her red mantle and blue garment can still be made out, as can the green tunic of the Child sitting on her lap, but his face has also disappeared. The relationship between the two figures can no longer be reconstructed in detail.

The saint at the right wears the white habit with a dark scapular of the Cistercians and holds a long staff ending in a crook, an abbot's crozier. He has short hair and a beard, and turns his head slightly towards the centre. He is usually identified as Bernard of Clairvaux, an identification that fits the Cistercian foundation, though no inscription confirms it.

The haloes of the three figures are in raised relief, with radial grooves that produce a pattern of rays. This technique, which the literature calls "Assisian", is one of the features on which the attribution to Manfredino rests, together with the solid modelling of Bartholomew's head. The fragmentary state of the painting makes a full stylistic comparison with the Fassolo fragments difficult.

The lunette has suffered from its exposure to the weather. Large areas of paint have been lost, especially in the centre and along the lower edge, leaving the bare plaster visible, and the colours have faded. A line of metal pins along the lower edge protects the painting from birds. What survives is enough to show the composition and the quality of the best-preserved head, but not to settle the question of authorship.

### Ascension with Apostles (Pistoia, Santa Maria a Ripalta)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-07.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Manfredino-da-Pistoia/manfredino-da-pistoia-07-zoom.jpg" alt="A semicircular apse with a white plastered arch. In the conch, Christ sits frontally on a throne inside a mandorla formed by concentric bands of red, white and ochre; he wears a red tunic and a blue mantle, raises his right hand in blessing and holds a book in his left. At each side of the mandorla, figures in blue and red garments, probably angels, are partly preserved. Below the conch the wall of the apse has lost most of its paint, leaving a large area of bare white plaster with a walled-up central window. At the left and right edges of the lower zone, rows of standing apostles survive under a decorative frieze. At the bottom fragments of a painted base with geometric motifs and small gilded details remain.">
  <figcaption>Pistoiese painter (Manfredino da Pistoia? Salerno di Coppo?), <em>Ascension with Apostles</em>, dated 1274. Fresco. Pistoia, Santa Maria a Ripalta, apse. Image: Regione Toscana, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

Santa Maria a Ripalta is a small church in the centre of Pistoia whose apse paintings were recovered during a restoration. An inscription carries the date 1274. The painting has been placed at the beginning of Manfredino's career by some scholars and given to Salerno di Coppo by others, and the attribution remains open. Its date and its Pistoiese setting nonetheless make it a point of reference for the painter's formation.

The decoration occupies the whole apse, in two zones. The conch above shows Christ enthroned, and the curved wall below was painted with standing figures of apostles, of which only those at the edges survive. Together the two zones form an Ascension, in which Christ appears in glory above the apostles who witness his departure, a scheme widely used in Romanesque and Duecento apse painting.

Christ sits frontally on a throne at the centre of the conch, inside a mandorla formed by concentric bands of red, white and ochre that follow the curve of the vault. He wears a red tunic and a blue mantle wrapped around his knees in broad folds, raises his right hand in blessing and holds a closed book on his left knee. His head, under a halo, is long and bearded.

At the sides of the mandorla figures in blue and red garments are partly preserved. Their position and the flame-like shapes of their contours suggest angels supporting or accompanying the mandorla, as in the traditional iconography of the Ascension, but their state does not allow a closer description.

The lower zone has lost most of its paint, leaving a large area of bare plaster in which the outline of a walled-up central window can be seen. At the left and right edges standing apostles survive, with haloes and long garments, under a band of painted ornament that separates them from the conch. The faces turn towards the centre.

At the bottom fragments of a painted base survive, with geometric motifs and small gilded details in the centre, which may belong to the furnishings of the altar area or to a later intervention. The losses make it impossible to reconstruct the lower part of the composition.

The style of the conch shows a broad, monumental figure of Christ with strong outlines and simple drapery, close to the Florentine and Pistoiese painting of the Marcovaldi circle in the 1260s and 1270s. The striped mandorla and the solid throne belong to the same tradition. Those who have connected the work with Manfredino see in it an early stage of his art before his encounter with Cimabue, while the attribution to Salerno di Coppo places it within the Marcovaldi workshop itself.

The only available photograph, from the regional heritage catalogue, is of low resolution and allows only a general reading of the composition. The fresco is fragmentary, with large losses in the lower zone and at the sides of the conch, and any stylistic judgement must take its condition into account.

## Two Painters from Spoleto

*Simeone* and *Machilone* are two painters known almost entirely from their signatures. Four inscriptions record them: a lost panel for the cathedral of Ancona, signed by both; a painted cross dated 1257, now in Palazzo Barberini in Rome, signed by both; a dossal now in Antwerp, on which a fragment of their signature has been read; and a painted wooden Madonna from Bugnara in the Abruzzo, signed in 1262 by Machilone and his son. Around these four points modern scholarship has built a workshop, a following and a school.

Their signatures call them *Spoletani*, men of Spoleto, and their activity extended across the wide territory of the diocese of Spoleto and beyond it, to Ancona on the Adriatic, to Orvieto and to the Abruzzo. They worked in partnership over a long period: the Ancona panel is dated around 1230, the Barberini cross 1257, the Bugnara Madonna 1262. The standard account is the entry on Machilone written by [Cristina Ranucci](/scholars.html#ranucci-cristina) for the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (2006).

Nothing is documented of their lives outside the inscriptions. No record names their parents, their training, their workshop or their deaths. Their relationship is described in the sources only as a partnership: Simeone is Machilone's *compagno*. The frequent statement that they were father and son, or brothers, has no basis. Machilone did have a son, who signed the Bugnara Madonna with him, but his name survives only in mutilated form.

The names themselves need care. Simeone appears as *Symeon* or *Simeon*, Machilone as *Machilon* or *Machilonus*; the older literature, following the first publication of the Barberini cross in 1926, called him *Machilos*. Simeone must not be confused with other painters called Simone, such as the fourteenth-century Bolognese [Simone dei Crocifissi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Simone-dei-Crocifissi.html). The rarity of the name Machilone is what allows the Machilonus of Bugnara to be identified, with probability, with the Spoleto painter.

The previous version of this entry, rewritten in September 2026, was careful in its distinctions between evidence and hypothesis, and much of its substance is kept here. It contained, however, two errors of fact. It gave the Antwerp dossal a date of 1258 "by inscription", whereas the dossal carries no date: only a fragment of the signature survives, read by Garrison, and the date 1258 is an echo of the alternative reading of the Barberini cross. It also gave the Barberini cross the dimensions of the Vatican processional cross, 104 × 74 centimetres.

It also named only four scholars and referred to most of the literature anonymously, as "the 1929 study" or "a 1962 article". The present entry names the authors and links them to the list of scholars of the site, following the bibliography of the *Dizionario Biografico*.

The account that follows examines the four inscriptions, the city and the diocese in which the two painters worked, their sources between Alberto Sozio and Giunta Pisano, the works attributed to them and to their following, and the modern recovery of their names. The catalogue then presents the works one by one, with photographs where free images exist and in words where they do not.

Three works, the Vatican processional cross, the Maestà of Otricoli and the triptych of Rinaldo di Ranuccio in Pittsburgh, are described without illustration, because no image with a free licence could be found. The images previously shown for them on this site had no credit and have been withdrawn.

## The Four Inscriptions

The first inscription is lost with its panel. A painting signed by both painters stood in the choir of the canons of the cathedral of San Ciriaco in Ancona. The seventeenth-century Anconitan writer Giovanni Pichi Tancredi recorded it in his manuscript *Anconologia*, now lost, with the signature *SIMEON ET MACHILON SPOLETANI* and a date. The panel disappeared from the cathedral around 1728.

The date of the Ancona panel is uncertain. Posti (1912), [Edward Garrison](/scholars.html#garrison-edward) (1949) and Ranucci give it as 1230; Luigi Serra, in his survey of the art of the Marches (1929), gives 1220, relaying the same tradition. The subject, too, is reported variously as a Deposition or a Crucifixion. What can be said is that the two painters were working in partnership by the 1220s or 1230s, and that their reputation already reached the Adriatic coast.

The second inscription is on the painted cross in the Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica in Palazzo Barberini, Rome. At the foot of the cross, below a kneeling woman, a plaque carries four lines of writing. The first gives the names of the painters, *SYMEON ET MACHILO[N]*; the third and fourth give the date, *ANNO D(OMI)NI M CC L V / II*, and the name of the donor, *D(OMI)NA MARIA FECIT FIE(RI)*, "the lady Maria had it made". The second line, damaged, completes the signature.

The date of the cross is read as 1257 by Ranucci and as 1258 by other writers, among them the catalogue of Palazzo Barberini (Ferrara 2006). The photograph of the plaque shows the numeral divided between the third and the fourth line, *M CC L V* followed by *II*, which supports the reading 1257; a lost stroke cannot be excluded. The entry uses 1257, as the *Dizionario Biografico* does.

The third inscription is on the Antwerp dossal. It is a fragmentary text in the lower middle zone, on the footstool of the Virgin's throne. [Millard Meiss](/scholars.html#meiss-millard), who first studied the panel in *The Burlington Magazine* in 1937, did not see it and gave the dossal to a Sienese painter of 1250–1260; Garrison, in 1949, read it as part of the signature of Simeone and Machilone. No date can be read on it.

The fourth inscription is on the base of the painted wooden Madonna from Bugnara, now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA) in L'Aquila. Its text, as published, reads *(A)NNO D(OMI)NI MCCLXII M(A)CH(I)LON(US) ET CE(P)LA(.) EI FILIUS (DE)P(IN)S(E)RU(N)T HOC OPUS*: "in the year of the Lord 1262, Machilonus and Ce…, his son, painted this work". It is the only inscription of the group whose complete text has been published.

The Bugnara inscription raises three questions. The first is identity: the museum identifies Machilonus with the Spoleto painter only with a "perhaps", and the identification rests on the rarity of the name and on the proximity of date and place. The second is the verb *depinserunt*, "painted", on a carved statue: either the workshop both carved and painted, or Machilone and his son painted a statue carved by someone else. The third is the son's name, which survives only as *CE(P)LA(.)*.

Taken together, the inscriptions give a career of at least thirty years, from around 1230 to 1262. They show a partnership that worked for a cathedral chapter, for a lay patroness and for rural churches, that produced panels, crosses and painted sculpture, and that by 1262 had become, at least for Machilone, a family workshop in which Simeone no longer appears.

## Spoleto and Its Diocese

Spoleto in the thirteenth century was a city recovering from its destruction by Frederick Barbarossa in 1155. Its cathedral, rebuilt, was consecrated by Pope Innocent III in 1198, and its façade received in 1207 the mosaic signed by [Solsternus](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Solsternus.html). The city lay between the Empire and the Papacy, and from the end of the twelfth century it was increasingly drawn into the papal orbit.

The diocese of Spoleto was exceptionally large. It extended eastward and southward across the Apennines towards the central Marches and the Abruzzo. Painters based in Spoleto could find work across this territory, and the geography of the works attributed to Simeone and Machilone, from Ancona to Bugnara, follows its routes.

The patrons of the signed works are of several kinds. The Ancona panel was made for the canons of a cathedral. The Barberini cross was commissioned by a lay woman, *domina Maria*, who had herself portrayed at its foot. The Orvieto Madonna belonged to a lay confraternity, and the Bugnara Madonna to a rural church. The workshop depended on no single great patron.

The Bugnara Madonna came from the church of the Madonna della Neve. According to the museum, the place had once been the site of a temple of Ceres, goddess of the harvest, and the women of the village continued to offer grain, now to the Virgin, in small copper bowls, the *concanelle* that gave the statue its name. The story is a local tradition reported by the museum, and it shows the devotional setting for which such statues were made.

Umbria in the mid-thirteenth century was not a provincial backwater. The basilica of San Francesco at Assisi, begun in 1228, drew painters from Tuscany, Rome and beyond, and Giunta Pisano painted for it a great cross in 1236. The two Spoletans worked in this environment, and their adoption of Giunta's model shows their awareness of it.

The Franciscan movement shaped the devotional climate. The *Christus patiens*, the dead Christ on the cross, spread through Franciscan churches; the cult of the Virgin grew; new orders and confraternities commissioned images. The works of Simeone and Machilone, a cross with the dead Christ and several images of the Virgin, belong to this climate.

The workshop's range of formats is notable. It produced a large panel for a cathedral, a painted cross for a private patroness, a dossal with narrative scenes and, in Machilone's case, a painted statue. Such versatility was normal in thirteenth-century workshops, which supplied whatever their clients needed.

The two Spoletans were among the leading painters of Umbria in the central decades of the century. Their activity, reconstructed from a handful of signed works, gives a glimpse of the organisation of a regional workshop, working across a wide territory for a varied clientele.

## Between Alberto Sozio and Giunta Pisano

The starting point of their style is the local tradition. Spoleto possessed, in the generation before them, a painter of distinction, [Alberto Sozio](/Content/Artists/XII-c/Alberto-Sozio.html), whose signed cross of 1187 hangs in the cathedral. Scholarship has located their formation in this tradition and described their art as a deeply felt Byzantinism of Spoletan derivation, with features of late Comnenian type.

The Spoletan inheritance is visible in their faces and figures: the long oval faces, the large dark eyes, the long straight noses, the small closed mouths; the angular drapery; the pale thrones with striated backrests; the mourners in three-quarter view at the ends of the cross. These features connect their work with the Spoleto school rather than with the Byzantinism of Tuscany.

Onto this base they grafted the model of Giunta Pisano. Giunta was the decisive figure in the history of the painted cross in the second quarter of the thirteenth century, the painter chiefly associated with the diffusion of the *Christus patiens*, the dead Christ with closed eyes and a body curving in an arc. His crosses for Assisi and Bologna set a model that painters throughout central Italy adopted.

The Barberini cross belongs to this Giuntesque orbit. [Evelyn Sandberg-Vavalà](/scholars.html#sandberg-vavala-evelyn), in her study of the Italian painted cross (1929), proposed as its model Giunta's lost cross of 1236 for San Francesco in Assisi, commissioned by Brother Elias. Rossella Vodret, in the catalogue of Palazzo Barberini (1998), connected it instead with Giunta's cross for San Domenico in Bologna.

The two proposals describe different aspects of a single relationship. Both establish that by the 1250s the Spoleto workshop knew the Pisan model of the painted cross and adapted it. The softening of Giunta's dramatic curve, observed in the Barberini cross, is the workshop's own contribution: the body bends, but less violently, and the expression is restrained.

[Ferdinando Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando) (1962) and [Pietro Toesca](/scholars.html#toesca-pietro) placed the two painters in the history of Duecento painting in Umbria and Italy, and [Filippo Todini](/scholars.html#todini-filippo), in his survey of Umbrian painting (1989), catalogued their works and those of their following. The image that emerges is that of a workshop rooted in Spoleto but open to the main currents of its time.

The Marian works show other sources. The Antwerp dossal, with its central enthroned Virgin and narrative scenes at the sides, follows a type diffused in central Italy in the mid-century, also in Siena and Arezzo; it was precisely its resemblance to Sienese works that led Meiss to call it Sienese. The Orvieto Madonna, with its blue glass gems and gold-striated Child, shows the workshop's taste for material splendour.

The style of the two painters is therefore a synthesis: a Spoletan base, Byzantine in its types, combined with the Pisan cross of Giunta and the Marian formats of central Italy. Whether two distinct hands can be separated within it is an open question; the signed works do not allow it, and the literature has generally treated the workshop as a unit.

## The Attributed Works and the School

Around the signed works, scholars have grouped attributed works and works of followers. The most important is the Madonna and Child enthroned in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo at Orvieto, which came from the Confraternita del Carmine. Ranucci (1996) attributed it to the two painters and dated it around the middle of the century; the *Dizionario Biografico* records a date around 1265, possibly earlier.

The Orvieto Madonna may be the surviving centre of a tabernacle with shutters, as proposed by Testa and Davanzo (1984) and [Corrado Fratini](/scholars.html#fratini-corrado) (1989). A Madonna in the church of San Giusto near Pievebovigliana, in the Marches, has been described as a faithful but provincial version of it, which shows that the Orvieto panel served as a model across the Apennines.

The painted cross from the monastery of San Lodovico in Orvieto, now in the same museum, is given to an Orvietan follower of the two painters and dated around 1250–1275. It has been attributed in turn to [Coppo di Marcovaldo](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Coppo-di-Marcovaldo.html) or his son Salerno, to [Margaritone d'Arezzo](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Margarito.html) and to the Spoletans themselves, and its history of attribution shows how fluid the boundaries between regional schools were.

A processional cross in the Pinacoteca Vaticana, inv. MV.40005, is catalogued by the museum as Orvietan school (Simeone and Machilone?), around 1260–1270. It shows the living Christ, with the Virgin and Saint John standing at full length beside his body, and its archaic iconography makes the attribution to the authors of the Barberini cross doubtful.

The Maestà of Otricoli, in the church of Santa Maria Assunta, was studied by Ranucci in 1996 in an article on the influence of Spoleto in southern Umbria. Its throne with a pale striated backrest connects it with the Spoletan corpus; the movement of the Child, who turns to look up at his mother, has been related to Coppo di Marcovaldo, and a date after 1261 is likely.

The *Dizionario Biografico* records the painters related to the two Spoletans. [Ada Labriola](/scholars.html#labriola-ada) (1987) connected with them [Rinaldo di Ranuccio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Rainaldetto-di-Ranuccio.html), the Spoleto painter of the next generation, and Margarito and Ristoro d'Arezzo, whose signed dossal is at Santa Maria delle Vertighe near Monte San Savino. [Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos) (1973) related them to the Maestro di San Francesco.

[Serena Romano](/scholars.html#romano-serena) (1982) connected them with the author of two scenes in the Lower Church of San Francesco at Assisi, the Renunciation of Worldly Goods and the Dream of Innocent III. Ranucci (2004) related them to an anonymous Roman master whose panel is in the Museo Civico of Viterbo. The network extends from the Marches to Rome and from Arezzo to the Abruzzo.

The attributed corpus therefore describes a workshop and a following rather than two individual hands. The signed works give the centre; the attributed works give the field of influence around it, with the attributions growing less secure as one moves from Spoleto towards Orvieto, Otricoli, the Marches, Rome and the collections of Antwerp and Pittsburgh.

## The Recovery of Two Painters

The modern recovery of Simeone and Machilone proceeded in stages. The earliest trace is Pichi Tancredi's record of the Ancona panel. For two centuries the names survived only in local antiquarian writing, and they reached the printed literature in the early twentieth century, through Posti (1912), Bandini (1921) and [Raimond van Marle](/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond) (1923).

The decisive step was the publication in the *Bollettino d'Arte* in 1926 of an article by Ozzola, "Un Crocifisso romanico firmato da Simeone e Machilos", which made known the signed cross then in the Bastianelli collection in Rome. From that moment the two painters had a surviving signed work.

In 1929 two works placed them in wider contexts. Sandberg-Vavalà's study of the Italian painted cross situated the Barberini cross in the history of the type and related it to Giunta Pisano; Serra's survey of the art of the Marches relayed the Ancona tradition with the date 1220.

The Antwerp dossal entered the discussion in 1937, when Meiss published it as Sienese. In 1949 Garrison read the fragmentary signature on its footstool and returned it to the Spoletans, and in the same year his illustrated index of Italian Romanesque panel painting gave the corpus its first systematic framework.

The second half of the twentieth century placed the two painters in successively wider frames: the studies of Bologna and Severi (1962) on Duecento painting, the catalogue of the Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria by [Francesco Santi](/scholars.html#santi-francesco) (1969), the work of Garzelli (1972) and Boskovits (1973), the studies of Nessi (1979), Romano (1982), Testa and Davanzo (1984), Labriola and Marques (1987), Fratini and Todini (1989).

The cross entered the Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica in 1962, and it was catalogued by Vodret (1998) and in the systematic catalogue of Palazzo Barberini (2008). Ranucci's studies of the Otricoli Maestà (1996) and of the Roman master of Viterbo (2004), and her entry in the *Dizionario Biografico* (2006), brought the research together.

Recent work has continued to use the Barberini cross as a point of comparison. [Angelo Tartuferi](/scholars.html#tartuferi-angelo) (2015) compared it with a painted cross formerly attributed to Rinaldo di Ranuccio, and the study of painting and sculpture between the duchy of Spoleto and the Abruzzo has reconsidered the Bugnara Madonna and its inscription.

Two observations arise from this history. The first is how much depends on a few physical discoveries: the publication of the cross in 1926, the reading of the Antwerp footstool in 1949, the recognition of the Bugnara inscription. The second is how often errors have been repeated: the Antwerp date, the kinship of the two painters, the name of Machilone's son. The present entry tries to keep the evidence and the conjectures apart.

## Significance

Simeone and Machilone are significant first as named painters. In a century in which most Italian painters are anonymous, they signed their works, named their city and, in one case, their patroness. Their inscriptions make them two of the best-documented painters of central Italy in the mid-thirteenth century.

They are significant as heads of a school. Their workshop, active for more than thirty years, carried the Spoletan tradition from Alberto Sozio into the second half of the thirteenth century, and its influence reached Orvieto, Otricoli, the Marches and the Abruzzo, and the next generation of Spoleto painters.

They are significant for the history of the painted cross. The Barberini cross shows the adoption of Giunta Pisano's *Christus patiens* in Umbria within two decades of its invention, and its softening of Giunta's model shows a regional workshop adapting rather than copying.

They are significant for the history of patronage. The portrait of *domina Maria* at the foot of the Barberini cross is an early instance of a lay woman commissioning a painted cross and having herself shown in it. The Bugnara Madonna, with its tradition of offerings of grain, shows a popular devotion that the workshop served.

They are significant for the history of workshop practice. The Bugnara inscription records a father and son working together and raises the question whether a painting workshop also carved statues, or only painted them. Either answer illuminates the organisation of artistic work in thirteenth-century central Italy.

They are significant, finally, for the history of scholarship. Their recovery, from a lost seventeenth-century manuscript to a signed cross published in 1926 and a signature read in 1949, shows how a small corpus can be built, and how easily errors enter it and are repeated.

The works themselves, the cross with its kneeling patroness, the dossal with its four stories of the Virgin, the painted Madonna of Bugnara, the jewelled Madonna of Orvieto, are the best introduction to the two painters. The catalogue that follows presents them, with the lost panel of Ancona and the works of the school.

Where no free image exists, the works are described in words. The descriptions are based on the published literature and on photographs consulted but not reproduced, and they are kept as precise as the sources allow.

## Catalogue Entry

### The Lost Panel of Ancona

The earliest work of Simeone and Machilone recorded in the sources is a panel that stood in the choir of the canons of the cathedral of San Ciriaco in Ancona. It is known only through the seventeenth-century manuscript *Anconologia* of Giovanni Pichi Tancredi, itself now lost, and through the modern writers who relayed its content.

According to this tradition the panel bore the signature *SIMEON ET MACHILON SPOLETANI* and a date. It is the source of the epithet *Spoletani*, by which the two painters identified themselves, and the only record of their activity on the Adriatic coast.

The date is given as 1230 by Posti, Garrison and the *Dizionario Biografico*, and as 1220 by Serra's survey of the art of the Marches (1929). The difference of ten years cannot now be resolved, because neither the panel nor the manuscript survives.

The subject is reported variously as a Deposition and as a Crucifixion. The two subjects are related but distinct, and the variation probably reflects the imprecision of the transmission rather than a real ambiguity in the panel.

The panel disappeared from the cathedral around 1728. Nothing is known of the circumstances of its loss: it may have been removed during a renovation of the choir, destroyed, or sold.

The value of the record is chronological. It shows that the two painters were working in partnership by the 1220s or 1230s, a generation before their surviving signed works, and that their reputation was established well beyond Spoleto.

It also shows the reach of their clientele. The canons of a cathedral on the Adriatic turned to painters from Spoleto, across the Apennines, which confirms the importance of the routes that connected Umbria with the Marches.

The Ancona panel should therefore be cited as evidence of early joint activity, with its date given as disputed. Any argument that requires the precise year 1230 rests on a contested reading of a lost record of a lost work.

### The Painted Cross of Palazzo Barberini, 1257

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-10.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-10-zoom.jpg" alt="A painted cross hanging on wires against a grey wall. Christ hangs dead on a deep blue cross, his head with closed eyes fallen on his right shoulder, his body curving slightly, in a pink-white striped loincloth, his feet crossed. A pale ornamental band borders the field beside his body. At the ends of the arms, the Virgin in red and blue at the left and Saint John in pink at the right, both raising a hand to the face. At the top, a medallion of Christ above a red band with white letters. At the foot, a small kneeling woman in a dark mantle above a plaque with four lines of writing.">
  <figcaption>Simeone and Machilone, painted cross, signed and dated 1257, tempera on panel. Rome, Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Palazzo Barberini, inv. 2465. Photo: Francesco Bini, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The painted cross in Palazzo Barberini is the principal surviving work signed by Simeone and Machilone. It came from the Roman collection of R. Bastianelli; nothing is known of its earlier history or its original church. It was published in 1926 and entered the Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica in 1962.

Christ is the *Christus patiens*. His head, framed by a large gilded halo, falls on his right shoulder; his eyes are closed, his face long and grave, his hair falling in dark locks over the shoulder. His arms are stretched almost horizontally, the hands open and pierced, with blood running from the wounds.

The body curves slightly to the side, the hips swinging outwards, following the model of Giunta Pisano but with a gentler arc. The anatomy is drawn in lines: the ribs as curved striations, the abdomen divided into schematic segments. The flesh is pale and yellowish. The legs descend and the feet are crossed, pierced by a single nail.

The loincloth is a light fabric, pinkish white, crossed by dark stripes, knotted at the waist and falling in folds to the knee. Behind the body the cross is painted a deep blue, and the field beside the body is bordered by a pale band of repeated ornament.

At the ends of the arms stand the mourners. At the left the Virgin, in a dark red mantle over blue, raises her hand to her face in grief. At the right Saint John, in pink and pale blue garments, turns towards Christ and raises his hand to his cheek. Both are shown in three-quarter view, a disposition that the literature considers characteristic of Spoletan crosses.

At the top of the cross, a small medallion encloses a half-length Christ blessing against a blue ground, carried according to the older literature by two angels, now largely lost. Below it a red band carries the titulus in white letters, *[IH]S NAZARENU[S] REX IUDEOR[UM]*.

At the foot, below Christ's feet, the blue continues with two small red streams of blood. Below, a kneeling woman in a dark mantle raises her hands in prayer: she is the donor, named in the inscription beneath her as *domina Maria*. The inscription and the donor are examined in the next entry.

The cross is damaged: its edges are irregular, the cimasa and the base are broken, and the paint surface is abraded in several areas. Its dimensions are not given in the sources consulted; the figure of 104 × 74 centimetres given in the previous version of this entry belongs to the Vatican processional cross and has been removed.

### Domina Maria and the Inscription

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-11.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-11-zoom.jpg" alt="Close detail of the lower part of a painted cross, with heavy craquelure. At the top, a strip of blue with two red streams of blood. Below, on a brown ground, a kneeling woman in a dark grey mantle drawn in outline raises her hands in prayer towards the left. Beneath her, a pale plaque carries four lines of writing, the first two in red, the last two in black Gothic letters, reading in part ANNO DNI M CC L V and DNA MARIA FECIT FIE. Below the plaque, the broken end of the cross.">
  <figcaption>Simeone and Machilone, the donor domina Maria and the inscription with the signature and the date 1257. Detail of the painted cross, Rome, Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Palazzo Barberini. Photo: Francesco Bini, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The detail shows the lower end of the Barberini cross, the part that carries the most important historical information: the portrait of the donor and the inscription with the names of the painters, the date and the name of the patroness.

The donor kneels at the left, turned towards the right, towards the foot of the cross above her. She wears a long dark grey mantle that covers her head and falls to the ground in a broad curve. Her face is pale, her hands raised in prayer, the fingers drawn in fine lines. The figure is small and drawn mostly in outline.

Beneath her a pale plaque carries four lines of writing, ruled with thin red lines. The first two lines are in red letters, the last two in black Gothic letters. The first gives the signature, *SYMEON ET MACHILO[N]*; the second, damaged, completes it, apparently with the verb and the words *hoc opus*, "this work".

The third line reads *ANNO D(OMI)NI M CC L V*, and the fourth begins with *II*, completing the date as 1257, and continues *D(OMI)NA MARIA FECIT FIE(RI)*, "the lady Maria had it made". The reading 1258, found in some writers and in the title of the photograph on Wikimedia Commons, would require a further stroke that is not visible.

The title *domina* indicates a woman of some standing, but nothing more is known of her. Whether she commissioned the cross for her private devotion, for a chapel, or as a gift to a church cannot be established from the inscription.

The portrait of a donor at the foot of a painted cross is relatively rare in the mid-thirteenth century, and it is the one point at which this cross admits a particular historical person into an otherwise typological image. It anticipates the more frequent donor portraits of the later Duecento and the Trecento.

The surface of the detail is covered by a dense craquelure, and losses show the wood at the edges. Below the plaque, the end of the cross is broken, and the original termination is lost.

The inscription is the only one of the signed works to give, in a surviving object, the names of both painters, a date and a patron together. It is the foundation of the chronology of the workshop.

### The Antwerp Dossal

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-12.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-12-zoom-v2.jpg" alt="A horizontal panel divided into three parts by painted columns. In the centre the Virgin, in a dark blue mantle over a red-orange dress, sits frontally on a throne with a pale striated rounded backrest and a green cushion, holding the Child on her lap; two small angels fly above. At the left, the Birth of the Virgin with Saint Anne reclining before towers, and below it the Annunciation with the angel and the Virgin before a building. At the right, the Assumption with the Virgin among angels above the apostles, and below it the Nativity with the Virgin lying on a mattress in a rocky landscape and the bathing of the Child.">
  <figcaption>Simeone and Machilone, dossal with the Virgin and Child enthroned and four scenes from the life of the Virgin, c. 1250–1260, tempera on panel, 79 × 120 cm. Antwerp, Museum Mayer van den Bergh, MMB 0195. Photo: DAMS Antwerpen / Museum Mayer van den Bergh, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The dossal in the Museum Mayer van den Bergh in Antwerp is a horizontal panel, 79 by 120 centimetres, of the type widely diffused in central Italy in the mid-thirteenth century: an altar panel with a hieratic image in the centre and narrative compartments at the sides. It is divided into three parts by painted columns.

In the centre the Virgin sits frontally on a throne, holding the Child on her lap. She wears a dark blue *maphorion* over a red-orange dress that falls in broad, heavy folds to the footstool. The throne has a tall rounded backrest, pale with dark vertical striations, and a large green cushion. Two small angels fly at the upper corners.

The left side holds two scenes. Above is the Birth of the Virgin: Saint Anne reclines on a bed before a group of pink and blue towers, attended by women, while below a woman prepares the bath. Below is the Annunciation: the angel at the left, in a pink garment, greets the Virgin, who stands at the door of a tall building.

The right side holds two further scenes. Above is the Assumption, or the Dormition with the Assumption: the Virgin stands among angels above a group of apostles. Below is the Nativity of Christ, in a rocky landscape: the Virgin lies on a white mattress in a cave, the Child and the animals appear above her, the midwives bathe the Child below, and Joseph sits apart.

Read chronologically, the cycle runs down the left column and up the right: Birth of the Virgin, Annunciation, Nativity, Assumption. The beginning and the end of the Virgin's earthly story stand at the same height, on either side of her enthroned image.

The dossal carries no date. A fragmentary inscription in the lower middle zone, on the footstool of the throne, was read by Garrison in 1949 as part of the signature of Simeone and Machilone. Meiss, who published the panel in 1937, had not seen it and gave the dossal to a Sienese painter of 1250–1260; the date has been accepted by Todini, Labriola, Marques and Ranucci.

The date 1258, given to the dossal in the previous version of this entry and in some online sources, is not inscribed on it. It appears to derive from the reading 1258 of the Barberini cross. The two works may well be close in date, but their relation rests on style, not on inscriptions.

The pale striated throne, the long face of the Virgin and the heavy red folds of her dress recur in the Orvieto Madonna and in the Otricoli Maestà, and they form one of the clearest links between the signed works and the attributed works of the school.

### The Madonna of Bugnara, 1262

  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-13.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-13-zoom.jpg" alt="A screenshot of a three-dimensional model of a carved and painted wooden statue, against a white background with a large pale grey logo. The Child, in a long brown tunic, sits high on her left knee, his bare feet on her hand, raising his right hand. On the front of the footstool is a line of writing. Three small numbered markers of the viewer appear on the figures.">
  <figcaption>Machilone and his son, Madonna and Child enthroned, called the Madonna delle Concanelle, 1262, carved and painted wood with traces of gilding, 121 × 48 × 28 cm, from the church of the Madonna della Neve, Bugnara. L'Aquila, MuNDA – Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo, OPA 1217. </figure>

The Madonna of Bugnara, called the Madonna delle Concanelle, is a carved and painted wooden statue of the Virgin and Child enthroned, with traces of gilding, 121 centimetres high. It comes from the church of the Madonna della Neve at Bugnara, in the province of L'Aquila, and is now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo, in room 6 of the sixteenth-century castle.

The image is a screenshot of the three-dimensional model published by the museum on Europeana. The licence does not allow it to be cropped or altered, and the logo of the viewer and its three numbered markers remain visible. The model can be explored in full on [Europeana](https://www.europeana.eu/en/item/281/PM_ABR_3D_0005).

The Virgin sits in strict frontality, her body conceived as a tall, almost columnar block. Her head is covered by a close veil that leaves the pale oval of the face exposed: large eyes, a long straight nose, a small closed mouth. The museum describes the execution as imposing and primitive: the massive head set between square shoulders, the body anchored to the ground by shoes like rustic clogs.

The Child sits high on his mother's left knee, his bare feet resting on her open hand. He wears a long tunic, faces the viewer, raises his right hand near his mother's face and extends his left arm outward. The Virgin's right hand is open, palm up, as if it once held an object or received offerings.

The throne is a box-like structure with a large red cylindrical cushion with a patterned surface. Its back is painted with a yellow ground with rows of dark vertical motifs and small lozenges, bordered in blue, imitating a precious textile or inlay. The footstool carries the inscription on its front face.

The inscription, as published by the museum and the Direzione Regionale Musei Abruzzo, reads: *(A)NNO D(OMI)NI MCCLXII M(A)CH(I)LON(US) ET CE(P)LA(.) EI FILIUS (DE)P(IN)S(E)RU(N)T HOC OPUS*, "in the year of the Lord 1262, Machilonus and Ce…, his son, painted this work". It is the only inscription of the group whose full text has been published.

The museum identifies Machilonus with the Spoleto painter only with a "perhaps". The verb *depinserunt* on a carved statue may mean that the workshop both carved and painted, or that Machilone and his son painted a statue carved by another. The son's name, conventionally expanded to Ceccarello, is a conjecture.

The name of the statue recalls a local tradition reported by the museum: the site was once that of a temple of Ceres, and the women of the village continued to offer grain to the Virgin in small copper bowls, the *concanelle*. If the Machilonus of the inscription is the Spoleto painter, the statue is the last dated trace of his activity, and Simeone no longer appears with him.

### The Madonna of the Confraternita del Carmine, Orvieto

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-14.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-14-zoom.jpg" alt="A tall round-headed panel with a gold ground framed by a red arch. The Virgin, in a blue mantle sprinkled with small gold stars over a red dress, sits frontally on a throne with a pale rounded backrest crossed by thin red bands, flanked by slender posts and ornamented sides in red and white. On her lap the Child, in a red-gold striated garment, raises his right hand in blessing and holds a book. Two roundels of deep blue glass are set into the gold on either side of the Virgin's head.">
  <figcaption>Simeone and Machilone (attributed), Madonna and Child enthroned, mid-thirteenth century, tempera and gold on panel with glass insets, from the Confraternita del Carmine. Orvieto, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. Photo: Sailko, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/">CC BY 3.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Madonna and Child enthroned in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo at Orvieto carries no signature, and its connection with Simeone and Machilone rests on attribution. It belonged to the Confraternita del Carmine, was catalogued in 1888 and had probably been moved from the church of Santa Maria del Carmine around 1559, during its remodelling.

The panel is vertical and round-headed. A red arch follows the curve of the top and frames a gold ground. Two roundels of deep blue glass are set into the gold on either side of the Virgin's head, a costly refinement that would have given the image the appearance of a jewelled object.

The Virgin sits frontally, wrapped in a blue *maphorion* edged at the forehead with dots and sprinkled with small gold stars, over a vermilion dress that falls in broad folds to the bottom of the panel. Her face is a long oval, with large dark eyes, a long straight nose and a small closed mouth, in the Byzantine type of the signed works.

The Child sits on her lap, facing outward, raising his right hand in blessing and holding a closed book at his side. He wears a tunic and mantle in warm red, densely striated with gold. The literature describes him as an adult in a toga holding the Gospel: his dress and posture present him as teacher and lawgiver rather than infant.

The throne has a pale rounded backrest crossed by thin red bands, flanked by slender posts with finials and rounded bolsters at the armrests. Its lower sides are covered with dense geometric patterns in red and white, in imitation of inlay. The pale striated backrest closely recalls the throne of the Antwerp dossal.

Testa and Davanzo (1984) and Fratini (1989) suggested that the panel may be the surviving centre of a tabernacle with shutters. A Madonna in the church of San Giusto near Pievebovigliana, in the Marches, has been described as a faithful but provincial version of it, which shows that the Orvieto panel served as a model.

Ranucci (1996) attributed the panel to the two painters and dated it around the middle of the century, close to the signed works; the *Dizionario Biografico* gives a date around 1265, possibly earlier. A date after 1262 would make a joint execution difficult, since Simeone no longer appears in the record.

The panel is the best-preserved work associated with the workshop. Its extensive gold, its glass insets and the gold-striated garments of the Child show a material richness that the damaged signed works now convey only imperfectly.

### The Painted Cross from San Lodovico, Orvieto

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-15.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Simeone-e-Machilone/simeone-e-machilone-15-zoom.jpg" alt="A painted cross on a gold ground with a deep blue cross behind the body. Christ hangs dead with his head fallen on his right shoulder and closed eyes, in a long silvery-white loincloth with dark stripes, his feet crossed on a brown footrest. A checkered border of red, black and white squares frames the field beside his body. At the ends of the arms, the Virgin in blue at the left and Saint John at the right, both grieving. At the top, Christ in a blue mandorla carried by two angels above a dark tablet inscribed IHS NAZARENUS REX IUDEORUM. At the foot, a small damaged roundel.">
  <figcaption>Orvietan follower of Simeone and Machilone, painted cross, c. 1250–1275, tempera and gold on panel, from the monastery of San Lodovico. Orvieto, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. Photo: Sailko, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/">CC BY 3.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

This painted cross in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo at Orvieto comes from the monastery of San Lodovico in the city and probably belonged originally to the Poor Clares of San Lorenzo delle Vigne. It is now given to an Orvietan follower of Simeone and Machilone and dated around 1250–1275.

Its history of attribution shows how unstable such attributions can be. It has been given in turn to Coppo di Marcovaldo or his son Salerno, to Margaritone d'Arezzo and to the two Spoletans themselves, before the present attribution to a local follower.

The whole surface is gilded, and against the gold stands a deep blue cross behind Christ's body, edged with pink bands. The field beside the body is bordered by a checkered band of small red, black and white squares. In the cimasa a dark tablet carries the titulus *IHS NAZARENUS / REX IUDEORUM*.

Christ is the *Christus patiens*. His head, framed by a large halo with incised rays, falls on his right shoulder; the eyes are closed, long locks of hair fall over the shoulder. The body inflects in a gentle arc, and the feet are crossed on a brown footrest, pierced by a single nail.

The loincloth is long and ample, silvery white traced with fine dark stripes, knotted at the waist and falling in layered folds almost to the knee. Its striped pattern recalls the loincloth of the Barberini cross, but its treatment is more voluminous.

At the ends of the arms the Virgin, in a blue *maphorion*, raises her hand to her face, and Saint John, in pale garments, inclines his head and brings his hand to his cheek. Both are shown in three-quarter view, as in the Barberini cross. In the cimasa Christ appears in a blue mandorla carried by two flying angels: the formula of the Spoletan cross, better preserved here than at Palazzo Barberini.

The comparison with the Barberini cross explains both the Spoletan attribution and its qualification. The structure, the mourners and the cimasa are shared; the checkered border, the longer loincloth and the different handling of the anatomy point to another hand.

The cross shows the diffusion of the Spoletan model in Orvieto, a city open to several currents, where Coppo di Marcovaldo also worked for the Servites. For a community of Franciscan nuns, it gave a Spoletan image of the suffering Christ for meditation.

### The Processional Cross of the Pinacoteca Vaticana

The Pinacoteca Vaticana keeps a processional cross, inv. MV.40005, catalogued by the museum as Orvietan school (Simeone and Machilone?), around 1260–1270. No image with a free licence could be found, and the cross is described here from the museum's information and the previous version of this entry. The old literature cites a cross by the two painters in the Vatican with the number "n. 5", which corresponds to the present inventory.

According to the museum, the cross is painted in tempera and gold on a poplar panel and measures 104 by 74 centimetres. Its size and function distinguish it from the large crosses hung above the altar: a processional cross was carried on a staff and had to be legible in motion and from a distance.

The central figure is the living Christ, not the dead *Christus patiens* of the Barberini cross. He is shown with open eyes, though his body has some of the features of the newer type: an emaciated torso and a slight curve. The loincloth is short, greenish blue, and the feet are crossed and pierced by a single nail, with streams of blood.

The mourners stand at full length on the gold ground beside Christ's body, each on a narrow strip of green ground suggesting the hill of Calvary: the Virgin at the left in a dark blue mantle over red, Saint John at the right in a brown garment. The ends of the arms carry small roundels, now abraded.

The rectangular cimasa holds a red field that probably carried the titulus. There is no trace of the Christ in a mandorla carried by angels that appears in the Barberini and San Lodovico crosses.

The cross differs fundamentally from the signed cross of 1257 in iconography: a living Christ instead of a dead one, the mourners beside the body instead of on the arms, no Ascension at the top. A workshop that adopted Giunta's most modern model in 1257 would hardly return a few years later to an archaic type.

The museum's question mark reflects this difficulty. The date it proposes, around 1260–1270, falls after the signed works and around the time of Machilone's last documented activity in 1262, when Simeone no longer appears.

The cross is best described as a work of the Spoletan or Orvietan sphere of the mid-thirteenth century, related to Simeone and Machilone by its types and its idiom, but not attributable to them with confidence.

### The Maestà of Otricoli

The panel of the Madonna and Child enthroned in the church of Santa Maria Assunta at Otricoli, in the chapel of the Madonna della Misericordia to the right of the presbytery, is one of the works most closely associated with the Spoletan painters in southern Umbria. No image with a free licence could be found, and the panel is described here in words.

Otricoli lies at the southern edge of Umbria, near the Tiber and the border with Lazio, the territory in which, according to Ranucci, the art of the two Spoletans became a point of reference. Ranucci devoted a study to the panel in 1996, on the influence of Spoleto in southern Umbria.

The panel has had a turbulent modern history. It was damaged during the Second World War, restored in 1965, stolen in 1978 and recovered, and recently restored again. The upper part is reduced to bare wood, the edges are broken, and the gold ground has almost disappeared.

The Virgin sits on the throne with her body slightly turned, her head inclined towards the Child. She wears a dark blue, almost black *maphorion* over a red dress that falls in deep folds, and a white veil marked by small groups of dark dashes. She holds the Child on one arm and points to him with her raised hand, the gesture of the Byzantine Hodegetria.

The Child, in a red tunic and a greenish mantle, turns towards his mother and lifts his face to look up at her, reaching towards her chest. The upward gaze creates a relationship between mother and son that departs from the frontal Child of the Orvieto Madonna.

The throne has a rounded backrest painted with vertical cream and yellow bands crossed by fine lines: the pale striated backrest of the Antwerp dossal and the Orvieto Madonna, one of the clearest links with the Spoletan corpus. Red uprights with a meander pattern frame it.

The attribution balances two components. The throne, the Byzantinising face of the Virgin and the red and dark blue colour scheme place the panel in the language of Simeone and Machilone; the movement of the Child and the exchange of gazes have been related to Coppo di Marcovaldo, to his *Madonna del Bordone* in Siena, dated 1261, and to his Madonna for the Servites of Orvieto.

If the elements derived from Coppo are accepted, a date after 1261 is likely, which would place the panel at the end of Machilone's documented activity or beyond it. The most prudent description is that of a work by a painter of the Spoletan school active in southern Umbria in the third quarter of the thirteenth century.

### Comparison: Rinaldo di Ranuccio, the Triptych in Pittsburgh

The portable triptych in the Frick Art Museum in Pittsburgh, inv. 1970.52, is attributed by the museum to Rinaldo di Ranuccio of Spoleto and dated around 1270. No image with a free licence could be found, and it is described here in words, as a comparison for the following of Simeone and Machilone. Rinaldo has his own entry, [Rainaldetto di Ranuccio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Rainaldetto-di-Ranuccio.html).

The triptych is painted in tempera on panel and measures 127 by 104.8 centimetres when open. The museum's title records the Madonna and Child enthroned, four scenes of the Passion and Saints Anthony and Francis; the saints, who do not appear on the interior, must occupy the exterior faces of the wings.

In the central panel the Virgin sits enthroned, in a dark blue *maphorion* over a red dress, holding the Child on her left arm and pointing to him with her right hand, the gesture of the Hodegetria. The Child turns towards her and reaches out to her, as in the Otricoli Maestà.

The left wing carries the Betrayal, with Judas embracing Christ amid soldiers with spears and torches, and below it the Flagellation, with Christ bound to a column between two tormentors. The right wing carries the Deposition, with Nicodemus drawing the nails from the feet, and below it the Lamentation, with the Virgin bending over her son's face.

The presence of Saints Francis and Anthony of Padua, canonised in 1232, places the triptych in a Franciscan context and suggests that it was made for a Franciscan community or for a patron connected with the order.

The attribution to Rinaldo rests on comparison with his signed crosses in Fabriano and Bologna, the latter dated 1265. Labriola (1987) related Rinaldo to the workshop of Simeone and Machilone, of which he appears as a successor in the next generation.

The comparison shows the continuity and the change of the Spoletan school after the two painters. The facial types, the colour scheme and the Marian formula continue; the composition becomes more animated and the relationship between mother and child more intimate.

The triptych is included here only as a comparison. It is not by Simeone and Machilone, and it shows the field of influence of their workshop at the moment when Machilone's documented activity ends.

## Introduction: A Name in Tesserae

Among the monumental images of central Italy around 1200, few are as familiar and as little studied as the mosaic in the upper register of the façade of Santa Maria Assunta, the cathedral of Spoleto. It forms the backdrop of the piazza at the foot of the Via dell'Arringo and is reproduced in every account of the city, yet it has received less critical attention than its importance would lead one to expect. The paradox is sharper because the mosaic is signed and dated. Its signature is no laconic *fecit* but a self-advertisement in rhyming Latin verse, in which a master who calls himself *doctor* and *summus in arte modernus* names himself and the year 1207.

Outside those few words in glass, the master is silent. No document, no second work and no notice in any chronicle has been connected with him, and it has even been questioned whether his name is a personal name. What can be known about Solsternus rests on the mosaic itself, on its inscription and on its place in the history of Spoleto, of its cathedral and of the revival of monumental mosaic in Italy at the beginning of the thirteenth century. Around these facts stands a body of scholarly inference, from which documented evidence, reasoned hypothesis and conjecture have to be carefully distinguished.

The literature devoted to the mosaic alone is small. The one published study is Maria Andaloro's essay "Il mosaico di Solsterno", in the volume *La Cattedrale di Spoleto: Storia, Arte, Conservazione* (2002), edited by <a href="/scholars.html#benazzi-giordana">Giordana Benazzi</a> and Giovanni Carbonara. Two university theses have taken the mosaic as their subject: one by E. Santilli at the Università della Tuscia (2000), on the history and techniques of execution of the mosaic, and one by Fabiana Fiorelli at the University of Pisa (2013). Neither has been published. Everything else comes from wider accounts: the entries on Spoleto (1999) and Umbria (2000) in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale*, Alessio Monciatti's synthesis on mosaic in the age of Frederick II (2005), the diocesan publications, and antiquarian and travel literature going back to 1702.

For the history of the cathedral, <a href="/scholars.html#sordini-giuseppe">Giuseppe Sordini</a>'s study of the documents (1908) and the exhibition "Spoleto sacra 1200", held in 2023 for the 825th anniversary of the dedication, add further evidence. On several points these sources disagree, and the disagreements remain open.

## Spoleto around 1200: Duchy, Commune and Papacy

When the mosaic was set up, Spoleto was recovering from catastrophe and redefining its political allegiances. The city had been the capital of a Lombard duchy from the later sixth century and the seat of an important diocese. Caught in the conflict between papacy and empire, it was destroyed by Frederick Barbarossa<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-barbarossa" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> in 1155. The commune emerged between the end of the eleventh and the beginning of the twelfth century and is documented from 1173. In 1198 Innocent III<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-innocent-iii" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> asserted papal sovereignty over the city, a sovereignty that Frederick II recognised in 1213. The years just before the mosaic were therefore those in which Spoleto passed from the imperial orbit into the territorial state of the Church.

The destruction of 1155 struck the area of the cathedral directly. Barbarossa's biographer describes the highest part of the city, which the imperial troops stormed, as lying opposite the principal church, the seat of the bishop, and the episcopal complex was very probably badly damaged. Frederick is said to have left the city the next morning because of the stench of fire and of the dead. In the two decades that followed, a series of bishops of imperial obedience held the see during the schism, and the cathedral and the bishop's palace probably remained in ruins.

Recovery began with the peace of Venice of 1177 between Frederick and Pope Alexander III<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-pope-alexander-iii" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, who invested a new bishop, Transarico. Around 1178 Transarico moved his residence to San Gregorio Maggiore so that the old bishop's palace could be pulled down to make room for a new cathedral. The building on which Solsternus worked was thus begun about thirty years before the mosaic.

The papal takeover of 1198 was a matter of rapid political action. Elected after the death of Henry VI, Innocent III called Duke Conrad of Spoleto to Narni and obtained his submission, then travelled through the duchy, receiving at Spoleto the submission of Terni and Norcia. The commune was meanwhile extending its own power: it fought a war with Foligno in 1200–1201, and a podestà of Spoleto is first recorded in 1201, when Norcia accepted the city's protection. The mosaic of 1207 therefore belongs to a city that was both newly papal and an expanding commune.

Two objects in the cathedral show the cultural horizons of the decades before 1207. The first is the Byzantine icon of the Virgin that Barbarossa is said to have given to the cathedral in 1185 as a sign of peace. The second is the painted triumphal crucifix signed by an "Alberto Sot[…]" and dated 1187, the oldest dated painted crucifix in the peninsula after that of Guglielmo at Sarzana. Spoleto around 1200 was neither isolated from Byzantine visual culture nor dependent on a single channel for it, and signing and dating works was a living practice in the city.

The icon is of the type of the Haghiosoritissa, the Virgin turned in prayer without the Child. The guide to the exhibition of 2023 calls it the work of a twelfth-century Constantinopolitan painter and the most important painting to have survived the later transformations of the interior. A second Byzantine panel was also in the city: a small Christ blessing, attributed to a Byzantine painter of the early thirteenth century, from the monastery of San Paolo inter vineas and now in the Museo Nazionale del Ducato in the Rocca Albornoz. Its silver revetment shows how precious the images kept in the churches and monasteries of Spoleto could be.

The painter of the cross of 1187, usually called Alberto Sotio, is also credited with frescoes in the church of Santi Giovanni e Paolo, from which the cross comes. They show the martyrdom of the titular saints and the murder of Thomas Becket. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* links the Becket scene to his canonisation in 1173, a year before the consecration of the church in 1174. The entry by M. E. Savi and <a href="/scholars.html#lunghi-elvio">Elvio Lunghi</a> describes Alberto's style as harsh and metallic and traces its influence in later Umbrian painting. <a href="/scholars.html#scarpellini-pietro">Pietro Scarpellini</a>'s entry on Umbria compares his late-Comnenian culture with the mosaics of Monreale, but insists on its original traits: an expressive calligraphy and bright, delicate colours that amount to a recognisably Spoletan manner.

The cathedral was also bound up with civic life. The Via dell'Arringo takes its name from the *arringo*, the assembly of the people, which by a custom documented from the twelfth century gathered in the piazza before the cathedral. The façade therefore faced a space of public deliberation as well as of liturgy, and its inscription was a public statement in a public place.

## The Cathedral, its Consecrations and the Façade

The site of the cathedral is unusual: set against the city walls on an artificial terrace of Roman date above a steep slope, somewhat apart from the centre of the town. An episcopal complex is documented there from 956, comprising the church of Santa Maria del Vescovato, a martyrial church of Saint Primianus whose semi-annular crypt survives, a baptistery and the bishop's residence. A new reconstruction began in the second half of the twelfth century, and Innocent III consecrated the building in 1198, perhaps still incomplete, in the dimensions it keeps today.

The incompleteness of the building in 1198 governs the relationship between the consecration and the mosaic of 1207. The cathedral was consecrated in 1198 but completed at the beginning of the thirteenth century, and the diocesan historical summary records a second consecration, perhaps in 1216, under Honorius III<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-pope-honorius-iii" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, once the works were finished. The mosaic thus falls within an interval in which the building was in use but still being completed, and its date of 1207 is the only firm chronological point inscribed on the façade.

The evidence for both consecrations is weaker than the dates suggest. Sordini, writing in 1908, observed that Cadolini, Sansi and other historians gave the consecration by Innocent III in 1198 on the authority of a chronicle source, the Anonimo Fuxense. He added that an old memorial once in the cathedral had only increased the confusion over the date and the name of the pope. His own conclusion was cautious: the consecration certainly took place no earlier than the last years of the twelfth century.

Several modern accounts state that what Innocent III consecrated in 1198 was an altar, while the church was still being built, rather than the whole building. The evidence for 1216 also concerns an altar. Sordini cites the historian Serafini, according to whom relics were placed by Pope Honorius III "in consecratione eius Altaris An. 1216"; the day is given as 3 August in one passage and 30 August in another. The two events are therefore better described as consecrations recorded by later sources than as documented dedications of the finished building.

The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* records some consequences of the consecration of 1198: buildings attached to the old church were demolished, and a road was opened towards the façade. The same entry describes the plan of the new church. It had three naves, the central one twice as wide as the aisles, a transept that did not project beyond the aisles and was enlarged in the fourteenth century, and probably a single semicircular apse. The interior was entirely remodelled in the seventeenth century, but the façade survived.

The façade is the product of several campaigns. An initial project, with an architraved portal, a porch and a central rose window above a blind gallery, was abandoned; vertical elements that break off incongruously show the change of plan, which made room for the great pointed arches of the upper register and for a crowning with crockets of an already Gothic character. The Renaissance portico, built between 1491 and 1504, and the alterations that followed the remodelling of the interior in 1638–1644 changed the façade again. When the nave was widened and the aisles raised in those years, the upper part of the front was extended to match; a painting of about 1636 shows the façade before these works. The present outline of the upper register is therefore not entirely medieval, although the central arch and its mosaic were kept.

The lower parts of the façade carry sculpture and tessellated decoration of their own. The central portal, now under the portico, is carved with a cross springing from an acanthus stem, a motif that the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* derives from the early Christian church of San Salvatore outside the walls; Scarpellini's entry on Umbria treats the portal as the high point of a Spoletan school of Romanesque sculpture. The central rose window, about four metres across, is set in a mosaic frame with the symbols of the Evangelists in the spandrels and was last restored in the 1990s. Tessellated decoration was thus used on the façade below the mosaic as well as in it.

Montfaucon, who visited Spoleto at the end of the seventeenth century, noted as a peculiarity of the cathedral that two ambones rose at the sides of the front, facing the square, so that the Gospel and the Epistle could be read to the people gathered there. He saw the same arrangement at Perugia. The pulpits he saw belong to the Renaissance portico, but the use he describes shows a church front turned towards the assembled city.

The mosaic sits in the central of the three pointed arches of the upper register. Since these arches belong to the later, Gothic phase of the façade, the date of 1207 works as a terminus ante quem for at least the central arch. It does not prove that the whole upper façade was complete in that year; the evidence allows only that it must have been largely complete by 1207.

## The Inscription: Text, Translation and Variant Readings

The inscription runs beneath the figures in two lines. The verses naming the artist are in dark letters on a light band, and the line with the date and the names of the operarii is in white letters on a red band below. The fullest scholarly transcription is Alessio Monciatti's, in his entry "Arte musiva" for the *Enciclopedia Fridericiana* (2005), which follows <a href="/scholars.html#andaloro-maria">Maria Andaloro</a>'s essay "Il mosaico di Solsterno" in the monograph on the cathedral published in 2002:

"Hec est pictura quam fecit sat placitura, doctor Solsternus hac summus in arte modernus. Annis inventis cum septem mille ducentis, operarii Palmerius de Sanso Transericus Enrici Diutesalve Pincurinu[s]".

A literal English rendering reads: "This is the picture, which will please well enough, that doctor Solsternus made, the highest of the moderns in this art, in the years reckoned as seven with one thousand two hundred; the operarii [being] Palmerius de Sanso, Transericus [son] of Enricus, Diutesalve Pincurinus". The diocesan publication gives an Italian version that captures the sense: "Questa è la pittura che, destinata a piacere molto, fece Solsterno, sommo tra i maestri attuali in questa arte".

The first two lines are built on internal rhyme, *pictura* with *placitura*, *Solsternus* with *modernus*, *inventis* with *ducentis*, in the manner of the leonine verses common in medieval monumental inscriptions. The dating formula is a periphrasis forced by the metre: the year is "found" by combining seven with one thousand two hundred. The reading 1207 is not in dispute.

The name of the master needs a closer look. In the mosaic the upper line reads DOCTOR SOLSTERNVS, but the T and the E of the name are joined in a ligature: the crossbar of the T runs across the top of the stem it shares with the E, so that the two letters occupy the space of one. The V of the ending is likewise written in a narrow form joined to the N, as in SVMMVS and MODERNVS in the same line. Read without attention to the ligature, the name appears as SOLSERNVS, and this is the form, "Solsernus", recorded by the Benedictine antiquary Bernard de Montfaucon, who travelled through Italy in 1698–1701 and published his *Diarium Italicum* in 1702; the index to Achille Sansi's nineteenth-century history of the commune lists the artist under "Solsterno, Solserno, Solsernus".

The variant therefore arises from the reading of a ligature, not from two different traditions of the name. The ligature of T and E is a common device of monumental inscriptions, used to save space in a line whose length was fixed by the verse, and it explains how an observer reading the letters one by one could miss the T. Letters in a mosaic can be lost or recomposed in restoration, and only the restoration records can confirm that the ligature is original, but the inscription as it stands supports the form Solsternus established in the modern literature.

The names of the operarii show similar variations. In the mosaic the second name is written D SĀSO, with an abbreviation mark above the A that stands for an omitted N; the reading "de Sanso" is therefore correct, and the form "de Saso" found in some transcriptions arises from overlooking the mark. The third name appears as "Diutesalve", very probably a Latinised form of the common Italian name Diotisalvi, followed by a cognomen read as Pincurinus. "Transericus Enrici" uses a patronymic genitive. These names are of a recognisably central Italian type, although onomastics alone cannot establish the origin of the persons named.

The earliest witness does not give these names in full. In the *Diarium Italicum* Montfaucon introduces the inscription as a mosaic work above the door, "musivum opus anno 1207 concinnatum", and copies the verses. He breaks off the second line at "Operarii Palmerius &c.", so the names of the second and third operarii enter the literature only with later transcriptions. In the same passage he notes that a history of Spoleto by Ciampelli had been left unfinished at the author's death, its second volume still in manuscript in his house.

The forms of the names still vary in the literature. Alongside Monciatti's "Palmerius de Sanso Transericus Enrici Diutesalve Pincurinu[s]", local accounts give "Palmerius de Saso, Transericus Enrici, Diutisalve Pingurini", with the abbreviation mark of the second name ignored and the third name in a vernacular form. The same accounts render "hac summus in arte modernus" as a statement that Solsternus made the work "in the modern style". As the next section explains, the Latin does not support that reading.

## "Doctor", "Summus", "Modernus": The Self-Designation and the History of Artists' Signatures

The inscription is remarkable less for naming its maker than for the way it praises him. "Doctor" is a rare title in place of the usual *magister*. In medieval school usage the two were largely interchangeable, and by the twelfth century the jurists of Bologna preferred "doctor", whose basic sense was one who teaches. Applied to a mosaicist, the title presents the art of composing in tesserae as a learned discipline with masters and pupils rather than a mere craft. Whether it reflects an actual status, such as the headship of a workshop that trained others, or a rhetorical amplification dictated by the verse cannot be determined. A nearby comparison is instructive: the Spoletan painter Rainaldo di Ranuccio signed a crucifix of 1265 as "magister", the title one would expect.

"Summus" and "modernus" complete the claim. In medieval Latin "modernus" means "of the present time", as opposed to the *antiqui*. "Hac summus in arte modernus" therefore means "the highest of present-day practitioners of this art", as the diocesan paraphrase renders it, "sommo tra i maestri attuali in questa arte". The sequence "doctor… summus… modernus" stresses the excellence of the master among the mosaicists of his own time. The boast is comparative and contemporary, not a programmatic claim to stylistic novelty. The phrase "sat placitura" adds the register of reception: the image is presented as an object that will delight its viewers, according to a widespread medieval convention in which verses beneath images praise the work and its maker together.

An unusual number of named artists of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries came from Spoleto or worked there. Besides Alberto's crucifix of 1187, they include the painter Petrus, who signed a crucifix at Campi in 1212; the painters Simeone and Machilone, who signed works in 1230 and 1258 as "Spoletani"; Rainaldo di Ranuccio; and, in sculpture, the masters Binello and Rodolfo, who signed the portals of San Silvestro (1195) and San Michele Arcangelo at Bevagna, inscriptions that attest the self-awareness of the Romanesque artists of the region.

The signatures are of different kinds and survive in different states. The work of 1230 by Simeone and Machilone is lost: a panel signed "SIMEON ET MACHILON SPOLETANI" is recorded in the cathedral of Ancona until 1728. Their dossal of 1258, with the enthroned Virgin and Child and four narrative scenes, is in the Museum Mayer van den Bergh at Antwerp. Rainaldo di Ranuccio's crucifix in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna is signed "Magister Rainaldo Ranucii" and dated 1265.

The date of the Petrus cross needs a caution. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* gives 1212, but its Umbria entry offers it as "perhaps" of that year, and the guide to the exhibition of 2023 dates the cross to 1241. The *Enciclopedia* also notes that the cross combines Byzantine and German iconography of the crucifix. Whatever its exact date, it belongs to the same generation of signed works as the mosaic or the next one.

The cathedral itself may carry a second signature. A local description of the central portal reads the name "Gregorius Melioranzio" on its left jamb, beside a small figure playing a fiddle. In sculpture, Scarpellini's entry on Umbria suggests that Binello and Rodolfo, the masters of the Bevagna portals, were also responsible for the transept front of the cathedral of Foligno, dated 1201. Neither concerns Solsternus directly, but both show how often the makers of major works in the area around 1200 put their names on them.

The inscription of Solsternus thus belongs to a regional culture of signing. What sets it apart is the scale of the claim and the prominence of its setting, high on the cathedral façade above the place of civic assembly.

## Who Was Solsternus? The Name, the Operarii and the Limits of Knowledge

Nothing is known of Solsternus beyond the inscription. Monciatti states that it is unknown whether he was Sicilian and that onomastic research has proved unhelpful, and the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* is equally explicit that the painter's provenance is unknown. Andaloro's essay of 2002 remains the fundamental modern study, and the dominant reading of his figurative culture derives from it.

The name itself is a problem. "Solsternus" is not a recognisable baptismal name of any common type. An unpublished master's thesis of 2013 at the University of Pisa by Fabiana Fiorelli, supervised by <a href="/scholars.html#ascani-valerio">Valerio Ascani</a>, states in its abstract that the name traditionally given as Solsternus might not be the artist's name at all, and proposes to discuss the existence of Solsterno as a person. The thesis is under embargo, and its argument cannot be evaluated. The scholarly literature continues to treat Solsternus as the name of the artist.

The three operarii raise a different question. In the institutional vocabulary of central Italian cities an *operarius* was very often an official of the *opera* or *fabbrica*, the body that administered the construction and upkeep of a major church, and the Pisan thesis calls them *fabbricieri*. On this reading the second line of the inscription names not the master's assistants but the officials responsible for the commission, inscribed beside the master as a formula of dating and responsibility. The interpretation fits medieval Italian usage, but no document from Spoleto has yet confirmed it. If it is correct, the operarii represented the commissioning institution, whether the chapter, the bishop, the commune or some combination of them, and say nothing directly about the workshop.

Of the patrons, then, three things can be said. The inscription names no bishop and no pope, which is notable in a decade when papal patronage of mosaic in Rome was intense. The cathedral had been consecrated by Innocent III nine years earlier. And the façade faced the space of the communal assembly. Whether episcopal, capitular or communal initiative drove the commission remains open.

## The Question of Origin: Sicily, Rome, Venice?

Because the inscription says nothing about the master's origin, every proposal rests on stylistic comparison. The most developed position is Andaloro's, restated by Monciatti in 2005. Monciatti groups the Spoleto mosaic with the Pentecost on the triumphal arch of the Greek abbey of Grottaferrata as the clearest witnesses of the arrival in the peninsula of a figurative language and of artists formed in Sicily, datable within the first decade of the thirteenth century under Innocent III. The argument has a historical premise: the great Norman workshops closed after the completion of Monreale, the foundation of William II<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-william-ii-sicily" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>, at the latest in the reign of Tancred (died 1194), releasing skilled mosaicists who carried their technique to the mainland.

It also has a stylistic premise. For Spoleto, Monciatti considers "indubbia la matrice tardocomnena di origine monrealese della sua cultura figurativa", while specifying that the affinities are mostly typological: they are recognised "nei caratteri della Vergine e nella forma del trono, nella conduzione dei panneggi e nel modulo della cornice a racemo". He then adds a crucial qualification: "la scelta della posizione in facciata e l'adozione del modello dell'acheropita lateranense per il Cristo testimoniano invece dell'avvenuta contaminazione con la cultura figurativa romano-laziale, a cui va ricondotta anche la rigidità di alcuni passaggi nella costruzione dei volti dei personaggi".

In this form the Sicilian hypothesis does not claim that Solsternus was Sicilian, nor that he was Greek. It claims that his figurative culture derived from Monreale and was modified by contact with Roman models. There is no documentary basis for calling him Byzantine in the sense of an artist from the Byzantine Empire.

The historical premise rests on what happened in Sicily after Monreale. In Monciatti's account the Monreale cycle was closed at the latest under Tancred. Frederick II did not revive the workshop, and the concentrated monumental production of the island came to an end within a few decades. The only mosaics in Sicily securely dated to Frederick's time are the canopies of the tombs of Roger II and Constance of Hauteville in Palermo cathedral, of about 1215. The Monreale tradition survived by spreading outward, in other media on the island and with the dispersal of the craftsmen.

Grottaferrata is the closest test case. Monciatti dates the Pentecost there close to the consecration of 1204 and reports that many scholars attribute it to the same workshop, or the same craftsmen, as the Pentecost on the end wall of the north transept at Monreale. The two works share regular square tesserae set loosely and irregularly, and similar solutions for the features of the faces. The decoration of the abbey church has been studied by <a href="/scholars.html#pace-valentino">Valentino Pace</a> (1988). No comparable analysis of the tesserae of Spoleto has been published, so the link between the two mosaics rests on date and typology.

Broader formulations leave other channels open. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* places his art within the introduction of Byzantine iconographies and models into the peninsula, "dalla Sicilia normanna ai mosaici veneziani", and describes the mosaic as the work of "maestranze esterne", representative "di una cultura figurativa ufficiale, accademica, di chiara impronta bizantina", in contrast with the more expressive manner of Alberto's crucifix. The diocesan commentary considers Solsterno a foreign master whose origin should be sought in workshops of Byzantine tradition such as Rome, Venice or Sicily.

The Venetian channel is documented on the mainland, but later. According to Monciatti, the apse mosaic of San Paolo fuori le Mura, begun under Innocent III and completed under Honorius III (1216–1227), introduced novelties brought by masters trained in Venice. For most of Frederick II's reign, he adds, this papal enterprise and the workshop of San Marco were the only major mosaic production in the peninsula. The clearest Venetian presence in central Italy is therefore attested in a project completed only after 1216. It offers no firm evidence for Spoleto in 1207.

On these points there is a genuine consensus: the mosaic is not a product of the local painting tradition, and its maker drew on a Byzantine-derived mosaic culture available in Italy around 1200 in Sicily, Venice and, increasingly, Rome. What is not agreed is which channel was decisive. The Sicilian reading is the most specific and the best argued. A Roman formation cannot be excluded, given the papal context, the setting on the façade and the type of Christ, and the difference between a Sicilian-trained master working under Roman influence and a Roman-trained master with Sicilian experience may be beyond what the surviving evidence can settle. A Venetian channel has been suggested in general terms but not argued in detail for Spoleto, and no published argument attributes Solsternus to a Tuscan workshop, while the major Tuscan monumental mosaics, such as the scarsella of the Florentine Baptistery of 1225, postdate Spoleto.

## Iconography: A Deesis on a Façade

The composition is simple and monumental: Christ enthroned and blessing, with the words of John 8:12, "I am the light of the world", in his open book, between the Virgin and a saint labelled "SCS IOHS", within a border of small plant motifs, the *cornice a racemo* that Monciatti singles out as a Monreale-derived feature.

The tips of the thumb and ring finger of Christ's blessing hand touch. Devotional commentary reads the three extended fingers as an allusion to the Trinity and the two joined ones as the two natures of Christ, a later theological reading of a conventional Byzantine gesture of blessing rather than a statement of the artist's intention.

The composition is a Deesis, the Byzantine image of Christ flanked by intercessors, normally the Virgin and John the Baptist, who pray on behalf of humanity. The identity of the second intercessor is a real problem, sharpened by the history of restoration. The label "SCS IOHS" fits either John. The closed book and the beardless, youthful face point to the Evangelist, and most Italian studies identify him as such. But the head was remade in 1927, almost completely or completely according to different accounts, and it is not known whether the new head reproduces the old one. The youthful face on which the identification largely depends may therefore be a twentieth-century interpretation, and the book remains the stronger argument. The substitution of the Evangelist for the Baptist would give the composition a coherent Johannine theme, since the words in Christ's book come from John's Gospel.

The identification still divides the sources. Jacob Burckhardt's *Cicerone* and the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* of 1911 speak simply of Mary and John. Some modern local accounts call the figure John the Baptist. The guide to the exhibition of 2023 names him John the Evangelist, which is the reading of the diocese today. None of these sources argues the point from the state of the head.

The Christ is not a Pantocrator in the Middle Byzantine sense of a half-length image in a dome, but a Christ enthroned and blessing, the type Western sources often call Maiestas. Monciatti observes that he adopts the model of the Lateran acheiropoieta, the venerated image of the Saviour kept at the Lateran, which links the central figure to the most authoritative Roman image of Christ and to the ideological world of the papacy of Innocent III.

The Roman enterprises of the same decades show how such a Christ could carry a papal message. In the apse of Saint Peter's, remade under Innocent III, the *Maiestas Domini* updated the early Christian composition of the *Traditio legis* and included the figures of the *Ecclesia romana* and of the pope. Around 1230 Gregory IX replaced the lamb at the top of the façade of Saint Peter's with an enthroned Christ, the kneeling pope at his feet. At Spoleto no patron appears in the image. The officials of the works are named only in the inscription, and no pope or bishop is named at all.

The choice of a Deesis for the façade of a cathedral dedicated to the Virgin is itself meaningful: it shows the patroness of the church in her role as intercessor, above the main door and above the piazza. Grottaferrata offers a comparable pairing of door and Deesis, a mosaic of Christ enthroned between the Virgin and John, with a small monk at their feet, over the main portal in the narthex, variously dated to the eleventh or twelfth century. At Grottaferrata, however, the image is inside the narthex; at Spoleto it is set on the external façade, a position that belongs to Roman practice.

## Style

Any stylistic analysis has to begin from the state of the surface, which is the product of successive restorations and in which at least one head has been largely remade. With that caution, the figures are tall, frontal or nearly frontal, and set against a gold ground with little indication of space. The throne is broad and architectonic. The drapery is articulated in linear patterns that recall, in Monciatti's words, the "conduzione dei panneggi" of Monreale, as does the type of the Virgin, while the "rigidità di alcuni passaggi nella costruzione dei volti" belongs to Roman pictorial culture. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* calls the mosaic "accademica": a learned, controlled, hieratic idiom rather than the agitated late-Comnenian manner of the most dynamic Sicilian scenes.

A stylistic reading therefore has to hold two things together: a typological vocabulary traceable to the Sicilian mosaics of the late twelfth century, and a manner of execution and composition that belongs to the Roman sphere. The visual evidence alone cannot decide whether this combination reflects the career of a single master, the collaboration of different hands or a commission that specified Roman models. The inscription speaks of one *doctor*, but medieval mosaic was a collective enterprise, and the surface of about thirty-two square metres makes the participation of a team virtually certain.

The specialist literature places Spoleto alongside the contemporary Roman enterprises, such as the apse of Saint Peter's remade under Innocent III, rather than ahead of them. The importance of the mosaic lies in its early date, its dated inscription and its setting, not in any precedence over Roman work.

Monciatti's description of the Roman comparison is precise. Compared with Monreale and, for the most part, with Grottaferrata, the surviving fragments of the apse of Saint Peter's look drier: the outlines are emphasised, the gaze is fixed, and there are local stiffenings absent from the Sicilian works. In the figures of the *Ecclesia romana* and of the pope, the Roman mosaic absorbs traits of local Roman painting. It is to this Roman and Latial culture that he traces the "rigidità" of some passages in the faces at Spoleto.

The earliest critical judgement of the style is Jacob Burckhardt's, in *Der Cicerone*. He discusses the Spoleto mosaic in a passage on the Byzantine relapses of mosaic in the time of Innocent III and his successors, but notes that here at least "der byzantinische Stil" is combined "mit einer gewissen Freiheit und Würde", especially in the gestures of Mary and John. He also observes that Christ has regained the youthful form that Byzantine art had replaced with the figure of an old man. His reading predates the restoration of 1927, and so concerns a John whose head had not yet been remade.

## Technique and Materials

No detailed analysis of the tesserae, the setting beds or the working procedures of the Spoleto mosaic has been published outside the conservation chapters of the monograph of 2002. The mosaic uses a gold ground and coloured glass tesserae, with pale tesserae in white, grey and silver for the garments of Saint John, and its inscription is executed in contrasting dark and light letters on bands of different colour.

The comparison with Grottaferrata shows what a technical study would need to test. For the Grottaferrata Pentecost, Monciatti notes "la forma quadrangolare regolare delle tessere e il loro accostamento sprezzato", traits shared with Monreale. Whether the Spoleto mosaic shows the same shapes and setting of tesserae would be a decisive argument for or against the Sicilian-trained hypothesis. The extent of later reintegration is equally critical: a mosaic exposed to the weather for eight centuries and restored several times contains an unknown proportion of replaced tesserae, and any argument from technique must distinguish original from restored areas.

The state of technical knowledge is summed up in the record of the mosaic in the database of Byzantine mosaics of the University of Sussex. It lists gold and coloured tesserae as present, records the presence of silver tesserae as unknown, and states that no analysis has been carried out and no samples taken. Its bibliography consists of Andaloro's essay and Santilli's thesis of 2000 on the history and techniques of execution of the mosaic. That thesis is the only work devoted to the technique of the mosaic, and it is unpublished.

The terms of comparison are better studied than Spoleto itself. Andaloro has examined the structures, techniques and materials of the Norman workshops of Palermo (1998), and Monciatti the practice of composing in tesserae between Rome and Florence up to the mid-thirteenth century (1997). Within the cathedral, the mosaic frame of the central rose window and the pavement of the nave, laid in the Roman tradition with stone, porphyry and serpentine, are the nearest examples of tessellated work. No technical comparison between them and the mosaic of the upper façade has been published.

## Spoleto in the Revival of Monumental Mosaic around 1200

The mosaic of 1207 belongs to a concentration of mosaic projects in central Italy in the early thirteenth century, which Monciatti's synthesis links together. At Grottaferrata the Pentecost, datable close to the consecration of the church in 1204, corresponds so closely to the same scene at Monreale that few have doubted the movement of craftsmen from one to the other. In Rome Innocent III promoted the remaking of the apse of Saint Peter's between 1205 and 1209–1212, whose surviving fragments show comparable working habits but a greater dryness of line and fixity of gaze. The apse of San Paolo fuori le Mura, begun under Innocent III and executed under Honorius III, introduced Venetian novelties brought by masters from the workshop of San Marco. Around 1230 Gregory IX<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gregory-ix" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> replaced the ancient mosaic of the façade of Saint Peter's with a Christ enthroned, the papal patron at his feet. In Florence the mosaic of the scarsella of the Baptistery (1225) names a Franciscan "Jacobus" as its maker, "in tali pre cunctis arte probatus", a phrase close in spirit to Solsternus's "hac summus in arte".

The Florentine inscription is in several respects a counterpart to Spoleto's. It dates the work by the reigning pope and emperor, Honorius and Frederick, and gives the year 1225 and the month of May in which the work began. Monciatti notes that it is the only explicit link between Frederick II and a mosaic, but that the context is open to doubt: the text calls Francis a saint, although he was still alive until 1226 and was canonised only in 1228. Where Florence names pope and emperor, Spoleto names neither, and records instead the officials of the works.

The apse of Saint Peter's was the papal enterprise nearest in date to Spoleto: Monciatti dates its remaking to about 1205–1209/1212, so that the two mosaics were being made in the same years. Only fragments of the Roman apse survive, studied by Antonio Iacobini in 1989. Burckhardt had already set Spoleto beside the Roman works. Of the great apse mosaic of San Paolo, which he dated "seit 1216", he wrote that its detail showed "Rückfälle in den Byzantinismus", relapses into Byzantinism, before turning to Spoleto as a case where the Byzantine style was at least combined with some freedom.

Within this panorama Spoleto holds a distinctive place. With Grottaferrata, it is among the earliest dated witnesses of the spread of Sicilian-trained mosaic culture to the peninsula. It lies outside Rome, in a city that had just passed under papal sovereignty. And it is a façade mosaic, one of the very few of the early thirteenth century still visible in Italy.

The tradition of façade mosaics was especially Roman. During the twelfth century the façades of San Bartolomeo all'Isola, Santa Maria Nova and San Giovanni in Laterano received mosaics, and the first half of the thirteenth century added Santa Maria in Trastevere and the renewal of Saint Peter's; of these, only the façade mosaics of Santa Maria in Trastevere and Santa Maria Maggiore survive. Spoleto thus combines a Roman format, a Byzantine-derived iconography and a Sicilian-derived manner adapted to Roman models. If, as Monciatti suggests, mosaic under Innocent III illustrated the theocratic vision of papal authority, the Spoleto façade, set up in the first decade of papal rule over the city, can be read as a statement of the city's new alignment, although the inscription names no pope.

In Umbria figurative wall mosaic was not a local tradition. Before 1207 the region offers only a ninth-century mosaic Redeemer at Narni. The Cosmatesque pavement of the nave and the mosaic frame of the central rose window show that tessellated decoration was present in the cathedral, but figurative mosaic on this scale was an import. Its legacy in Umbrian painting is hard to trace. Local painters such as the Petrus of the Campi crucifix and Simeone and Machilone took up Byzantine iconographies introduced in this period, but no specific dependence on Solsternus has been demonstrated.

## Restoration History and Conservation

The physical history of the mosaic is known only in outline. According to the diocesan publication, the mosaic, which measures about thirty-two square metres, has undergone several restorations, and in one of the last, in 1927, the head of Saint John was almost completely remade. A local account counts five restorations, the last in 1976 by the Coo.Be.C., and attributes the remaking of the lost head of Saint John in 1927 to the Opificio delle Pietre Dure of Florence.

These accounts agree on the two dates and on the remaking of the saint's head; they differ on whether the head was remade almost completely or completely, and only one names the Opificio and the firm responsible in 1976. The cathedral was the subject of major studies and works after the earthquake of 1997 in Umbria and the Marche, and the monograph of 2002 was produced in that context, but whether the mosaic itself was treated in the campaign that followed has not been established.

This history has consequences for every interpretation. The remade head bears directly on the identification of the saint. Replaced tesserae may affect the inscription, and so the question of the name. And judgements of style on the faces need to be checked against a map of original and restored areas that only the conservation documentation can provide.

The written witnesses earlier than 1927 offer little help with this. Montfaucon's transcription of 1702 records the text of the inscription but not the state of the image. Burckhardt describes the gestures of Mary and John and the youthful Christ, but says nothing of the head of John or of any damage. The *Britannica* of 1911 and the *Catholic Encyclopedia* of 1912 mention the mosaic in a single sentence. None of these sources describes the lost head that was replaced in 1927.

The setting of the mosaic has also changed. The upper part of the façade was extended in the remodelling of 1638–1644, and the central rose window below, with its mosaic frame, was restored again in the 1990s. A painting of about 1636 shows the façade before the seventeenth-century works; whether it shows the mosaic in enough detail to be compared with its present state has not been established.

## Historiography and Critical Fortune

The critical fortune of the mosaic begins with the antiquarians. Montfaucon's transcription of 1702, with its reading "Solsernus", which overlooks the ligature of T and E, and its translation "the ablest of the Moderns in this art", is the earliest known witness. Nineteenth-century travellers mention the work with varying accuracy. Ferdinand Gregorovius, describing an excursion through the Sabina and Umbria in 1861, noted the façade "adorned by a great mosaic, the work of Solferino, bearing the date 1207", a slip presumably prompted by the battle of 1859, and called it an early specimen of Umbrian art, a label that the modern literature, which stresses the non-local character of the work, would reject. Sansi's history of the commune recorded the inscription and its names.

Montfaucon's notice is short but precise. He places the mosaic on the front of the church, above the door, as a "musivum opus anno 1207 concinnatum", copies the verses and stops at the first of the operarii. The remark comes in the same paragraph as his observation on the two ambones facing the piazza, so that the earliest description of the mosaic already sets it in relation to the square in front of the cathedral.

Jacob Burckhardt gave the mosaic its first critical judgement in *Der Cicerone*. He called its maker "einem Maler Solsernus", a painter, just as the inscription calls the work a *pictura*, and he used the same form of the name as Montfaucon. He saw in it a Byzantine style combined with some freedom and dignity, and set it in a sequence of Roman and central Italian mosaics from San Paolo to Santa Costanza. He did not discuss the inscription's claims or the origin of the master.

The form "Solsernus" lasted into the reference works of the early twentieth century. The *Encyclopaedia Britannica* of 1911 describes "a large mosaic of Christ enthroned, with the Virgin and St. John, by the artist Solsernus (1207)". The *Catholic Encyclopedia* of 1912, in an article by Umberto Benigni, says that the façade of 1207 "is adorned with a large mosaic by Solsernus". Gregorovius's "Solferino" belongs to the same period. In the same passage Gregorovius dated the cathedral itself to 617 and to the Lombard duke Theodelapius, a date for which the documentary history of the site, beginning in 956, offers no support.

Local scholarship took a different path, through the documents of the cathedral. Sordini's study of 1908 reviewed the sources for the consecrations and showed how slender the evidence for 1198 was. <a href="/scholars.html#toscano-bruno">Bruno Toscano</a> published *Il duomo di Spoleto* in 1969. The volume edited by Benazzi and Carbonara in 2002 gathered the archaeological, architectural and art-historical research that followed the earthquake of 1997, and Andaloro's essay on the mosaic appeared in it.

The decisive modern contributions belong to the last quarter-century. The entries on Spoleto (1999) and Umbria (2000) in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* gave the mosaic a secure place in the general narrative while insisting on its unknown provenance. Andaloro's essay of 2002, by a specialist both in the iconography of the Deesis, on which she had published as early as 1970, and in Norman Sicilian mosaic, provided the reading based on Monreale. Monciatti adopted it in 2005, within his account of mosaic in the age of Frederick II and Innocent III, after examining mosaic practice between Rome and Florence in the thirteenth century in a study of 1997. Antonio Iacobini's survey of mosaic in Italy from the eleventh to the early thirteenth century (2006) provides the broader framework for the papal and Sicilian projects of the period. The Pisan thesis of 2013 set out to reopen the questions of the name, the patrons and the restorations, but its embargo leaves its contribution inaccessible.

The most recent public presentation of the mosaic is the exhibition "Spoleto sacra 1200", held from 2 April to 16 July 2023 for the 825th anniversary of the dedication of the cathedral. Panels on the Romanesque façade presented "il Redentore benedicente in trono fra la Madonna e S. Giovanni Evangelista, opera di Solsternus del 1207". The exhibition also showed two sources for the interior before the Barberini works: the manuscript of the Barberini visitation of 1610, and a plan drawn in 1614 by the Spoletan Gellio Parenzi for Bishop Maffeo Barberini, now in the Vatican Library. Neither concerns the mosaic directly, but both belong to the documentation that a history of the medieval cathedral has to use.

Several points recur inaccurately outside the specialist literature: the mosaic dated to 1198, confusing it with the consecration; the master called "Byzantine"; the operarii called his assistants; "modernus" taken as a claim to novelty; the façade said to have been completed in 1207; the consecration of 1198 treated as a documented dedication of the finished building, when the sources speak of a church still under construction and, in some accounts, of an altar; and the second consecration under Honorius III given as certain. Each arises from the same mechanism, a cautious inference or a misunderstood word turned into a confident statement.

## Conclusions

Solsternus is a name in glass. The inscription that carries it is a primary document of real importance, both for the date it gives the façade of the cathedral of Spoleto and for the self-image it projects: a mosaicist who calls himself *doctor*, claims to be the best master of his art among his contemporaries, and promises that his work will please. That self-image belongs to a region where artists' signatures were unusually common around 1200, and to a moment when mosaic was being revived, with papal backing, as a prestigious medium in central Italy. It also belongs to a specific building and city: a cathedral consecrated by Innocent III in 1198, perhaps still incomplete, in a city that had just passed from imperial to papal sovereignty, whose citizens gathered in the piazza beneath the image.

Even the chronology of that building is less firm than it seems. The rebuilding began around 1178, after the peace of 1177. The consecration of 1198 rests on a chronicle source reported by later historians, and some accounts describe it as the consecration of an altar. The evidence for 1216 likewise concerns an altar. Against these reported dates, the year in the inscription of the mosaic stands out as a date written on the building at the time, the only firm chronological point inscribed on the façade.

Scholarly consensus holds that the mosaic was made by an artist or workshop from outside Spoleto, working in a Byzantine-derived idiom. The most specific and best-argued hypothesis, Andaloro's as adopted by Monciatti, traces that idiom to the late-Comnenian culture of Monreale, while recognising Roman features in the type of Christ, the setting on the façade and the rigidity of the faces. Roman and Venetian channels remain possible, and a Tuscan origin finds no support in the published literature.

Beyond this, nothing is known: not the artist's birthplace, his training or his other works, nor with certainty whether his name is a personal name at all, although the inscription, read with its ligature, confirms the form Solsternus. The second intercessor may be the Evangelist, but the face that seems to prove it is largely of 1927. The operarii were probably officials of the cathedral works rather than members of the workshop, a reading that still awaits documentary confirmation.

The questions that remain open are concrete. The restoration records of 1927 and 1976, and of any intervention after 1997, would allow original and restored areas to be mapped, and with them the history of the letters of the name. A technical study of the tesserae and setting beds would test the Sicilian hypothesis on material rather than typological grounds. Archival research into the opera of the cathedral in the early thirteenth century would clarify who the operarii were and who commissioned the work.

Until then, the portrait of Solsternus is the one he left in the inscription: a proud, learned master of his own time, whose excellence the citizens of Spoleto were invited to admire, and whose identity beyond that claim remains unrecoverable.

The cathedral of Spoleto stands at the lower end of a broad piazza that descends towards it from the town, so that the façade is seen from above and at a distance, against the wooded slope of the hill behind. The mosaic of Solsternus was designed for this view. Set high in the upper register, it is the only figurative image of the façade and the point towards which the whole front leads the eye.

The view shows the three registers of the façade, built in different campaigns. At the bottom the portico of five round arches, with a pulpit at each end facing the piazza, was built between 1491 and 1504 and is now attributed to Ambrogio Barocci and Pippo di Antonio. It conceals the central portal of the twelfth-century church and changes the proportions of the medieval front.

The middle register is pierced by five rose windows. The central one, about four metres in diameter, is set in a square frame carved with the symbols of the four Evangelists and rests on a small blind loggia whose columns include two telamons. A band of small arches separates this register from the upper one, and the lateral rose windows, smaller and of different designs, give the front a rhythm of circles across its whole width.

The upper register, under a gable whose edges carry stone crockets, contains three tall pointed arches and three further rose windows. The lateral arches are blind and plain; the central one holds the mosaic. The pointed arches and the crocketed gable belong to a phase already described as Gothic, which replaced an earlier project for the façade, and the date of 1207 in the mosaic marks the moment by which at least the central arch was complete.

Within this structure the mosaic appears as a panel of gold and colour set into the pale stone. Its pointed shape follows the arch that contains it, and its inscription forms a dark band across the base of the arch, just above the band of small arches that closes the middle register. At this distance the composition reads clearly: the frontal Christ, the two intercessors turned towards him and the broad throne.

The bell tower at the left rises beside the façade and frames the view from the piazza. The relation between the tower, the gable and the hill behind gives the cathedral its characteristic profile, and the mosaic, at the height of the upper rose windows, occupies the centre of this composition.

The mosaic is the earliest dated element of the present upper façade, and one of the very few façade mosaics of the early thirteenth century still visible in Italy. Façade mosaics were a Roman practice, used on several churches of the city in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and the choice of this format at Spoleto, in the first decade of papal rule, aligned the cathedral with the churches of Rome.

The central portal, now hidden behind the portico, is carved with a cross springing from an acanthus stem, a motif derived from the early Christian church of San Salvatore outside the walls. The central rose window above it is set in a mosaic frame, so that tessellated decoration appears on the façade below the mosaic as well as in it.

The upper part of the front was extended in the remodelling of 1638–1644 to match the widened nave and the raised aisles. The upper façade seen in the view therefore reflects that extension in part, while the central arch and its mosaic were kept.

The façade has been restored several times, and its stone surface and rose windows are in good condition. The view shows the mosaic in its setting, exposed to the weather, which explains the repeated restorations of its surface and the replacement of tesserae over eight centuries.

The mosaic on the façade of the cathedral of Spoleto is the only known work of Solsternus, and his name survives only in its inscription. It occupies the central of the three blind pointed arches of the upper register, above the great rose window and the Renaissance portico, and it faces the piazza that descends towards the cathedral. Dated 1207 by its inscription, it was set up nine years after the consecration of the cathedral by Innocent III, while the upper parts of the façade were still being completed, and in the first decade of papal rule over the city.

The mosaic fills the pointed field and is framed by a border of small plant motifs, blue and pink on a gold ground, set within a narrow red band. The figures stand on a strip of green ground scattered with small plants and rocks, and at each lower corner rises a chalice-shaped vessel on a stem. Below, two lines of inscription run across the whole width: the first in dark letters on a light band, the second in white letters on a red band. The surface, about thirty-two square metres, implies the work of a team under the direction of the master named in the inscription.

At the centre Christ sits frontally on a broad throne decorated with jewels and covered with a green cushion. He wears a blue mantle over a darker tunic and has a cross-shaped halo, with the letters IC XC, the Greek abbreviation of his name, at either side of his head. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds in his left an open book whose two pages read, column by column, "EGO SUM LUX MUNDI", the words of John 8:12. His bare feet rest on a dark green hemisphere, which has been read as an allusion to his dominion over the world.

At Christ's right stands the Virgin, labelled "SCA MARIA", in a dark purple mantle drawn over her head and a green dress. She turns towards her son and raises her hands, one to her breast and one extended, in the gesture of intercession. At his left stands a young, beardless saint labelled "SCS IOHS", in pale grey and white garments, holding a closed book adorned with jewels and extending his right hand towards Christ. The closed book and the youthful face suggest John the Evangelist rather than the Baptist usually found in the Deesis, although the head was remade in 1927.

The composition is the Byzantine Deesis, the image of Christ flanked by intercessors who pray on behalf of humanity, set on the façade of a cathedral dedicated to the Virgin. Placed above the main door and above the piazza, it shows the patroness of the church in her role as intercessor before her son. The central Christ follows the model of the acheiropoieta of the Lateran, the venerated image of the Saviour in Rome, and the setting of a figurative mosaic on an external façade belongs to Roman practice.

Beneath the figures two lines of inscription name the master and praise his art, give the date and record the operarii of the cathedral works. The master's name is written with a ligature of T and E, which explains the variant Solsernus recorded by Bernard de Montfaucon around 1700.

The style combines two components. The figures, tall, frontal and set against the gold, with drapery articulated in fine linear patterns and the Virgin's type close to those of Monreale, recall Sicilian mosaic of the late twelfth century. The rigidity of some passages in the construction of the faces, the frontal Christ and the composition belong rather to the Roman sphere. The mosaic thus belongs with the Pentecost at Grottaferrata and the apse of Saint Peter's remade under Innocent III, the other great mosaic enterprises of the first decade of the century.

Exposed to the weather for eight centuries, the mosaic has been restored several times, and the present surface contains an unknown proportion of replaced tesserae. The head of Saint John was remade almost entirely in 1927. The gold ground, the border and the inscription have been consolidated, and the colours of the garments, the blue of Christ's mantle, the purple of the Virgin's and the pale grey of John's, are bright and clearly legible. Any argument about the style or the technique of Solsternus has to distinguish the original areas from those renewed in successive campaigns.

The enthroned Christ is the centre of the composition, and his head is the point towards which the gestures of the two intercessors converge. Seen from the piazza far below, it had to be legible at a great distance, and its features are drawn with strong dark contours and simple, large forms.

The face is long and narrow, framed by dark hair parted in the middle and falling in parallel locks to the shoulders, each lock described by rows of tesserae in shades of green, grey and black. The beard is short and forked at the chin. The eyes are large, with dark irises set under high, arched brows that join the line of the long, straight nose.

The modelling of the flesh follows the Byzantine practice of building the face from a greenish shadow towards light tones, with a round spot of pink on each cheek. The transitions are abrupt rather than gradual, and the features are set within firm outlines, a manner that suits a mosaic made to be seen from below and in the open air.

The halo is gold, outlined by a ring of white tesserae alternating with dark ones, like a string of pearls. Within it the arms of the cross are red, set with small jewels in blue and white, the cruciform halo reserved for Christ. At either side of the head appear the Greek letters IC XC, each with its abbreviation mark, in large dark characters on the gold.

Christ looks straight out at the viewer, with a grave, fixed expression. The frontality and the fixity of the gaze recall the "fixity of the gaze" that has been observed in the surviving fragments of the apse of Saint Peter's, remade under Innocent III in the same years.

The gold ground around the head is uneven, with darker tesserae scattered among the gold and patches of different tone. Some of this unevenness belongs to the original working, in which tesserae were set at slightly different angles to catch the light; some of it reflects later repairs, which inserted new tesserae in areas of loss.

The detail shows the qualities that link the mosaic to Sicilian practice: the linear treatment of the hair, the outlined features and the abrupt modelling, all characteristic of the mosaics of Monreale. The rigid construction of the face, however, has been related to the Roman tradition, and the head illustrates the mixture of components that defines the style of Solsternus.

Burckhardt singled out this figure in *Der Cicerone*: in his view Christ had here regained the youthful form that Byzantine art had replaced with the figure of an old man.

The figure of Christ appears to be among the better preserved parts of the mosaic. The features, the hair, the cross of the halo, the blessing hand and the book are clear, while the gold ground shows the irregularities of repeated consolidation. Its original state cannot be distinguished from later work without close technical examination.

  <figcaption>Solsternus, <em>Deesis</em>, 1207, detail: the Virgin. Mosaic. Spoleto, Cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta, façade. Photo: Wolfgang Sauber, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons (cropped).</figcaption>

The Virgin stands at Christ's right, the place of honour, labelled "SCA MARIA" in dark letters on the gold beside her head. She is the patroness of the cathedral, and her presence at the side of her son on the façade presents her as the intercessor of the city before Christ.

She wears the maphorion, the mantle that covers the head and shoulders in Byzantine images of the Virgin, here in a dark purple, over a long green dress. On her forehead the mantle carries a small gold star, one of the three stars that in the Byzantine tradition signify her virginity before, during and after the birth of Christ.

Her face is long, with large dark eyes, a long thin nose and a small mouth, modelled with greenish shadows and a spot of pink on the cheek, like the face of Christ. The features are outlined by dark tesserae, and the edge of the mantle frames the face with a band of lighter tesserae, which separates it from the dark mass of the cloth.

The gesture is the gesture of intercession. The Virgin raises her right hand, open, towards Christ, and holds her left hand before her breast, the palm turned outwards. The two hands, drawn with long, slender fingers, carry the meaning of the Deesis: the supplication offered on behalf of humanity.

The drapery of the mantle falls in long folds, rendered in parallel lines of dark and lighter tesserae, and at the hem it breaks into zigzag edges. The green dress below is modelled with highlights in yellow and gold. The type of the Virgin and the treatment of her drapery have been compared with those of the mosaics of Monreale.

Burckhardt wrote that in this mosaic the Byzantine style was combined with a certain freedom and dignity, "zumal in den Gebärden der Maria und des Johannes", above all in the gestures of Mary and John.

At the Virgin's feet small white rocks rise from the green ground, a schematic landscape that sets the figures on the earth while the gold above evokes heaven. Similar rocks appear at the feet of Saint John on the other side of the composition.

In the lower part of the dress, at the level of the knees, a patch of tesserae of a different tone interrupts the modelling of the folds. Such areas, visible across the mosaic, mark later repairs, which replaced lost tesserae without reproducing the original pattern exactly.

The figure is in good condition overall. The face, the hands, the star on the forehead and the purple of the mantle are well preserved, and the inscription beside the head remains clearly legible. The repairs in the lower part of the dress show the effect of eight centuries of exposure on the façade.

### Detail: Saint John

He wears a dark blue tunic under a pale grey and white mantle whose folds form oval loops over the thigh and zigzag hems below.   <figcaption>Solsternus, <em>Deesis</em>, 1207, detail: Saint John, with the head remade in 1927. Mosaic. Spoleto, Cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta, façade. Photo: Wolfgang Sauber, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons (cropped).</figcaption>

Saint John stands at Christ's left, labelled "SCS IOHS". In the Byzantine Deesis the second intercessor is John the Baptist, but here the figure is young and beardless and holds a closed book, the attributes of John the Evangelist. The question of his identity depends largely on the state of the head.

The head was remade in 1927, and whether it reproduces the lost original is unknown. The face that can be seen today, with its light, wavy hair swept back from the forehead and curling behind the ear, is therefore at least in part a twentieth-century work.

The difference is visible in the detail. The face is modelled with broader areas of light and shadow than the faces of Christ and the Virgin, the contours are heavier, and the head turns downwards to the saint's left, with the eyes looking away from Christ, a pose that interrupts the converging movement of the composition. These features may reflect the restorer's interpretation rather than the original design.

The rest of the figure is more reliable. The saint wears a dark blue tunic under a pale grey and white mantle, rendered in tesserae of white, grey and silver, which gives the figure a luminous quality against the gold. The folds of the mantle form oval loops over the thigh and fall in zigzag hems, a treatment of drapery derived from late Byzantine painting and found in the mosaics of Monreale.

In his left hand the saint holds a closed book with a gold cover set with jewels in blue, green and red, held against his body. His right hand is extended towards Christ in the gesture of intercession that mirrors the Virgin's. The book is the strongest argument for the identification with John the Evangelist, whose Gospel provides the words written in Christ's book.

If the figure is the Evangelist, the composition acquires a coherent Johannine theme: the words "I am the light of the world" from John's Gospel, and the Evangelist at Christ's side. The substitution of the Evangelist for the Baptist in a Deesis is unusual.

The sources do not agree on the name. The guide to the exhibition of 2023 calls the figure John the Evangelist, while some local accounts call him the Baptist and speak of the head of the Baptist as the one replaced in 1927. Older descriptions, from Burckhardt to the *Britannica* of 1911, say only "John".

At the saint's feet small white rocks rise from the green ground, as at the feet of the Virgin, and beyond the figure runs the border of the arch with its blue and pink plant motifs, well preserved in this part of the mosaic.

The figure is in fair condition. Apart from the remade head, the mantle, the tunic, the book and the hands are clear, and the pale tesserae of the garments keep their brightness. Any judgement on the original appearance of the saint has to set aside the face and rest on the body, the drapery and the attributes.

### Detail: the inscription

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Solsternus/solsternus-06.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Solsternus/solsternus-06-zoom.jpg" alt="Detail of a mosaic showing two lines of inscription beneath the figures. The inscription runs in two lines across the whole width of the mosaic, beneath the feet of the figures. It names the maker, praises his skill, gives the date and records the operarii, the lay officials responsible for the cathedral works. It is the only source for the existence of Solsternus.

The upper line is written in dark tesserae on a light grey band: "+ HEC EST PICTURA QUAM FECIT SAT PLACITURA: DOCTOR SOLSTERNVS HAC SVMMVS IN ARTE MODERNVS". The lower line is written in white tesserae on a red band: "ANNIS INVENTIS CUM SEPTEM MILLE DUCENTIS: OPERARII PALMERI·", followed by the abbreviated names of the operarii. The contrast of colour separates the verses on the work and its maker from the dating and administrative line.

The letters are Romanesque capitals mixed with uncial forms, such as the rounded E and the H, typical of monumental inscriptions around 1200. Ligatures save space in several words: the V of the endings is written in a narrow form joined to the preceding letter in SVMMVS, MODERNVS and in the name of the master.

In the name of the master, the T and the E are joined in a ligature: the crossbar of the T extends over the top of the stem it shares with the E, so that SOLSTERNVS is written in the space of nine letters rather than ten. The ligature is easy to overlook at a distance, and it explains the reading Solsernus of the older transcriptions.

The verses are divided by separators of three superimposed dots, which mark the end of each rhyming unit: after placitura, after modernus and after ducentis. The rhythm of the leonine verse, with its internal rhymes, is thus made visible in the layout of the line, where each half of the couplet occupies a measured length of the band.

The date, written in full words rather than in numerals, occupies the first half of the lower line, as the metre required. Its large white letters on the red band make it the most legible part of the inscription from the piazza, so that the year of the work was the information most clearly offered to the viewer.

The second half of the lower line gives the names of the operarii in abbreviated form, with superscript marks above several letters. The abbreviation mark above the A of D SĀSO, which stands for an omitted N, can be read clearly, and the crowding of the letters towards the right end shows that the mosaicist had to compress the names to fit the remaining space.

It is this compressed part of the line that the earliest transcription leaves out. Montfaucon copied the verses in full but stopped after the first name, "Operarii Palmerius &c.", so the names in the abbreviated second half of the line appear only in later transcriptions.

The inscription is well preserved and legible across its whole length, although some letters at the right end of the lower line are worn or damaged. The bands of colour, the dark letters above and the white letters below, keep their contrast, and the ligatures and abbreviation marks can be read in the detail.

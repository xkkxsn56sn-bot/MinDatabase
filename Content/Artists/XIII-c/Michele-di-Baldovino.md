## A Name on a Suppedaneum

Michele di Baldovino is a name read on a painted cross. No document of the thirteenth century records a painter of that name, no contract or payment survives, and nothing is known of his life. Everything rests on a few letters painted in white on the red panel below the feet of Christ, on a cross that has been in the Cleveland Museum of Art since 1995.

The cross appeared on the art market in the 1980s. It was shown for the first time in New York in 1987 and passed in a short time from a private collection to Cleveland, where it was bought with the Leonard C. Hanna Jr. Fund. It is a monumental work, 185 centimetres high and 160 wide, with twelve narrative scenes beside the body of Christ.

Restoration had brought to light the remains of an inscription on the suppedaneum, the panel at the foot of the cross. Only a few letters survive, in two separated groups, and a final syllable lower down. They were evidently the end of a painter's signature, of the kind that Giunta Pisano placed on his crosses.

In the early 1990s [Antonino Caleca](/scholars.html#caleca-antonino) and [Mariagiulia Burresi](/scholars.html#burresi-mariagiulia) read the letters «HAEL Q[...] // [...]VINI[...]IT» as «micHAEL Quondam baldoVINI me pinxIT»: "Michael, son of the late Baldovino, painted me". With this reading the painter of the cross acquired a name.

The museum is more cautious. It reads only «[GH]AEL q(uondam) / …VINI / [pinx]IT», catalogues the cross as the work of an unknown Pisan painter, and notes that the signature is illegible, the artist's first name being perhaps Michael. The name Michele di Baldovino is thus a hypothesis, not a reading of the whole text.

[Magdi A.M. Nassar](/scholars.html#nassar-magdi-a-m) has weighed the two halves of the reading separately. The first name seems to him reasonably secure, because the H rules out names such as Rafael. The father's name is another matter: the letters VINI could belong to Biduino, Ansuino or another name as easily as to Baldovino.

The surviving letters still tell something certain. The painter signed in the first person, with a formula that named his father and declared that he had made the work. A Pisan painter of the early thirteenth century thus claimed authorship of a large cross in the same way as the most famous masters of his city.

The evidence falls into two parts: the Cleveland cross, the only work on which the name appears, and the group of crosses and dossals that Burresi and Caleca gathered around it, with the objections that the group has met.

## The Cross for Saint Bona

The cross was first studied by [James H. Stubblebine](/scholars.html#stubblebine-james-h), in an article published in *Apollo* in 1987 with the title "A crucifix for Santa Bona". Stubblebine identified the figures on the terminals of the arms and recognised among them the Pisan saint Bona.

On the left terminal stand the Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist, the usual mourners of the Crucifixion. On the right terminal stand Saint James the Great and a woman in a dark habit, whom Stubblebine identified as Bona of Pisa. The pairing of James and Bona is the key to the cross's origin.

Bona was a Pisan laywoman who died in 1207. She made pilgrimages to the Holy Land, to Rome and many times to Santiago de Compostela, the shrine of Saint James, and her legend made the apostle her particular protector. A painter who placed James beside her was illustrating the devotion for which she was known.

Her cult was centred in the church of San Martino in Pisa, in the quarter south of the Arno, where she had lived and where she was buried. The museum notes that her relics were kept there by a community of Franciscan nuns. Stubblebine concluded that the cross was painted for San Martino.

The connection gives the cross a precise setting. A cross with Saint Bona must have been made for a place where her cult was active, and San Martino is the obvious candidate. It also gives a date after which the cross was painted, since Bona could only appear with a halo after her death in 1207.

The museum dates the cross to about 1230–1240. A date in these decades would make it one of the early images of the saint, painted within a generation of her death, while her memory was still alive in the parish where she had lived.

The presence of a local saint on the terminals is unusual. The terminals of Pisan crosses normally carry the mourners or angels; here a recent Pisan holy woman joins the Virgin, John and an apostle at the ends of the cross. The patrons clearly wished to see their saint in the company of the Crucifixion.

How the cross left San Martino is not known. Many Pisan crosses were removed from their churches in later centuries, when new altars and new images replaced them, and some reached the market. The Cleveland cross is one of the few to have left Italy.

## A Living Christ in Pisan Painting

The Christ of the Cleveland cross is alive. He stands upright against the cross with open eyes, his head erect, his arms stretched straight along the crossbar, his feet set side by side. This is the Christus triumphans, the Christ who has conquered death even while hanging on the cross.

The type belongs to the older Pisan tradition. The crosses of the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, made in Pisa and Lucca, showed Christ in this way, often surrounded by scenes of the Passion on the side panels of the shaft. The Cleveland cross is a late and large example of this tradition.

The new type that replaced it came from Giunta Pisano. Giunta, known from crosses signed "Iuncta Pisanus", painted the Christus patiens, the dead Christ whose body curves to one side, his head fallen on his shoulder and his eyes closed. His crosses date from the 1230s to the 1250s, and after them the triumphant type was rarely painted in Pisa.

The Cleveland cross therefore stands at a moment of transition. If the museum's dating is right, it was painted in the same years as Giunta's first crosses, by a painter who still chose the older image. Nassar describes the painter of the Cleveland cross as an earlier interpreter of the Giuntesque current, in whom its language was still incomplete.

Byzantine art lies behind both types. The body of Christ is built from Byzantine conventions: the abdomen divided into rounded segments, the ribs marked by curved lines, the long face with large dark eyes, the hair falling in separate locks over the shoulders. The museum relates its severe, hieratic character to Pisa's links with the Byzantine East.

The narrative scenes belong to the same tradition. Six scenes on each side of the body tell the Passion from the Betrayal to the Resurrection, in small crowded compositions on gold grounds. Above, the cimasa shows the Ascension, with the Virgin among the apostles and two angels.

The painter's craft is careful. The figures in the scenes are outlined firmly, their garments modelled with white highlights, their faces small and alert. The borders of red and black lozenges frame every part of the cross and tie the body of Christ, the scenes and the terminals into one design.

The cross thus shows a Pisan painter of the first half of the century working within an established form. Its interest lies less in invention than in the quality of its execution and in the survival of the inscription, which allows a name, however uncertain, to be attached to a work of this kind.

## The Catalogue of Burresi and Caleca

In the catalogue of the exhibition *Cimabue a Pisa*, held in Pisa in 2005, Burresi and Caleca gave the name Michele di Baldovino a body of work. They saw a strong resemblance between the Cleveland cross and the works given by [Edward B. Garrison](/scholars.html#garrison-edward) to the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, and proposed that the two painters were one.

Garrison had grouped under that conventional name a cross from Castelfiorentino, now in the Museo Diocesano of Volterra, and a cross from the Opera del Duomo of Pisa, now in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo. Later study added the cross of Sant'Andrea in Chinseca and the two hagiographic dossals of Saint Veranus and Saint Nicholas.

Burresi and Caleca enlarged the group further. They gave to the same painter the cross of San Pietro in Vinculis in Pisa, called San Pierino, and the cross of the church of Santi Ippolito e Cassiano at Riglione, near Pisa. Under the name of Michele di Baldovino they thus assembled a catalogue of crosses and panels covering several decades.

The proposal had obvious attractions. It joined a named painter to an anonymous group, gave the Castelfiorentino master a Pisan identity, and placed the works of the second half of the century in a single career that began with the Cleveland cross.

It also raised a difficulty. The Cleveland Christ is alive, with open eyes; the Christ of all the other crosses in the group is dead, in the manner of Giunta. A painter who began with a Christus triumphans around 1230–1240 would have had to adopt the new type later and practise it for decades.

Such a change is not impossible. Painters of the period did adapt to new types, and the triumphant Christ was abandoned in Pisa within a few years. But the identification then rests on stylistic resemblance between works of different types and different dates, and that resemblance is a matter of judgement.

The exhibition *Volterra d'Oro e di Pietra*, held at Volterra in 2006 and also edited by Burresi and Caleca, reproduced some of the works again, and the name of Michele di Baldovino entered the labels and the literature. Several panels are now published under it.

Because of the catalogue of 2005, several works are discussed both under the name of Michele and under that of the [Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Maestro-della-Croce-di-Castelfiorentino.html). The same panels belong to both discussions, and their attribution depends on which view is accepted.

## Nassar's Objections

The most detailed response to the proposal is Nassar's study of the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, published in the *Rassegna Volterrana* in 2019. It follows an earlier note on the same master by [Diego Balestri](/scholars.html#balestri-diego), published in the same journal in 2011.

Nassar judges the attempt to build a catalogue for Michele forced. In his view the Castelfiorentino master is clearly distinguishable from the painter of the Saint Bona cross, although both belong to the same Giuntesque current. The Castelfiorentino master was a contemporary of the Cleveland painter or a slightly younger follower.

He rejects outright two of the additions. The crosses of San Pierino and of Riglione seem to him irreconcilable with the works already given to the Castelfiorentino master, and he does not discuss them further. He accepts, on the other hand, the cross of Sant'Andrea in Chinseca, which he considers clearly by the same hand as the cross of Castelfiorentino.

For the two dossals, Nassar proposes a different context. He compares the standing bishops of the Saint Veranus and the Saint Nicholas with the bishop on the silver grossi struck by the mint of Volterra in the 1290s, and suggests that the Castelfiorentino master led a workshop in Volterra at the end of the century.

His conclusion separates the two names. The Castelfiorentino master, in his reading, responded to the same Giuntesque models as Michele di Baldovino, but later and more fully, while the Cleveland painter represents an earlier and still incomplete stage of the same current.

Nassar also reopens the inscription. Accepting the first name, he notes that the patronymic is uncertain, so that even the name Michele di Baldovino may be only partly right. The identification that seemed to rest on a signature thus rests, in part, on a reconstruction.

The question is not settled. Burresi and Caleca knew the works well and their catalogue is published, while Nassar's objections are argued from the same works and from their geography. The difference between the living Christ of Cleveland and the dead Christ of the other crosses is the point on which the debate turns.

The prudent course keeps the name for the painter of the Cleveland cross and treats the other works as attributions made under that name, each with its own history and the opinions recorded for it, so that the group can be judged as a whole.

## Catalogue Entry

### The Cross of Saint Bona, Cleveland: History

The cross is in the Cleveland Museum of Art, accession number 1995.5. It is painted in tempera with gold on panel and measures 185 by 160 centimetres without its frame. The museum catalogues it as the work of an unknown Pisan painter and dates it to about 1230–1240.

It was shown for the first time in New York in 1987 and was studied in the same year by Stubblebine in *Apollo*. It passed through the Viennese dealer Galerie Sanct Lucas and a private collection before it was bought by the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1995, with the Leonard C. Hanna Jr. Fund.

The cross is almost complete. It keeps its shaft with the body of Christ, the twelve narrative scenes of the side panels, the two terminals with their pairs of figures, the cimasa with the Ascension and the suppedaneum with the inscription. Only the upper part of the shaft above the head, which is dark and empty, has lost whatever it carried.

The identification of Saint Bona on the right terminal, with Saint James the Great, connects the cross with the church of San Martino in Pisa. The museum, following Stubblebine, notes that her relics were kept there by Franciscan nuns, and that the cross may have been made for that church.

The inscription on the suppedaneum is the basis of the name Michele di Baldovino. Its few surviving letters were read by Caleca and Burresi in the early 1990s; the museum's own reading is more restricted, and it records only that the painter's first name was perhaps Michael.

The cross is the only work on which the name appears. All the other works described in this catalogue are attributions made by comparison with it, and their relation to it is the subject of the debate between Burresi and Caleca and Nassar.

The cross has the full Pisan form of the early thirteenth century. A broad shaft carries the body of Christ, flanked by side panels with narrative scenes; the crossbar ends in two square terminals; a rectangular cimasa crowns the top, and a red suppedaneum projects below the feet. Every part is framed by borders of small red and black lozenges.

Christ is alive. He stands upright against the dark blue of the cross, his head erect and his large eyes open, looking out at the viewer. His halo is gold, crossed by bands of ornament, and its edge is outlined in brown against the dark shaft above.

His arms are stretched almost horizontally along the crossbar, with the hands open and the wounds marked by small red spots from which thin threads of blood run. The arms are long and stiff, divided into segments by curved lines at the elbows, and the hands are large.

The body is pale yellow, modelled with greenish shadows. The chest is marked by curved lines for the ribs, and the abdomen is divided into three rounded segments by a vertical line, a Byzantine convention that the painter renders with great regularity.

The loincloth is dark blue and falls to the knees, its hem cut into points. It is held at the waist by an orange belt tied in a large knot, whose end falls in front. The legs are straight and the feet are set side by side on the suppedaneum, each with its own nail.

On either side of the body, six scenes in three rows tell the Passion. On the left are the Betrayal, the Flagellation and the preparation for the Crucifixion; on the right the Deposition, the Lamentation and the holy women at the tomb. Each scene is crowded with small figures on a gold ground.

The terminals carry four standing figures: the Virgin and Saint John at the left, Saint James and Saint Bona at the right. The cimasa shows the Ascension, with the Virgin standing at the centre between two angels and the apostles in two groups.

The cross is thus a complete programme. The living Christ at the centre is surrounded by the story of his death and resurrection, by the mourners and saints who witness it, and by the Ascension above, and the signature at his feet names the painter who made it.

The cimasa is a long rectangular panel at the top of the cross, framed by the same border of red and black lozenges as the rest. On its gold ground the painter set out the Ascension as a single frieze of figures standing on a common line.

At the centre the Virgin stands frontally, in a dark blue mantle that covers her head and falls to her feet over a red dress. Her hands are crossed on her breast, and her face is still and grave. She is the fixed point of the composition.

At either side an angel turns towards the groups of apostles. The angel at the left, in pink and yellow, raises a hand towards the sky; the angel at the right, in a dark blue mantle, does the same, so that both direct the eyes of the witnesses upward.

The apostles stand in two crowded groups, six at each side. They throw back their heads and raise their hands, some shading their eyes, as if looking at a light above them. Their mantles, in green, pink, yellow and blue, are modelled with white highlights and dark folds.

Christ himself does not appear in the panel. The angels' gestures point to the place where he has gone, and the scene is read as the moment after his departure, with the Virgin among the apostles, as in the account of the Acts of the Apostles.

The detail shows the whole crossbar, with the arms of Christ stretched along the dark blue ground between bands of lozenges. The arms are almost horizontal; they do not sag under the weight of the body as in the later crosses of the Giuntesque type.

On the left terminal stand the Virgin and Saint John. The Virgin wears a dark red mantle over her head and gestures towards the body of her son; John, young and beardless, in a pink mantle over a blue tunic, turns towards her and points to Christ with one hand.

On the right terminal stand Saint James the Great and Saint Bona. James, bearded, in a dark blue mantle over a pink tunic, turns towards the woman beside him and extends his hand towards her. Bona wears a black habit and a white veil, and stands quietly with her hands joined.

The gesture of James towards Bona echoes her legend, in which the apostle appeared to her and protected her on her pilgrimages to his shrine at Compostela. The two figures form a pair as the Virgin and John do, so that the local saint is placed on the same level as the witnesses of the Crucifixion.

The figures are small and tall, standing on a gold ground without setting. Their drapery falls in long folds, outlined in dark lines and highlighted in white, with the clarity of a miniature, and they have the same proportions as the figures of the narrative scenes below.

The head of Christ is held upright, slightly tilted, and looks straight out of the panel. The eyes are large and dark, with heavy lids and high arched brows, and they give the face the watchful, solemn expression of the triumphant Christ.

The face is long and narrow. The nose is straight, with a white highlight along its ridge; the mouth is small and closed, the cheeks hollowed with green shadows. A short dark beard frames the chin and divides into two curled points.

The hair is painted in fine parallel wavy lines of brown. It falls from a central parting over the ears and onto the shoulders in separate locks, two on each side, which curl at their ends against the pale skin of the chest.

The halo is gold, crossed by the arms of a cross filled with black scrolling ornament on gold. Its edge is outlined by a brown rim that stands out against the dark shaft of the cross above, so that the head is set within a ring of light.

The chest below is modelled with curved lines for the collarbones and the ribs, and the neck is marked by a single curved fold. The painting is linear rather than plastic: the forms are described by lines and by gradations of colour, in the Byzantine manner that the museum associates with Pisa's links to the East.

#### The Betrayal

The first scene, at the upper left, shows the arrest of Christ in the garden. The painter has filled almost the whole field with a crowd, so that the gold ground appears only at the top, where torches and lances rise above the heads like a thicket.

At the centre Judas, in a pink tunic and a yellow mantle, throws his arms around Christ and presses his face to his in the kiss of betrayal. Christ, wrapped in a dark blue mantle, stands still and lets himself be embraced, his head inclined towards his disciple.

Around them the soldiers press in from every side. Their heads are painted in rows, one behind the other, with the same rounded caps of brown hair, so that the crowd seems to stretch far back into the darkness of the night.

The torches are rendered as long staves crowned with red and orange flames, and among them rise the points of lances. They recall that the arrest took place at night, and their flickering colour breaks the regular pattern of the heads.

At the left a man in a blue tunic and a red cloak strides into the scene, while at the right an old bearded disciple in an orange mantle, probably Peter, turns towards the group. The scene opens the Passion with the moment in which Christ is handed over by one of his own.

#### The Flagellation

Below the Betrayal, Christ is scourged. He stands at the centre, bound to a tall slender column with a capital, naked except for a short white cloth around his loins, his body pale against the gold ground.

Two men flank him and raise their arms to strike. The one at the left wears a yellow tunic, the one at the right a pink tunic with a blue band in his hair; their bodies twist with the effort of the blow, while Christ remains upright and still.

At the left a group of men watches the punishment. The foremost wears a long red mantle and raises his hand, and behind him figures with white headcloths crowd together, the elders and priests who have handed Christ over.

At the upper right a small building with a tower opens on a seated figure in red. It is Pilate in his palace, who ordered the scourging, watching from above as the sentence is carried out.

The column, rising through the whole height of the scene, divides it into two halves and fixes Christ at its centre. The painter has reduced the episode to its essentials, the bound victim, the raised arms and the witnesses, in a few clear shapes.

#### Christ Led to the Crucifixion

The third scene on the left shows Christ brought to the place of execution. In the upper left corner the cross floats diagonally against the gold, a dark shape that announces what is about to happen.

At the centre Christ, in a dark blue robe and with a cross-nimbus, is seized by the arm and drawn forward. His head is bowed, and his face, turned towards the women at the left, is calm and sad.

Around him stand men in rich garments. One, in a long yellow robe whose hem is set with jewels and pearls, faces him; others in patterned mantles and soldiers in caps crowd behind. At the right a man in a red mantle holds a hammer, ready to drive the nails.

At the left, before a small building, the Virgin and the holy women stand wrapped in their mantles, one of them in red. They watch from a little distance, the first appearance of the mourners who will reappear in the scenes on the right.

The scene is the last on the left side of the cross and leads directly to the body of Christ painted beside it. The hammer and the floating cross prepare the eye for the Crucifixion, which is not narrated as a scene because it is the image of the whole cross.

#### The Deposition

At the upper right the narrative resumes after the death of Christ. The cross stands in the centre of the scene, its dark wood clearly outlined against the gold, and the body is being taken down.

Joseph of Arimathea, in a red tunic, holds the body of Christ in his arms as it comes away from the cross. Christ's head falls against his shoulder, and his pale body, wrapped in a blue cloth at the loins, hangs limp and heavy.

The Virgin, in a dark blue mantle, has reached her son and presses his hand to her face. Behind her stand two holy women, one in red and one with a white veil, who lean forward and raise their hands in grief.

At the right a man in a dark tunic reaches up towards the cross, drawing out the nails, while Saint John stands at the edge of the scene in a pink mantle, his hand raised to his cheek in the gesture of mourning.

The ground beneath is a rocky slope painted in yellow, red and blue. The painter has arranged the figures in a dense, symmetrical group around the cross, so that the movement of the descending body is the centre of attention.

#### The Lamentation

In the middle scene on the right the body of Christ lies on a white shroud before a landscape of rocks. A great yellow hill rises at the left and a reddish rock at the right, framing the group like the walls of a cave.

The Virgin, in a dark blue mantle, bends over the head of her son and holds it in her arms, her face close to his. Christ's eyes are closed, and his body, stretched horizontally across the scene, contrasts with the agitated figures around it.

At his feet a bearded man in a green mantle kneels and bends to kiss them, and other disciples crowd behind the body, leaning over it with grieving faces. One of them, in pink, bends low over the shroud.

Above them, against the hill, three holy women stand together. Two are wrapped in dark and green mantles; the third, in red, throws both arms up above her head in a cry of grief, a gesture that the Byzantine tradition gave to Mary Magdalen.

The scene combines the lamentation over the body with the preparation for burial. It is the most emotional of the six, and the painter conveys the grief through gestures rather than faces, with the raised arms of the woman as its sharpest note.

#### The Holy Women at the Tomb

The last scene, at the lower right, shows the morning of the Resurrection. At the left the holy women approach from a town, with a pink building and a tower behind them; they wear long mantles, in red and dark blue, and carry jars of ointment for the body.

Before them an angel sits on the stone of the tomb. He has large wings spread behind him, wears a pale green robe, holds a staff and points with his other hand to the empty tomb, announcing that Christ is risen.

The stone on which he sits is painted as a slanting slab with a gold pattern, pushed away from the entrance. Behind it opens a dark cave in the rock, and in a small niche the white burial cloths can be seen, left behind in the empty tomb.

In the lower right corner the soldiers who guarded the tomb sleep, huddled together in their helmets, their spears leaning against their shoulders. Their heavy sleep contrasts with the angel's wakeful gesture and the women's arrival.

The scene closes the narrative of the cross with the Resurrection, the victory over death that the living Christ at the centre already expresses. The cimasa above, with the Ascension, continues the story beyond the tomb to Christ's return to heaven.

The feet of Christ rest side by side on the dark blue ground of the shaft, turned slightly outward. Each is pierced by its own nail, from which two thin threads of blood run down over the toes. The four nails of the triumphant Christ, two for the hands and two for the feet, are an older convention than the three nails of later crosses.

Below the feet the shaft ends in a projecting panel painted red, framed in gold. On it the inscription is written in white Gothic capitals, but most of the letters are lost, and what survives is scattered in separate groups on the red ground.

At the top a line of letters can be read in part: the museum reads it as «[GH]AEL q(uondam)», the end of a first name followed by the abbreviation for "the late". Lower down, at the right, the letters «…VINI» end the father's name, and beneath them the syllable «IT» closes the formula, completing a verb such as «pinxit».

The reading «micHAEL Quondam baldoVINI me pinxIT» proposed by Caleca and Burresi fills these gaps. The letters that survive support the general form of a signature, "[Mic]hael son of the late [...]vinus painted [me]", but the father's name and part of the first name are restored.

The signature in the first person, placed at the feet of Christ, follows a Pisan custom known from Giunta's crosses. Even in its damaged state, it records the painter's wish to be named and remembered at the foot of the image he had made.

### The Cross of San Pierino, Pisa: History

The cross belongs to the church of San Pietro in Vinculis in Pisa, known as San Pierino, a Romanesque church near the Arno consecrated in the early twelfth century. Its dedication to Saint Peter in chains explains the small scenes of Peter's story painted at the foot of the shaft.

Burresi and Caleca gave the cross to Michele di Baldovino in the catalogue of *Cimabue a Pisa* in 2005. It is one of the two additions that most enlarged the painter's range, since it shows the Christus patiens of the Giuntesque type with an elaborate cimasa and an inscription on the crossbar.

Nassar rejects the attribution. In his study of the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino he states that the crosses of San Pierino and of Riglione seem to him irreconcilable with the works already given to that master, and he does not include them in his catalogue.

The cross is one of the larger and more complete Pisan crosses of the second half of the thirteenth century. It keeps its cimasa with two tiers of images, its terminals, its suppedaneum and the small scenes at the foot, and the frame is set with round gilded bosses.

The inscription on the crossbar, «+ MORTIS DESTRUCTOR» at the left and «VITE REPARATOR ET AUCTOR» at the right, calls Christ "the destroyer of death, the restorer and author of life". It states in words what the image of the dead Christ shows: that his death is the victory over death.

The dead Christ places the cross after Giunta Pisano's crosses of the 1240s and 1250s, which established the type in Pisa. Its relation to the living Christ of Cleveland is the point at which the attribution to a single painter is most open to doubt.

The cross is broad and complex. Its shaft is wide, without the narrative side panels of the Cleveland cross; the crossbar is long, and the cimasa rises in two tiers above the head of Christ. Bands of ornament in red and blue frame every part, and round gilded bosses are set along the edges.

Christ hangs dead. His head falls on his right shoulder, his eyes are closed and his face is long and sorrowful, framed by dark hair that falls in locks onto the shoulders. The halo is raised in relief and gilded, with a cross marked on it.

His arms, stretched along the dark crossbar, bend slightly under the weight of the body, and blood falls from the hands in thin threads that end in red drops. The body curves to the left, the chest modelled with curved lines, the abdomen divided into rounded segments that swell above the loincloth.

The loincloth is pink and orange, falling in broad folds to the knees, and is held by a dark belt tied in a heavy knot. The legs are long and close together, and the feet rest side by side on a slanting red suppedaneum, each with its own nail and its stream of blood.

On the crossbar the inscription runs in two parts above the arms: «+ MORTIS DESTRUCTOR» at the left and «VITE REPARATOR ET AUCTOR» at the right. At the ends stand the mourners, the Virgin at the left wrapped in a dark mantle and Saint John at the right, holding a book and resting his cheek on his hand.

The cimasa is the richest part. At the top Christ sits enthroned within a roundel, blessing and holding a book, between two angels in flight. Below, a rectangular panel shows the Virgin standing with raised hands among the apostles, the Ascension, and at each side a roundel holds a half-length angel.

At the foot of the shaft, on either side of the suppedaneum, are two small scenes. At the left Saint Peter sits beside a building on which a cock is perched, the sign of his denial; at the right a figure in red stands before a building and raises a hand, probably another episode of Peter's story, appropriate to the church's dedication.

The cross unites the dead Christ of Giunta with an older taste for many images. The heavenly Christ of the cimasa, the Ascension, the inscription and the scenes of Peter turn the cross into a summary of the faith of the church for which it was made.

### The Cross of Sant'Andrea in Chinseca: History

The cross comes from the church of Sant'Andrea in Chinseca, in the quarter of Pisa south of the Arno usually called Kinzica, and is now in the nearby church of San Martino. San Martino is the church associated with the cult of Saint Bona, for which the Cleveland cross is thought to have been made.

Burresi and Caleca included the cross in their catalogue of Michele di Baldovino, and it is published under that name. It was reproduced in the catalogue of the exhibition *Volterra d'Oro e di Pietra* at Volterra in 2006.

Nassar accepts the cross as fully convincing, but not for Michele. In his view it is clearly by the same hand as the cross of Castelfiorentino, and he dates it to 1270–1280, in the same years as that work.

The cross has lost its terminals and its cimasa. It keeps the shaft with the body of Christ, a gold ground cracked all over, and the two mourners painted on the side panels beside the body.

The feet are nailed side by side, each with its own nail. This detail distinguishes the cross from the crosses of Castelfiorentino and of the Opera del Duomo, where the feet are crossed, and brings it closer to the Cleveland cross, although the Christ is dead.

The cross is thus the work that most clearly joins the two groups. It is the strongest point of the identification proposed by Burresi and Caleca, and also, for Nassar, one of the clearest works of the Castelfiorentino master.

The cross survives as a shaft and crossbar of gold, cracked all over into a fine network of lines. The ends of the arms and the top of the shaft are cut, so that the figure of Christ fills almost the whole surviving panel.

Christ hangs dead, his head inclined to his right shoulder and his eyes closed. His face is long, with a straight nose and a short beard, and dark locks of hair fall onto his shoulders. The halo is gold, decorated with an ornamented cross whose arms are filled with scrolling patterns.

The arms are stretched almost straight along a dark crossbar edged with bands of ornament. Thin threads of blood run from the wounds of the hands, and drops fall from them onto the dark ground.

The body is long and slender, with the chest and belly marked by curving segments. The belly swells above the loincloth, which is red, knotted at the waist and falling in broad folds over the thighs; its hem is edged with dark lines.

The legs are straight and close together. The feet are nailed side by side on a dark red suppedaneum, each with its own nail, a feature that the cross shares with the Cleveland cross and with the cross of San Pierino.

On the panel at the left the Virgin stands in a dark mantle; much of her figure is lost, and only her head and part of the mantle can be read. On the right Saint John, in a pink mantle, rests his cheek on his hand, his head bent in grief.

The mourners are small beside the body of Christ, pressed against the edges of the panels, and the figure of the crucified dominates the cross. The gold ground, now worn, once surrounded the body with light.

The cross shows a Giuntesque Christ painted with restraint. The modelling of the body is soft, the lines of the anatomy less emphatic than on other crosses of the group, and the red loincloth gives the image its strongest note of colour.

### The Cross with Two Archangels, Museo Nazionale di San Matteo: History

The cross is in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo in Pisa, inventory 5721, and comes from the Opera del Duomo. It is painted in egg tempera with silver leaf on panel and measures 152 by 140.5 centimetres.

Garrison gave it to the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, together with the cross from Castelfiorentino, and these two crosses formed the original core of that master's catalogue. The two share, among other features, a halo of Christ set with bosses in relief.

The cross is published under the name of Michele di Baldovino as part of the catalogue assembled by Burresi and Caleca. Nassar keeps it with the Castelfiorentino master, following Garrison.

The national catalogue dates the cross to about 1240–1260. A date in the middle of the century would place it among the earliest Pisan crosses to follow the model of Giunta.

The use of silver leaf instead of gold for the ground is unusual. Silver tarnishes and darkens with time, and the ground now has a warm brownish tone that differs from the bright gold of the other crosses.

The cross keeps its titulus, its terminals with two archangels and the side panels with the mourners. It is one of the better preserved crosses of the group and shows how the cut-down cross of Castelfiorentino must originally have looked.

### The Cross of Riglione: History

The cross belongs to the church of Santi Ippolito e Cassiano at Riglione, a village in the plain east of Pisa. It is one of the works given to Michele di Baldovino by Burresi and Caleca in 2005.

Nassar rejects the attribution together with that of the San Pierino cross. Both seem to him irreconcilable with the works of the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, and he excludes them from his catalogue.

The cross differs from the others in the group in the pose of Christ. The body curves strongly, the knees bend, and the feet are crossed and fixed with a single nail, so that the whole figure describes a deep S-curve against the shaft.

The gold ground is decorated with an incised lattice pattern, and the cimasa carries two half-length angels in flight. The terminals of the arms are badly damaged and their figures can hardly be read.

The pronounced curve of the body and the transparent loincloth are features of the Pisan crosses of the later decades of the century. They set the Riglione Christ apart from the more upright figures of the other crosses gathered under Michele's name.

The cross remains in a parish church in the Pisan countryside, as many crosses of the period once did. Its presence there shows how far the production of painted crosses extended beyond the city.

### Saint Nicholas and Four Scenes from His Life, Peccioli: History

The dossal of Saint Nicholas is at Peccioli, in the Valdera, in the Museo d'Arte Sacra of the Prepositura di San Verano. Its original provenance is not known.

It came to Peccioli through a mistake. In the nineteenth century a member of the Toscanelli family, wishing to bring back to the town the image of its patron Saint Veranus, bought this panel on the market instead, deceived by its close likeness to the dossal of Saint Veranus. The people of Peccioli long venerated it as an image of their patron.

The dossal is published under the name of Michele di Baldovino, as one of the works grouped around the Cleveland cross by Burresi and Caleca. Nassar gives it to the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, by the same hand as the Saint Veranus now in Brera.

Nassar places both dossals in the territory of Pisa or Volterra and dates them to the last years of the thirteenth century. He considers the Saint Nicholas particularly close to the standing bishop on the silver grossi struck at Volterra in the 1290s.

The panel has lost its frame and is cut at the top, so that its original shape, probably with a pointed gable like the Saint Veranus, is uncertain. Four scenes survive, two at each side of the saint.

The cult of Nicholas, bishop of Myra, was widespread in Pisa and its territory, among merchants and sailors who honoured him as a protector. The dossal adapts to him the form of the hagiographic panel that the same painter used for the patron of Peccioli.

Saint Nicholas stands frontally at the centre, filling the height of the panel. He wears a white mitre, a red chasuble patterned with roundels over a blue alb, and a white pallium marked with black crosses. His right hand is raised in blessing, and his left holds a crozier.

His face is that of an old man, with a short grey beard curled at the chin and large eyes under arched brows. The halo is gold, and the gold ground around him is plain, so that the figure stands out as an icon.

The four scenes are set two at each side, each framed by a red border and filled with buildings in pink, blue and yellow. They tell the birth of the saint and three of his miracles, read from top to bottom.

At the upper left a woman lies in bed after childbirth, and the newborn Nicholas stands upright in his basin, as the legend tells, the first sign of his holiness. At the upper right a woman embraces a boy before an altar while men in white watch: the return of Adeodatus to his parents.

At the lower left the same story is told from its beginning. Adeodatus, a slave at the table of a pagan king, holds a cup, and Nicholas, flying in from above, seizes him by the hair to carry him home, before the crowned king and queen.

At the lower right a crowned man sleeps in a red bed, and a bishop appears at a doorway. This is the dream of the emperor Constantine, to whom Nicholas appeared at night to save three innocent officers condemned to death.

At the bottom right a small figure kneels, perhaps a donor who commissioned the panel. The figure is tiny beside the saint, according to the hierarchy of scale usual in the painting of the period.

The bishop's pose, the pallium with black crosses and the crozier are those of the Saint Veranus in Brera. The likeness is close enough to explain why the panel was mistaken for the image of the patron of Peccioli.

### Saint Veranus and Six Scenes from His Legend, Brera: History

The dossal of Saint Veranus is in the Pinacoteca di Brera in Milan, Reg. Cron. 5452. It is painted in tempera on panel and measures 157 by 97 centimetres according to the museum (152 by 97 in the national catalogue). The museum catalogues its painter as the Maestro di San Verano.

The panel very probably comes from the pieve of San Verano at Peccioli, in the Valdera, where it would have stood on the high altar. Veranus, bishop of Cavaillon in Provence in the sixth century, is the patron of Peccioli, which claimed to hold his relics.

The provenance recorded in the national catalogue confirms the link with the Toscanelli. Before 1883 the panel belonged to the Toscanelli in Florence; it then passed to Mario Viezzoli in Genoa and, in 1943, to Count Paolo Gerli of Villa Gaeta in Milan, whose heirs gave it to the Brera in 1982.

Its attribution has varied. [George Kaftal](/scholars.html#kaftal-george) gave it in 1952 to the Florentine school and [Enzo Carli](/scholars.html#carli-enzo) in 1958 to Meliore. Garrison placed it with the Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino, and Nassar confirms that it is by the same hand as the Saint Nicholas of Peccioli.

Since the Saint Nicholas is published under the name of Michele di Baldovino, the Saint Veranus is drawn into the same debate. Nassar dates both panels to the last years of the century, which would separate them by several decades from the Cleveland cross.

One scene, the beheading of a bishop, does not belong to the legend of Veranus of Cavaillon, who died peacefully. Nassar suggests that the painter, working from scarce local accounts, confused the bishop of Cavaillon with another saint of the same name, without weakening the connection with Peccioli.

### Saint Veranus and Six Scenes from His Legend

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Michele-di-Baldovino/michele-di-baldovino-12.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Michele-di-Baldovino/michele-di-baldovino-12-zoom.jpg" alt="A tall panel with a pointed gable, in a dark frame painted with small ornaments and set with round gilded bosses.   <figcaption>Maestro di San Verano (Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino), Saint Veranus between two angels and six scenes from his legend, last decades of the thirteenth century. </figure>

The panel has the shape of a house with a pointed gable, the common form of the Tuscan dossal in the thirteenth century. Its dark frame, painted with small ornaments and set with round gilded bosses, encloses a gold ground on which the saint stands at full length.

Veranus stands frontally, filling the height of the panel. He wears a white mitre set with stones, a dark brown chasuble falling in long folds over a blue-green dalmatic, and a white pallium marked with black crosses. His red shoes rest on a patterned step.

His right hand is raised in blessing before his breast, and his left holds a slender crozier whose head curls into a scroll. Beside his head, in red letters on the gold, fragments of his name can still be read.

At either side of his head a half-length angel turns towards him, wings spread, one hand open. The angels wear robes set with jewels, like Byzantine court dignitaries, and they frame the bishop as a figure of heavenly rank.

Below the angels, three scenes are set on each side, each within a red frame. They are crowded with towers and buildings in pink, green and yellow, and in several of them a dark blue arc of sky hangs above the action, from which angels appear or cords descend.

On the left, from top to bottom, are a baptism in a red font, the raising of a dead boy and a beheading in a rocky landscape. On the right are an exorcism, the fall of a statue at the saint's prayer and the death of the saint, attended by an angel.

The scenes are painted with small figures and lively gestures, close in type to those of the Saint Nicholas. The buildings are drawn as boxes with sloping roofs and narrow windows, and the colours are bright and varied.

The panel is the best known of the works associated with the group. Its monumental bishop, its jewelled angels and its narrative scenes show the painter as a narrator as well as a painter of crosses, working for a local cult in the Valdera.

The angel appears at half length in the upper left corner of the panel, beside the painted frame with its blue and red ornament and a gilded boss. The gold ground around him is cracked and worn.

His face is oval and pale, with large eyes turned sideways, a long nose and a small mouth, and an expression of gentle melancholy. His auburn hair is bound with a red fillet, and his halo is decorated with a fine punched pattern.

His wings are large and brown, their long lower feathers painted in pink and red with white tips. One wing rises above his head; the other falls at his side, so that the figure is framed by the two curves.

He wears a brown robe with a broad collar and a central band set with dark oval stones, red lozenges and rows of white pearls, the costume of the Byzantine court. His left hand holds a small dark object at his waist; his right is open towards the saint.

At the right of the angel a red letter S is painted on the gold, the beginning of the inscription with the saint's name. The angel's gesture of presentation directs the viewer from the corner of the panel towards the bishop at the centre.

The detail shows the head and upper body of the saint. His mitre is white, divided by a jewelled band set with red and blue stones, and its two points rise above his forehead against the halo.

His face is long and severe. The eyes are brown and staring under heavy brows, the nose long and shaded in red, the cheeks lined. His grey beard is painted in short curls and turns at the chin into a tight spiral, a mannerism that recurs in the bishop of the Saint Nicholas.

The halo is incised in the gold with scrolling foliage, a finer ornament than the plain haloes of the crosses. Beside the head, in red letters, the inscription with the saint's name can be read in fragments, «…RANU…» at the right.

His right hand is raised in blessing before the white pallium, with long, flat fingers. His left grasps a slender green crozier, whose white head curls into a roundel enclosing a small creature with a curved neck.

The pallium with its black crosses, the dark chasuble and the frontal pose give the saint the authority of an icon. Nassar compares this figure with the bishop on the Volterran grossi of the 1290s, standing in the same way with the same attributes.

### Saint Veranus: Baptism and Ordination

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Michele-di-Baldovino/michele-di-baldovino-15.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Michele-di-Baldovino/michele-di-baldovino-15-zoom.jpg" alt="Detail of a panel: a scene in a red frame on a gold ground.   <figcaption>Maestro di San Verano (Maestro della Croce di Castelfiorentino), the baptism and ordination of the saint, detail of the dossal of Saint Veranus. Milan, Pinacoteca di Brera. Photo: Francesco Bini, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/">CC BY-SA 4.0</a>, via <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/?curid=153345792">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

The first scene, at the upper left of the panel, joins two episodes in one frame. At the left an old bearded man with a halo, standing on a rocky ledge, lays his hand on the head of a young man who sits naked in a red font: the baptism of Veranus.

Above the font an angel flies down head first from a dark blue arc of sky, holding a small crown towards the youth. The crown marks the baptised as one chosen for holiness from the beginning of his Christian life.

At the right the second episode takes place before a group of buildings. An old haloed man, in a brown mantle over a blue tunic, holds out an open book to a young man with a halo, dressed in a white robe with a jewelled collar, who bows his head and holds a dark garment.

The gesture of giving the book belongs to the rite of ordination, in which the cleric received the instruments of his office. The youth behind, without a halo, is a companion or an assistant at the ceremony.

The painter places the two episodes side by side without any division, so that the youth appears twice in the same space. The red font, painted as a solid box with a panelled front, is one of the boldest colour accents of the panel.

The scene takes place in a square between towers. At the left the bishop, in a mitre and a grey chasuble with the white pallium, raises his right hand in blessing and with his left takes the hand of a boy, accompanied by a cleric who stands behind him.

The boy appears twice. Below, he lies stretched on the ground, his eyes closed, dead; above, he stands alive in a red tunic and black hose, his hand on his breast, looking up at the bishop who has raised him.

At the right three men watch. The first, in a jewelled cap and a long pink mantle over a grey tunic and armoured legs, extends his hand in wonder; beside him stand a youth and an old man with a striped headscarf, probably the boy's family.

The buildings are painted in pink, grey and yellow, with narrow arched windows and pointed roofs, and a long wall closes the background. The gold sky above is empty, so that the action is concentrated in the lower half of the scene.

The double image of the boy, dead and alive in the same frame, is a narrative device common in the painting of the period. It allows the viewer to see at once the miracle and its result, as the onlookers do.

The scene is set before a pale green rocky hill, its slopes dotted with small trees, between towers in pink and yellow. At the left a group of soldiers stands in mail hoods and red caps, holding spears and a red shield.

In front of them a young executioner, in a pink tunic gathered by a grey sash and grey hose, raises a long sword above his head with both hands. His body leans back to give force to the blow, and the blade cuts diagonally across the scene.

At the right a bishop kneels with his head bowed, his mitre slipping, in a grey chasuble with the pallium. He bends forward close to a pale naked figure whose form the damaged surface makes hard to read.

The scene shows the martyrdom of a bishop. As Nassar notes, it has no place in the legend of Veranus of Cavaillon, who died in peace, and the painter may have confused him with another saint of the same name.

The composition is driven by the diagonal of the sword and the curve of the hill, which bends over the kneeling bishop like a canopy. The scene is the most violent of the six and stands at the lower left, opposite the peaceful death of the saint.

The second angel mirrors the first. He appears at half length in the upper right corner, between the head of the saint's crozier and the painted frame with its gilded boss.

His face is turned towards the saint, with the head tilted and the eyes raised, and a red fillet binds his auburn hair. His halo, like that of his companion, is decorated with a fine punched pattern.

His wings are brown, the lower feathers painted pink and red with white tips. His robe repeats the jewelled collar and band of the other angel, with dark oval stones, red lozenges and rows of pearls.

His left hand is open towards the saint, in the same gesture of presentation, while the right holds a small object at his waist. Above his hand, in red letters, the end of the saint's name, «…RANU…», can be read, next to the white roundel of the crozier.

The two angels, almost identical, give the panel its symmetry. Their courtly costume and their gestures turn the image of the bishop into a scene of heavenly presentation, as if Veranus were introduced to the viewer by the angels.

The scene, at the upper right of the panel, shows two bishops. At the left one of them looks up with his hands raised in prayer, before a tall ochre rock at whose top an angel reaches down from an arc of blue sky.

At the centre the second bishop, in a mitre and a grey chasuble with the pallium, raises his right hand in blessing towards a woman possessed. She stands in a pale green dress, her long red hair loose, her face distorted, her hands bound by a chain.

From her head a small black demon with spread wings flies out towards the tower behind her. Its thin limbs and claws are painted with sharp strokes, and its dark shape stands out against the green wall.

At the right her companions hold the end of the chain and press together to watch: women and an old man, in pink and brown, their faces full of fear and wonder. Behind them rise a tower and a red domed building.

The two bishops may represent the same saint twice, first praying for help and then driving out the demon, or Veranus with another bishop. The scene is the clearest image of the saint's power as a healer, the reason for which his help was sought.

At the left the bishop kneels and raises both hands, his head thrown back, towards a pale figure that stands on a carved bracket at the centre of the scene. Above it a dark blue arc of sky hangs from the gold, with thin cords descending.

The figure on the bracket is painted in pale tones, almost transparent against the gold, and fragments seem to fly from it. It appears to be a statue, perhaps an idol, which breaks or falls at the prayer of the saint.

Below, beside a lectern with an open scroll, a man in a red cap and a blue tunic falls backwards, his hands raised, while a second bearded head appears beneath him. They are struck by the fall or overwhelmed by the miracle.

At the right a woman in a white dress and a pink mantle kneels with her hands raised, her eyes fixed on the event above. Her calm attitude contrasts with the confusion of the fallen men.

The surface of the scene is worn, and its exact subject is uncertain. The kneeling bishop, the shattered figure and the startled witnesses show a miracle of power over an image, in a building painted with a green wall and arched windows.

</figure>

The last scene, at the lower right of the panel, shows the death of Veranus. The bishop lies stretched on the ground in his grey chasuble and white pallium, his mitre on his head, his hands crossed on his breast and his eyes closed.

Above him at the right stands an angel with a halo and large brown wings, in a red mantle over a blue robe with a jewelled band. He raises one finger towards heaven and holds a long staff ending in a lily, as if announcing the saint's entry into glory.

At the left a youth in a pink striped mantle and grey hose looks towards the angel with an open hand, and behind him a veiled woman watches. They are the witnesses of the saint's peaceful death.

Above the scene a dark blue arc of sky hangs from the gold with cords descending to the ground, a canopy that marks the presence of heaven. Behind, a long yellow building with arched windows and red roofs closes the space.

This scene was almost entirely lost before the panel's restoration. Its calm composition, with the horizontal body of the saint, answers the violence of the beheading at the lower left, and closes the cycle with the passing of the bishop into heaven.

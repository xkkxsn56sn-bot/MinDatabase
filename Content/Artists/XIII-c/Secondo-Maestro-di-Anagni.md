## Identity and Historical Context

The artist conventionally designated as the *Secondo Maestro di Anagni*, known in earlier scholarship as the *Maestro Ornatista*, a denomination introduced by Pietro Toesca in his foundational 1902 study, belongs to the distinguished yet anonymous tradition of medieval Italian wall painters whose identities have been irretrievably lost to history, surviving only through the evidence encoded in the material fabric of their works. The appellation "Secondo Maestro," which eventually superseded the older Toesca terminology through the decisive intervention of Miklós Boskovits in his 1979 monograph *Gli affreschi del Duomo di Anagni: un capitolo di pittura romana*, places this painter in a tripartite schema of workshops responsible for decorating one of the most extraordinary pictorial ensembles in the entirety of medieval Europe: the Crypt of San Magno beneath the Cathedral of Anagni.

His birth date remains irrecoverable from documentary sources; on the basis of stylistic analysis and the probable chronology of his known commissions, scholars tentatively position his birth in the closing decades of the twelfth century, approximately between 1185 and 1200, most likely in the Lazio region or in the broader cultural sphere of Rome and its periphery. His death, equally unattested in surviving written records, is presumed to have occurred sometime in the third quarter of the thirteenth century, perhaps around 1255 to 1265, consistent with the completion of the great fresco campaign in Anagni and the subsequent absence of traceable works attributable to his hand or workshop. The cause of his death is, like virtually every biographical detail pertaining to this master, unknown, a silence that is itself eloquent testimony to the social invisibility of the medieval craftsman, whose art was conceived as an act of devotional service rather than an expression of individual genius.

## Family and Workshop Origins

The Secondo Maestro di Anagni was, by every indication available to modern scholarship, a product of the complex and hierarchically organized system of medieval craft transmission in which artistic knowledge passed not primarily through formal instruction but through the intimate bonds of family lineage and the institutional structures of the workshop, or *bottega*. It is a historiographical commonplace, though no less significant for being familiar, that the overwhelming majority of medieval painters of his generation, active in the Lazio and the broader Italian peninsula during the second and third decades of the thirteenth century, remain entirely anonymous to us, their family names, places of origin, and biographical circumstances preserved only in the rarest instances, typically through contractual documents, inscriptions, or the chance survival of notarial archives.

In the case of the Secondo Maestro, no such documentary evidence has come to light; not a single charter, guild registration, or notarial act can be associated with his name or with any workshop structure that might be identified as his own. His social background must therefore be reconstructed, tentatively and with due scholarly circumspection, from the nature and quality of his technical formation, from the evidence of collaborative workshop practice visible in the Anagni frescoes themselves, and from what is known of the milieu in which artists of comparable training and aspiration operated during this period.

The presence of distinct but recognizably coordinated hands within the Anagni crypt suggests that the Secondo Maestro worked with a team of assistants and collaborators who shared his decorative sensibility and technical vocabulary, even if they did not match the refinement of his most accomplished passages. This collaborative structure is entirely consistent with the family-based workshop model characteristic of medieval artistic production, in which a *capo bottega*, a master craftsman who might be the biological father, an uncle, or an adoptive master of younger practitioners, trained and supervised a small group of subordinates whose labors were essential to the completion of large-scale commissions.

The Roman painting milieu of the early thirteenth century was particularly dense with such family enterprises; the Cosmati, active in precisely the same decades and in closely overlapping geographical territories, offer the most celebrated example of how craft identity could be transmitted across generations while maintaining a recognizable stylistic continuum. It is plausible, though strictly speculative, that the Secondo Maestro was born into a family with established connections to the mosaic and fresco workshops active in Rome during the late twelfth century, workshops whose production would have provided him with both a technical foundation and a network of ecclesiastical patronage indispensable for securing the kind of major commission represented by the Anagni crypt.

The hypothesis advanced by Boskovits that the Secondo Maestro may have been active as a mosaicist in the workshop of San Paolo fuori le Mura before his documented presence at Anagni lends particular weight to the notion of a family background deeply embedded in the monumental decorative arts of the Roman church. Mosaic workshops of this period typically recruited their practitioners through hereditary channels, and a young painter trained in the demanding medium of tessera-based mosaic would have acquired precisely the kind of rigorous chromatic discipline and ornamental precision that characterizes the Secondo Maestro's fresco technique at its most distinctive.

The intense blues and vivid gold yellows that dominate his painted surfaces at Anagni recall unmistakably the luminous chromatic world of Byzantine-inflected mosaic production, and it is entirely reasonable to suppose that his formative years were spent in direct contact with mosaicists of Venetian or Byzantine origin working in Roman ecclesiastical commissions. Such a formation would have been mediated, in the first instance, by the family environment, by a father or older relative who had himself participated in the prestigious mosaic campaigns of the Roman patriarchal basilicas and who transmitted to the younger man not merely technical skills but also the patronage relationships and artistic contacts that sustained a career in monumental religious decoration.

The Secondo Maestro's evident mastery of both decorative organization and figural representation implies a lengthy and intellectually demanding apprenticeship, almost certainly spanning at least a decade of practical training under the supervision of an experienced master. In the workshop culture of the medieval Lazio, such apprenticeships typically began in early adolescence, when a boy of perhaps twelve or thirteen years would be placed, through family arrangement or guild agreement, in the bottega of an established painter, there to learn the grinding of pigments, the preparation of *intonaco*, the application of sinopia underdrawing, and the complex procedural sequence of buon fresco painting.

The quality and range of the Secondo Maestro's surviving work suggests that he completed this apprenticeship with distinction, emerging as a practitioner capable of operating independently and of directing the collaborative labor of subordinate painters on large-scale wall decoration campaigns. His workshop identity, his capacity to train and lead others, implies that he himself subsequently became a *capo bottega*, transmitting to younger painters the techniques and iconographic traditions he had absorbed through his own formation. The diffusion of his stylistic characteristics into related but qualitatively inferior works, such as the frescoes associated with his workshop at the Santi Quattro Coronati in Rome, testifies to this teaching function and to the existence of a recognizable bottega culture organized around his distinctive ornamental sensibility

## Patronage and Ecclesiastical Commission

The primary and most consequential patron associated with the career of the Secondo Maestro di Anagni was the ecclesiastical establishment of the Cathedral of Anagni, acting under the broader authority of the Anagnine bishops during one of the most politically charged and artistically productive decades in the history of the medieval papacy. The fresco campaign in the Crypt of San Magno, to which the Secondo Maestro contributed some of its most visually arresting passages, is generally dated by scholars to the period between 1231 and 1255, a chronological bracket established by Toesca through the convergence of two documentary anchors: the epigraphic evidence recording the removal of the altar of San Magno in 1231 and the formal consecration of the crypt in 1255.

Bishop Alberto of Anagni, under whose supervision the cosmati pavement was executed by Maestro Cosma and his sons Luca and Jacopo, the pavement itself dated 1231, must be regarded as the principal initiating patron of the entire decorative enterprise, even if the specific terms of his commission to the individual workshop masters remain undocumented. The bishop's decision to undertake so ambitious and expensive a pictorial program for the crypt, a space dedicated to the veneration of the relics of San Magno, must be understood within the context of the intense political and symbolic rivalry between the papacy and the Holy Roman Empire that dominated the ecclesiastical life of central Italy during the pontificate of Gregory IX (1227–1241), himself a native of Anagni.

The political and theological dimensions of the patronage context at Anagni have been illuminated by the important study of Hungenholtz (1979), who argued persuasively that the iconographic program of the crypt frescoes was designed in the light of the conflict between Gregory IX and the Emperor Frederick II, culminating in the imperial excommunication, and that the crypt was conceived as a kind of papal chapel in which the painted imagery would assert, through a carefully orchestrated sequence of biblical, hagiographic, and cosmological subjects, the absolute sovereignty of divine and ecclesiastical authority over secular power.

Within this ideologically loaded decorative program, the contribution of the Secondo Maestro occupied a strategically significant zone of the crypt, including the area surrounding the Cristo Pantocratore and the scenes of hagiographic narrative involving San Magno, imagery whose visual authority was critical to the overall symbolic structure of the ensemble. The patron who commissioned this specific section of the decoration almost certainly collaborated with a theologian or learned ecclesiastic in designing the iconographic program, while the Secondo Maestro's responsibility was to give pictorial form to specifications that had been articulated by the patron's theological advisors. This relationship between learned patron and skilled craftsman-executor is entirely typical of medieval monumental painting commissions, in which the *inventio*, the intellectual conception of the subject matter, was typically regarded as belonging to the patron's sphere, while the *dispositio* and *elocutio*, the compositional arrangement and technical execution, fell to the artist.

It is worth considering the possibility that the Secondo Maestro's commission at Anagni was not his first association with papal or episcopal patronage in the Lazio region. Boskovits's hypothesis that he may have worked as a mosaicist in the workshop of San Paolo fuori le Mura, a commission directly controlled by the papacy and executed with the participation of Venetian craftsmen during the second and third decade of the thirteenth century, would imply that he had already demonstrated his capabilities in the context of high-profile ecclesiastical patronage before being engaged at Anagni.

The network of recommendations and professional endorsements through which medieval craftsmen obtained major commissions was dense and socially complex; a painter who had worked satisfactorily on a papal mosaic project would naturally have been known to the bishops and canons of the regional churches within the papal orbit, making the transition to the Anagni commission an entirely plausible consequence of prior successful engagement in the Roman ecclesiastical world. The Anagnine bishops of the first half of the thirteenth century were, moreover, extraordinarily well connected to the highest levels of papal administration, four of them becoming popes in this period alone, and their patronage decisions would have been informed by knowledge of the best available artistic talent within the Roman milieu.

The attribution to the Secondo Maestro of frescoes in the church of San Nicola at Filettino, a small settlement in the Monti Ernici southeast of Rome, opens a further dimension of his patronage profile, suggesting that his commissions were not confined exclusively to the great cathedral project at Anagni but extended to smaller, local ecclesiastical establishments whose patrons sought the services of a painter recognized within the regional artistic network. The nature of the Filettino commission remains poorly documented, but the stylistic affinity between these frescoes and the Anagni work is sufficiently compelling to place them within the same workshop tradition, if not necessarily from the master's own hand.

This pattern of diffusion, in which the distinctive stylistic idiom of a workshop master appears in both major urban commissions and smaller regional decorations, is characteristic of the medieval Lazio painting milieu, where itinerant craftsmen regularly traveled between important centers and provincial destinations, bringing with them the visual languages they had developed in more prestigious contexts. The patrons of Filettino, almost certainly local ecclesiastical figures of more modest means and ambition than Bishop Alberto of Anagni, nonetheless shared with their more powerful contemporaries the fundamental aspiration to dignify the sacred space of their church with painted imagery of recognizable quality and devotional efficacy.

The question of the Secondo Maestro's possible involvement in the decorative program of the Santi Quattro Coronati in Rome, an attribution suggested by Toesca and subsequently nuanced by Boskovits, who associated the Roman works with the Secondo Maestro's bottega rather than with the master himself, introduces a further important layer of patronage into the analysis. The frescoes of San Silvestro at the Santi Quattro Coronati, executed around 1246 and depicting the legend of the Emperor Constantine and Pope Sylvester, were commissioned by Cardinal Stefano Conti, a nephew of Pope Innocent III and vicar of Innocent IV, in the context of the renewed conflict between the papacy and Frederick II.

This commission would place the Secondo Maestro's workshop in direct relationship with one of the most politically active and theologically sophisticated patrons of mid-thirteenth-century Rome, a figure whose program for the San Silvestro cycle was explicitly propagandistic in its assertion of papal authority over imperial power, deploying pictorial narrative as a weapon in a war of institutional legitimacy. Whether the Secondo Maestro personally directed this work or whether it was executed by workshop assistants who had absorbed his stylistic vocabulary, the association between his decorative idiom and the ideological priorities of the Conti patronage is deeply revealing of the cultural and political networks within which he operated.

The broader context of patronage in which the Secondo Maestro functioned was shaped by the extraordinary efflorescence of ecclesiastical building and decoration that accompanied the pontificates of Innocent III, Gregory IX, and Innocent IV, a period during which the papacy deployed art on an unprecedented scale as an instrument of institutional self-assertion. The Lazio region, as the immediate geographical sphere of papal authority, was particularly rich in commissions for monumental pictorial decoration during these decades, and the artists who succeeded in obtaining major papal or episcopal commissions found themselves embedded in a network of demand that could sustain entire careers. The Secondo Maestro was evidently one of the painters who benefited from this patronage environment, his decorative skills and Byzantinizing chromatic refinement precisely suited to the aesthetic expectations of an ecclesiastical elite that associated ornamental splendor with the dignity of the sacred and the authority of the institution. His workshop's capacity to combine rigorous ornamental patterning with iconographically complex figural narratives made him an ideal collaborator for the kind of multi-subject, theologically ambitious pictorial programs that characterized the great thirteenth-century crypt and chapel decorations of the Lazio.

## Painting Style

The Secondo Maestro di Anagni is distinguished within the rich and complex pictorial landscape of thirteenth-century Italian fresco painting by a style of exceptional decorative intensity, organized around a distinctive repertoire of ornamental motifs that are deployed with consistent precision and evident delight throughout his surviving works. His most immediately recognizable characteristic, the one that earned him the earlier designation of *Maestro Ornatista*, or "Master of Ornament," assigned by Toesca in his 1902 study, is the lavish deployment of decorative framing elements: rows of pearls encircling cornices, pearl-bordered arches, rich interlaced borders, and a vocabulary of foliate, geometric, and jeweled ornament that suffuses every architectural division of the painted surface with chromatic splendor.

These ornamental systems are not merely decorative addenda to the figural scenes they frame; they are structurally integral to the visual logic of his compositions, establishing the hierarchical relationships between different zones of the painted surface and guiding the viewer's attention through the complex iconographic program with a directness and confidence that reveals a painter of considerable intellectual sophistication. The use of golden yellow, applied with a consistency and brilliance that suggests familiarity with mosaic technique and with the gold grounds of Byzantine icon painting, gives his surfaces a luminosity that distinguishes them sharply from the more austere chromatic palette of the Primo Maestro working in the same crypt.

The figural style of the Secondo Maestro is governed by a pronounced linearity that sets him clearly apart from the naturalistic tendencies of the Terzo Maestro active in the same ensemble. His figures are defined primarily through the rhythmic articulation of drapery folds, rendered in a manner that reveals deep familiarity with the conventions of Byzantine icon painting and illuminated manuscript art, in which line carries the primary burden of formal definition rather than the modulation of light and shadow. The drapery systems of his painted figures are characterized by a elegant, somewhat schematic quality, the folds falling in regular, decoratively pleasing patterns that prioritize visual harmony over anatomical fidelity, and this quality of regulated elegance extends to the treatment of facial features, which are rendered with a calm, frontal dignity characteristic of the Byzantine hieratic tradition. The hands of his figures are invariably elongated and refined, the gestures measured and formal, the postures governed by a decorum entirely consistent with the sacred context of the imagery. There is, in the Secondo Maestro's figural work, a quality of jeweled precision that connects his practice to the world of the Byzantine enamel-worker and goldsmith, an association that is consistent with the hypothesis of his formation in a workshop environment where different media of sacred art were produced in close proximity to one another.

The chromatic organization of the Secondo Maestro's frescoes reveals a painter of exceptional sensitivity to the expressive possibilities of color as an autonomous compositional force, independently of its role in the description of natural appearances. The intense blues, lapis lazuli-derived pigments applied in deep, saturated passages that recall the midnight registers of Byzantine mosaic, are deployed in systematic counterpoint to the warm golden yellows and the vivid reds of his ornamental borders, creating a chromatic fabric of extraordinary visual energy.

These color relationships are not arbitrary but are organized according to conventions inherited from the Byzantine tradition, in which specific hues carried theological significances, the blue of celestial authority, the gold of divine light, the red of martyrial sacrifice, that a learned ecclesiastical patron would have recognized and valued. The flowering meadows depicted in several passages of the Secondo Maestro's work, dense with schematized floral forms rendered in clear, saturated colors against lighter grounds, constitute one of his most characteristic and personal contributions to the ornamental vocabulary of the Anagni cycle, combining a love of natural ornament with the organizing discipline of his decorative system. These meadow passages have no precise parallel in the work of his co-workers in the Anagni crypt and may reflect a specific regional tradition or a direct engagement with Southern Italian manuscript illumination of the Norman period.

The relationship between the Secondo Maestro's ornamental sensibility and his capacity for narrative pictorial composition is one of the most interesting and complex aspects of his artistic personality, revealing a painter who had not simply absorbed a decorative formula but had developed a coherent pictorial aesthetic in which ornament and narration were fully integrated. His hagiographic scenes, depicting episodes from the life of San Magno and the story of San Giovanni Evangelista, are characterized by a compositional clarity and an expressive control that demonstrate genuine narrative intelligence, even when the figural vocabulary employed is more hieratic and less naturalistically inflected than that of the Terzo Maestro. The spatial organization of his narrative scenes is essentially two-dimensional, the figures arranged in horizontal registers against flat or minimally articulated ground planes, in a manner consistent with the Byzantine pictorial tradition from which his formal training derived. Yet within this fundamentally non-illusionistic spatial convention, the Secondo Maestro achieves moments of surprising expressive intensity through the manipulation of gesture, the contrast of saturated colors, and the rhythmic placement of figures across the picture field. His narrative compositions function not as windows onto a represented reality but as hierarchically ordered symbolic statements, in which every visual element is subordinated to the logic of theological significance rather than the demands of naturalistic plausibility.

The ornamental treatment of architectural elements in the Secondo Maestro's work, the way in which he deploys simulated marble revetment, decorative columns, carved capitals, and interlacing arches as pictorial framing devices, reveals a sophisticated awareness of the visual languages of Cosmati mosaic work and of the architectural settings in which his paintings were designed to function. The crypt of Anagni Cathedral, with its twelve columns supporting twenty-one vaults, presented the painter with an architectural environment of exceptional complexity, and the Secondo Maestro responded to this challenge by developing a decorative system flexible enough to adapt itself to the varying shapes and dimensions of different vault compartments while maintaining a recognizable visual consistency throughout his section of the ensemble. His treatment of the vault surfaces, with ornamental bands defining the webs and ribs, figural compositions adapted to the curvature of the painted field, demonstrates a technical command of the relationship between pictorial surface and architectural substrate that was evidently the product of long practical experience with the specific challenges of vault painting. This mastery of the vault as a pictorial field is one of the most technically demanding aspects of medieval fresco painting, requiring the painter to conceive his compositions in three dimensions, projecting onto a curved surface the visual effects that would be experienced by a viewer standing below and looking upward.

The relationship between the figural and the ornamental registers of the Secondo Maestro's work reveals a painter who understood decoration not as an addendum to imagery but as its essential matrix. In this respect, his approach to painted surfaces differs fundamentally from that of his co-worker the Terzo Maestro, whose developing naturalism was pushing toward a conception of the painted surface as a representation of visual experience in which figural and spatial illusionism gradually assumed dominance over decorative patterning. For the Secondo Maestro, the pictorial surface remained a richly textured fabric of interwoven ornamental and figural elements, in which the figure, however expressively individualized it might be, was always part of a larger decorative whole governed by the rhythmic and chromatic laws of Byzantine artistic tradition. This is not a limitation but a positive artistic choice, reflecting a coherent aesthetic philosophy that understood sacred art as the expression of a divine order in which beauty was a function of harmony, hierarchy, and symbolic precision rather than of naturalistic fidelity. The Secondo Maestro's style, in this sense, represents the fullest and most sophisticated expression of a genuinely Byzantine-derived pictorial tradition in the context of thirteenth-century central Italian painting, a tradition that his more celebrated contemporary, the Terzo Maestro, was already beginning to transcend in the direction of the protoclassical naturalism that would reach its culmination in the art of Pietro Cavallini at the century's end.

The technique of the Secondo Maestro, insofar as it can be analyzed through close examination of the Anagni frescoes, reveals a painter who worked with meticulous precision and systematic planning. The preparatory sinopia drawings that underlie his painted surfaces, visible in areas of later paint loss, suggest a careful preliminary articulation of both figural and ornamental elements before the application of the *intonaco*, consistent with a practitioner trained in the rigorous procedural discipline of the Byzantine workshop tradition. His pigment choices, the costly lapis lazuli for the deep blues, lead white for highlights, red and yellow ochres for the warm tones of drapery and ornament, black for outlines, reflect the material resources available to a well-funded ecclesiastical commission and demonstrate a technical competence that must have been recognized and valued by his patrons. The exceptional state of preservation of the Anagni frescoes, conserved in the controlled environment of the underground crypt and restored with painstaking care during the campaign of 1987–1994 directed by the Istituto Centrale per il Restauro di Rome, means that the chromatic original of his surfaces survives with a completeness that is rare among medieval Italian fresco cycles and that allows scholars to read his technical intentions with unusual clarity.

## Artistic Influences

The artistic formation of the Secondo Maestro di Anagni was shaped above all by the powerful current of Byzantine pictorial culture that penetrated the Roman and Lazio artistic milieu during the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, transforming the indigenous Roman tradition through the importation of Greek and Venetian-Byzantine stylistic idioms mediated primarily through the great mosaic campaigns of the Roman patriarchal basilicas. Pietro Toesca, in his authoritative 1902 analysis, placed the Secondo Maestro specifically in relation to the "byzantinism of Venetian type" present in Rome during the second and third decade of the thirteenth century, a phenomenon associated above all with the workshop that produced the apse mosaic of San Paolo fuori le Mura, a commission in which, according to Boskovits's later hypothesis, the Secondo Maestro himself may have participated as a mosaicist. This Venetian-Byzantine tendency was characterized by a particular handling of gold grounds, by the deployment of intense, saturated color harmonies, and by a figural canon of elongated elegance that distinguished it from the more robust and expressionistically inflected Byzantine manner associated with the late Comnenian tradition and its diffusion through the great Norman mosaic campaigns at Monreale and Cefalù in Sicily. The Secondo Maestro absorbed this specific inflection of the Byzantine tradition thoroughly and consistently, making it the foundation of his mature artistic personality rather than merely one element among several competing influences.

The influence of Byzantine illuminated manuscript art on the Secondo Maestro's pictorial practice is equally perceptible and equally important in understanding the specific character of his decorative sensibility. The ornamental systems deployed in Byzantine lectionaries, psalters, and evangeliaries of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, with their jeweled canon tables, their elaborate headpieces of interlaced foliate ornament, their use of pearl borders and simulated gemstone incrustations, find remarkably close parallels in the ornamental vocabulary of the Secondo Maestro's fresco work at Anagni. The pearl-bordered arches and cornices that constitute one of his most distinctive hallmarks are a direct transposition into the medium of wall painting of ornamental conventions developed in the context of Byzantine manuscript illumination and enamel work, and their presence in the Anagni frescoes confirms the hypothesis that the Secondo Maestro's formation encompassed familiarity with the luxury arts of the Byzantine tradition as well as with its monumental painting. This cross-media fertilization was entirely typical of the medieval workshop environment, in which painters, mosaicists, goldsmiths, and illuminators worked in close proximity and frequently shared both techniques and iconographic resources.

The Norman artistic tradition of Southern Italy, particularly the extraordinary mosaic programs produced in the twelfth century at Monreale, Cefalù, and the Cappella Palatina in Palermo, constitutes a further and significant layer of influence in the formation of the Secondo Maestro, even if the precise channels through which this influence reached him remain difficult to specify with certainty. The Anagni cathedral's own geographical position between Rome and the Kingdom of Naples, and the close political and cultural relationships between the papal Lazio and the Norman-Swabian south during the decades when the Secondo Maestro was active, would have provided multiple opportunities for contact with artistic objects and traditions originating in the southern kingdom. The Atlas Obscura description of the Anagni frescoes notes explicitly that their chromatic vivacity and ornamental character show "clear influence from both Rome and southern Italy (Norman Sicily)", a judgment that is consistent with the broader scholarly assessment of the Secondo Maestro's cultural identity. The Sicilian Norman mosaic tradition, which represented the most spectacular synthesis of Byzantine figural art and Islamic ornamental abstraction in medieval Europe, would have offered the Secondo Maestro a particularly rich source of decorative motifs and chromatic strategies, especially in the domain of the jeweled border and the gold ground.

The Roman pictorial tradition of the twelfth century, exemplified by the famous frescoes of San Clemente (lower church) and Castel Sant'Elia near Nepi, works that Toesca identified as points of reference for the Primo Maestro rather than for the Secondo, nonetheless provided an important contextual framework within which the Secondo Maestro's more Byzantine-inflected practice was understood and evaluated by his contemporaries and patrons. The Roman tradition, with its emphasis on hieratic compositional gravity and its preference for large-format figural presentations organized against simplified grounds, established the visual conventions of monumental ecclesiastical decoration in the Lazio region and constituted the normative backdrop against which the more specifically Byzantine qualities of the Secondo Maestro's style would have been perceived as a distinctive, and evidently desirable, chromatic and ornamental refinement.

His ability to synthesize the inherited Roman conventions of monumental fresco painting with the more precious and ornamentally elaborate vocabulary of the Byzantine-Venetian tradition was precisely the quality that made him an attractive collaborator in the multi-workshop enterprise of the Anagni crypt decoration, where different stylistic registers were deployed in different zones of the space to create a richly variegated but coherent pictorial whole. This synthetic capacity, the ability to absorb multiple artistic traditions and integrate them into a coherent personal style, is one of the marks of genuine artistic intelligence in any period, and its presence in the work of the Secondo Maestro distinguishes him clearly from mere workshop craftsmen working in an unreflective tradition.

The influence of the Secondo Maestro's own work on subsequent generations of painters in the Lazio and beyond represents the final dimension of his artistic significance, and it reveals how thoroughly his distinctive stylistic vocabulary had penetrated the regional artistic environment of the mid-thirteenth century. The frescoes of San Nicola at Filettino, attributed by Toesca and Boskovits to his workshop, demonstrate the diffusion of his ornamental repertoire into provincial contexts where the full resources of the master's personal technique were no longer available but where his decorative conventions remained legible and desirable. More significantly, the influence of his Byzantinizing decorative sensibility can be traced, albeit at a considerable distance and through multiple mediations, in the development of the Roman painting milieu during the second half of the thirteenth century, a period in which the tension between the Byzantine ornamental tradition he represented and the emerging proto-naturalism of the Terzo Maestro's current provided one of the generative polarities around which the great achievements of Cavallini and his contemporaries would eventually crystallize.

## Travels and Geographical Activity

The reconstruction of the geographical itinerary of the Secondo Maestro's career necessarily proceeds from the evidence of his attributed works rather than from any documentary record of his movements, and the resulting picture, however tentative, suggests a painter whose professional life was organized around the gravitational centers of Roman ecclesiastical patronage while extending into the provincial territories of the broader Lazio region. The most securely attributed works place him in Anagni itself, specifically within the crypt of the cathedral, during a period that can be localized to the second quarter of the thirteenth century, with the precise years of his active involvement in the fresco campaign presumably falling somewhere within the bracket 1231–1255 established by Toesca on the basis of the epigraphic and documentary evidence. The journey from Rome to Anagni, a distance of some sixty kilometers along the ancient Via Latina, would have been the most fundamental and regularly repeated displacement of his working life, whether he was based permanently in the city of Anagni during the period of the commission or divided his time between the cathedral site and a Roman studio or workshop. Medieval painters engaged on major commissions sometimes established semi-permanent residence in the locality of their work, particularly when the scale of the project demanded sustained presence over months or years, and the complexity and extent of the Anagni fresco cycle suggests that the Secondo Maestro and his workshop spent considerable time in the town itself.

The hypothesis advanced by Boskovits that the Secondo Maestro was active as a mosaicist in the workshop of San Paolo fuori le Mura before his Anagni commissions implies a professional trajectory that began in Rome and moved outward to the regional periphery, a pattern of geographical mobility thoroughly consistent with what is known of comparable careers in the Roman painting milieu of the period. The great basilica of San Paolo, situated outside the Aurelian walls in the southern reaches of the city, was one of the most prestigious ecclesiastical building sites in medieval Rome, and the mosaic campaign conducted in its apse during the second and third decades of the thirteenth century, with the participation of Venetian craftsmen brought to Rome specifically for their expertise in the Byzantine tessera technique, would have attracted painters and craftsmen from across the Roman region seeking association with a high-profile commission. The Secondo Maestro's participation in this workshop, if confirmed, would testify to a period of intensive professional activity in the Roman urban milieu, during which he would have absorbed the specifically Venetian-Byzantine character of the mosaic tradition there cultivated, and established the network of patronage relationships that eventually led to his commission at Anagni. This Roman phase of his career, precisely because it is hypothetical rather than documented, must be treated with scholarly caution, but its plausibility as a framework for understanding his subsequent artistic development is considerable.

The attribution of frescoes at San Nicola in Filettino to the Secondo Maestro's workshop introduces a further geographical dimension to his professional activity, extending his orbit southeastward from Anagni into the Monti Ernici, a mountainous zone that falls within the historical territory of the diocese of Anagni itself. The town of Filettino, remote and economically modest by comparison with the episcopal city, would have been accessible from Anagni by a journey of perhaps one to two days along mountain roads, and the commission there, if indeed it was carried out by the Secondo Maestro's workshop rather than by local painters imitating his style at secondhand, would represent either a deliberate expansion of his patronage networks into provincial territory or a commission arranged through the institutional mediation of the Anagnine diocese. It is entirely plausible that the bishop of Anagni, satisfied with the work being executed in the cathedral crypt, recommended the same workshop to the clergy of smaller churches within his diocese, a form of patronage dissemination through episcopal recommendation that would have been entirely natural within the institutional structures of the medieval church. The Filettino frescoes thus suggest a pattern of provincial itinerancy that was a normal feature of the medieval painter's professional life, in which the periods of concentrated work on a major urban commission alternated with shorter displacements to provincial clients seeking the prestige of artistic fashions originating in the great centers.

The question of whether the Secondo Maestro traveled beyond the Lazio region in the course of his career, to Umbria, to the Kingdom of Naples, or to the Venetian territories whose artistic traditions so profoundly marked his style, is one that cannot be resolved with the available evidence but that deserves careful consideration in the light of what is known of artistic mobility in thirteenth-century Italy. The distinctive Venetian-Byzantine character of his decorative sensibility might be explained either by direct contact with Venetian artists working in Roman commissions, specifically in the San Paolo workshop, or by a period of residence or travel in Venice or Byzantium itself, but the former explanation is sufficiently economical to render the latter hypothesis unnecessary as a first-order argument. The itinerant habits of medieval craftsmen are well attested in contemporary sources, and painters of genuine quality and ambition regularly traveled considerable distances in pursuit of prestigious commissions, the careers of Cimabue, Duccio, and Pietro Cavallini, all active in the generation following the Secondo Maestro, provide well-documented examples of extensive geographical mobility in the service of art. Whether or not the Secondo Maestro personally traveled to Venice or to the Norman south, the artistic languages he deployed in his Anagni frescoes bore testimony to the remarkable permeability of medieval Italian visual culture across regional boundaries, a permeability sustained by the movement not only of artists but of portable objects, manuscripts, and ivory carvings through the networks of trade, pilgrimage, and ecclesiastical diplomacy that connected the diverse regions of the medieval Mediterranean world.

## Principal Works

The most important works attributed to the Secondo Maestro di Anagni are concentrated in the Crypt of San Magno beneath the Cathedral of Anagni, a subterranean space of exceptional architectural richness divided into three transverse naves by twelve columns supporting twenty-one vaults, the entire ensemble constituting what is often described as the "Sistine Chapel of the Middle Ages" for the quantity, quality, and state of preservation of its frescoed surfaces, which cover approximately 540 square meters.

The Christ Pantocrator, depicted in the hieratic Byzantine convention of the Onnipotente, a full-frontal, enthroned Christ blessing in the Greek manner, is flanked by four saints: from left, Saint Peter the Apostle, a further saint possibly identifiable as Saint Mark the Evangelist, Saint Leonard, and Saint John the Evangelist, their figures rendered in the elongated, linearly defined style that is the Secondo Maestro's most unmistakable personal signature.

The commission for this section of the crypt was arranged under the broad supervision of Bishop Alberto of Anagni, acting within the context of the larger project of crypt construction and decoration that was brought to completion with the consecration of the space in 1255, and the patron's investment in the Venetian-Byzantine decorative grandeur of the Secondo Maestro's work reflects the aspiration to create a space of overwhelming sacred magnificence worthy of the relics of San Magno housed therein.

The fresco is organized according to a cross pattern that divides the vault into four irregular octagonal panels, framed by wide bands of deep blue decorated with white floral scrolls. At the center of the composition, at the intersection of the bands, is a circular medallion containing a Christogram (the Greek monogram of Christ, ☧), which serves as the theological and visual fulcrum of the entire scene. At the four corners of the composition, in small circles, are depicted radiant wheels—the so-called ofanim—which refer directly to Ezekiel's prophetic vision (Ezekiel 1:15-21), where the wheels accompany the four living creatures in their celestial motion.

Each of the four panels houses a tetramorphic being (from the Greek tetra, four, and morphé, form), i.e., the winged creatures described by Ezekiel (Ezekiel 1:4-14) and repeated in the Apocalypse of John (Revelation 4:6-8).

The four beings, identified by patristic tradition—starting with St. Irenaeus in his Adversus Haereses—with the symbols of the four Evangelists, are depicted here as angelic figures with golden halos, literally enveloped in a whirlwind of multi-layered wings rendered in brown, reddish, and gray tones. The human faces with halos emerge from the center of the open wings, in a frontal and hieratic position, according to the purest Byzantine iconographic tradition.

The two figures in the upper panels have enormously dilated wings that occupy almost the entire pictorial field, with their heads overwhelmed by the swirling movement of the feathers. The two lower figures appear more clearly angelic and legible: their faces are clear, their wings unfold symmetrically on either side of their bodies, and the figures seem almost suspended in flight in the white space sprinkled with small six-pointed red stars, a recurring decorative motif in the Second Master's repertoire.

The Second Master of Anagni is unmistakable for his marked decorative accents: the skilful use of lapis lazuli blue for architectural backgrounds, borders with racemes and white flowers, cartouches inlaid with pearls, and the virtuosic rendering of feathers with multiple parallel hatching to create effects of depth. His style owes much to the Byzantine artistic culture of southern Italy, but with a Roman-inspired ornamental sensibility, which has led some scholars to identify him with the same artist who painted the frescoes in the chapel of San Silvestro ai Santi Quattro Coronati in Rome.

## Introduction and Historiographical Framing

*Giovanni Pisano* stands among the small number of medieval Italian artists whose personality is so forceful that it seems to press through the marble toward us, refusing the anonymity that shrouds most of his contemporaries. Son and pupil of Nicola Pisano, he inherited a workshop that had already reoriented Italian sculpture toward the study of antiquity, and he transformed that inheritance into something new: an art of pathos, torsion, and psychological immediacy that his own generation found astonishing and that the twentieth century would recognise as one of the summits of European Gothic. The English sculptor Michael Ayrton, whose 1969 monograph carried an introduction by Henry Moore, gave voice to this modern enthusiasm, and Moore, referring specifically to the statues for the façade of Siena Cathedral, called Giovanni "the first modern sculptor" — a phrase recorded in the modern literature and now inseparable from the artist's reputation.

Yet the very intensity of Giovanni's art has made him a difficult subject for the historian. He signed his major works with proud, sometimes combative Latin verses; he moved restlessly between Pisa, Siena, Perugia, Pistoia, Padua, Prato, and Genoa; and he left behind an oeuvre in which autograph passages of the highest quality stand beside workshop production of uneven merit. The documentary record, though richer than for most thirteenth-century artists, is punctuated by long silences. The result is that almost every major question about Giovanni — the date of his birth, the extent of his hand in his father's works, the nature of his contact with France, the reconstruction of his dismembered monuments, even the year of his death — remains to some degree open.

This essay attempts a synthesis grounded in primary sources and in the scholarship that has proven most durable. The authoritative modern biographical account is that by [Valerio Ascani](/scholars.html#ascani-valerio) in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (vol. 56, 2001), which supersedes much earlier writing and provides the documentary spine followed here. The interpretive frame owes much to the summation offered by [Max Seidel](/scholars.html#max-seidel), whose two-volume *Father and Son: Nicola and Giovanni Pisano* (Hirmer, 2012) is the culmination of a lifetime's research and is organised around a single thesis: that between father and son there was not the "abyss" that [Adolfo Venturi](/scholars.html#venturi-adolfo) and much subsequent writing had posited, but an essential continuity of art and outlook. That contrast between Venturi's rupture and Seidel's continuity is the central historiographical axis of Giovanni studies, and it will recur throughout what follows. For the survey framework of Italian Gothic sculpture as an art with its own visual language — neither a provincial offshoot of France nor a mere prelude to the Renaissance — the standard reference is the work of [Anita Fiderer Moskowitz](/scholars.html#moskowitz-anita-fiderer), while the English-language tradition of connoisseurship descends from [John Pope-Hennessy](/scholars.html#pope-hennessy-john). For the history of reception itself, the indispensable model is [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni)'s *La fortuna dei primitivi*.

## Pisa and Siena c. 1250–1315: The Political and Religious Context

Giovanni's career cannot be separated from the two cities that shaped it. Pisa, where he was born and where he would ultimately die a citizen of Siena, was in the mid-thirteenth century still one of the great maritime republics of the Mediterranean, Ghibelline in allegiance, proud of a cathedral complex — the Duomo, the Baptistery, the Campanile, and later the Camposanto — that constituted one of the most ambitious architectural ensembles in Christendom. The institution that governed this complex, the Opera del Duomo (the Opera della Primaziale Pisana), was a permanent civic-ecclesiastical fabric authority, and it was within its orbit that both Nicola and Giovanni found their most sustained employment.

The pivotal event of Giovanni's lifetime for Pisa was the naval catastrophe of the battle of Meloria, fought on 6 August 1284 off the Tuscan coast near Livorno, in which the Genoese fleet under Oberto Doria annihilated the Pisan navy. The defeat, described by historians as one of the largest naval engagements of the Middle Ages, cost Pisa roughly 5,000 killed and some 9,000–11,000 taken prisoner to Genoa; a measure of the human catastrophe is that, of the approximately ten thousand prisoners carried to Genoa, only about a thousand ever returned home. The battle inaugurated the long decline of Pisan sea power. It also emboldened the Guelph cities of the interior — Florence and Lucca — and left Pisa politically convulsed, a context that helps explain why so much artistic energy in these decades was invested in the monuments of the cathedral square, which functioned as assertions of civic and religious identity precisely as the republic's fortunes on the water waned. There is a grim historical symmetry in the fact that Giovanni's last great commission would be a tomb in Genoa, the city of Pisa's undoing, for the wife of an emperor.

Siena, by contrast, was a Guelph<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guelphs" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> banking city governed for much of Giovanni's active life by the regime known as the Nine (il Governo dei Nove), and it was bound up in a fierce and productive rivalry with Florence. Siena had placed itself formally under the protection of the Virgin, and the Marian civic cult found its supreme expression in the cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta, administered by its own Opera del Duomo. It was into this environment, saturated with Marian devotion and civic pride, that Giovanni was drawn as capomaestro, and it was for the façade of this cathedral that he conceived the most revolutionary of his architectural-sculptural programmes. The Dominican<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> and Franciscan<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> orders were powerful presences in both cities — the Dominicans of Santa Caterina in Pisa, the mendicant preaching culture that shaped the iconography of pulpits everywhere — and the demand for elaborate pulpits (pergami) from which the friars preached is itself a symptom of this religious ferment. Indeed, the iconographic programme of the earlier Pisa Baptistery pulpit has been related by scholarship to the *Sermones* of the Pisan archbishop Federico Visconti, and the choice of Christological scenes on the pulpits to the Franciscan devotion to the infancy and Passion of Christ — a reminder that these monuments were instruments of preaching before they were objects of aesthetic contemplation.

## The Documentary Skeleton of a Life

The bones of Giovanni's biography can be assembled from a scatter of documents, inscriptions, and the testimony of [Vasari](/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari) and, more sparingly, [Ghiberti](/endnotes.html#fn-lorenzo-ghiberti). He declared in the signature-inscriptions of both the Pistoia and Pisa pulpits that he was born in Pisa, which requires that Nicola's family — perhaps of Apulian origin — had settled in the city before the mid-century; Ascani places Giovanni's birth around 1248. No documents survive from his formative years. According to the customs of medieval sculptural workshops, a boy might begin to work regularly alongside his father from about the age of fourteen, though it is more than likely that Giovanni had watched and studied Nicola from early childhood.

The first firm mention of Giovanni comes in the documents surrounding the pulpit of Siena Cathedral: the contract of allocation of 29 September 1265, which bound Nicola, his collaborators, and his son Giovanni to begin work, and the final quittance of the completed work, with Nicola's last payment recorded on 6 November 1268. Thereafter he appears at Perugia (the Fontana Maggiore, inscribed 1278), obtains Sienese citizenship in 1284, and is named in a Sienese document of 3 April 1287 in connection with the cathedral works. He left Siena abruptly in 1297; undertook the Pistoia pulpit (1298–1301) and the Pisa Cathedral pulpit (1302–1310/11); carved the Scrovegni Madonna in Padua (c. 1305–1306); and produced the tomb of Margaret of Brabant in Genoa (1312–1313). The last secure documentary reference places him in Siena in 1314; he died there within the range c. 1315–1319, with the strongest scholarly reconstruction pointing to late 1318, his name being struck from the city's tax registers in 1319. This bare chronology conceals as much as it reveals: between the fixed points lie undocumented years, contested attributions, and works of uncertain date, so that the biography must be built as much from the monuments and their inscriptions as from the archive.

### The Empoli Tondo: Giovanni Pisano's Earliest Independent Work, c. 1265–1270

  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, marble tondo, c. 1265–1270. Originally destined for the sacristy wall of the pieve di Sant'Andrea, Empoli; transferred to the Museo della Collegiata at the end of the nineteenth century. Empoli, Museo della Collegiata di Sant'Andrea.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the earliest work in his corpus, and everything that follows — the Siena façade prophets, the Pistoia and Pisa pulpits, the Pisan Madonnas — must be read as departure from it.

The relief is a marble roundel bounded by a plain raised moulding. Within it, a seated Virgin and Child are compressed into the circular field with considerable ingenuity: the group is not a bust but a full seated figure, foreshortened and gathered so that the Virgin's sandalled feet and the Child's bare ones appear at the lower edge of the disc.

The Virgin occupies the left of the field, crowned over a veil that falls in heavy folds to her shoulder. The crown is a low circlet of foliate fleurons. Her mantle is drawn across the breast in a broad diagonal sweep, the sleeve of the undergarment finished with a fine pleated border at the wrist. Her right hand rises toward the Child's shoulder; her left, open and cupped, rests across her body.

The Child sits to the right, frontally disposed and turned slightly outward. He wears a mantle secured at the chest by a large circular clasp, a detail of some weight: the brooch is an attribute of rank, not of infancy, and it declares the seated child a sovereign. His hair is carved in tight, deeply drilled curls of a distinctly antique cut. His left hand closes around a small object, and the whole body has the compact solidity of a Roman *putto* rather than the mobile softness of Gothic infancy.

The state of preservation is perfect, and allows the two figures to be seen emerging from a recess left in the roughed-out state. This is worth dwelling on. The ground behind the figures was never finished; the tool marks of the initial blocking-out remain visible, and the polished figures rise out of an unresolved matrix. Whether this was intended or simply left is not established, but the effect — smooth flesh and drapery detaching themselves from raw stone — is one Giovanni never repeated at this scale.

The tondo is a work poised between two worlds: on one side the reinterpretation of the antique that Giovanni had inherited from his father Nicola, on the other the opening toward a naturalism of Gothic derivation, of which Giovanni was an early interpreter.

The antique element is not only stylistic but formal. The type of a half-length or seated figure set within a circular field is the *imago clipeata* of Roman funerary and honorific sculpture, familiar from sarcophagi of exactly the kind Nicola had studied in the Camposanto at Pisa. To adopt it for a Marian image is to place the Virgin in the position occupied on a Roman tomb by the commemorated dead — a transposition that would have been legible to the patrons and is consistent with everything the Pisani were doing with antique models in these years. This reading should be presented as an interpretation, since no source consulted advances it explicitly.

What is absent is as telling as what is present. Mother and child do not look at one another; her gaze goes out and to the side, his outward and forward. The reciprocal exchange that would later define the *Madonna del Colloquio* has not yet been invented. The Empoli tondo shows Giovanni working within a hieratic, frontally addressed scheme; the colloquy is a discovery still some two or three decades away, and the distance between the two works measures the whole trajectory of his development.

The small tondo is the oldest surviving evidence of the sculptural furnishing of the pieve of Sant'Andrea, transferred to the Museo della Collegiata at the end of the nineteenth century under a generic ascription to the "Pisan school". It was originally intended to be set into the wall of the sacristy of the Collegiata di Sant'Andrea. The recessed setting matters: the roundel was conceived to be seen frontally at close range, embedded in a wall, in a room used by clergy rather than by the congregation — conditions of intimacy quite unlike the *di sotto in su* calculation Giovanni would later deploy at Siena.

The autograph status has been the object of differing opinions, oscillating between Tino di Camaino and Giovanni Pisano; scholars now refer it unanimously to Giovanni. The Tino alternative is instructive rather than merely wrong: it reflects the difficulty of separating the Pisano workshop's products in a period for which few secure benchmarks exist, and it should be reported as a stage in the historiography, not dismissed.

The tondo travelled to London for "Donatello: Sculpting the Renaissance" at the Victoria and Albert Museum, 11 February – 11 June 2023, an exhibition largely devoted to Donatello's masters, part of a three-venue collaboration between the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, the V&A, the Fondazione Palazzo Strozzi, and the Musei del Bargello. The curatorial placement is itself an argument: the marble roundel with a compressed seated Virgin, ground left rough, figures rising from it, is the ancestor of the Quattrocento marble tondo and of Donatello's *rilievo schiacciato*. A work made around 1270 was exhibited as a precondition of the Renaissance, and the claim is defensible.

### The Siena Cathedral Pulpit, 1265–1268: Nicola Pisano's Workshop and the Formation of Giovanni

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-07.jpg" alt="Octagonal marble pulpit raised on nine columns, four of them standing on lions devouring their prey; trilobed arches span the intervals and the parapet above is carved with densely populated relief panels separated by standing figures, with an eagle lectern projecting at the right.">
  <figcaption>Siena, Cattedrale di Santa Maria Assunta. Nicola Pisano with Arnolfo di Cambio, Lapo, Giovanni Pisano and workshop, Pulpit, contracted 29 September 1265, final payment 6 November 1268. Carrara marble with granite and porphyry columns. Originally beneath the dome to the right of the choir; dismantled in the early sixteenth century and re-erected in the present position by 1543, with Renaissance stair and bridge carved by Bernardino di Giacomo, 1536–1539, probably to a design by Baldassarre Peruzzi.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Siena pulpit is among the best-documented sculptural enterprises of the Italian Duecento, and its chronology rests on archival record rather than on stylistic inference.

The contract of *allogagione*, in which the Opera del Duomo of Siena and Nicola Pisano established the content, materials, and terms of work and payment, is dated 29 September 1265; a series of payments follows through 1267 and 1268, the last on 6 November 1268. These two dates may accordingly be taken as the official dates of the work's beginning and completion. The contract is preserved in the Archivio di Stato di Siena (Diplomatico Opera Metropolitana, 1265 September 29).

The document is unusually specific about labour. Nicola had until November of that year to procure the necessary marble; he undertook to take up residence in Siena from 1 March 1266 and to bring with him from Pisa his *famuli*, Arnolfo and Lapo; he was further permitted to avail himself of a third assistant and of his son Giovanni. The instrument, drawn up in duplicate, was solemnly subscribed in the Baptistery of Pisa, where the operaio Fra Melano had before his eyes the pulpit Nicola had completed five years earlier, and where he proposed to obtain for Siena one considerably richer and more beautiful.

That last circumstance deserves emphasis. The Siena commission was signed in the presence of its own prototype, with the explicit intention of surpassing it. The competitive relation between the two pulpits is not a modern art-historical construction; it was written into the transaction.

The pulpit is an octagonal structure with seven narrative panels and nine columns, carved in Carrara marble, executed between the autumn of 1265 and the autumn of 1268. The documentation permits attribution to Nicola and his young assistants — his son Giovanni, Arnolfo di Cambio, Goro, Lapo, and Donato.

Nicola's great pulpit for the Pisa Baptistery, completed in 1259–60 and carved almost entirely by his own hand, was finished when Giovanni was a boy, and it is unlikely to have offered him more than the menial workshop tasks of polishing.

Its inscription:

> ANNO MILLENO BIS CENTUM BISQUE TRICENO HOC OPUS INSINGNE SCULPSIT NICOLA PISANUS LAUDETUR DINGNE TAM BENE DOCTA MANUS

"In the year 1260 Nicola Pisano carved this noble work; may so gifted a hand be praised as it deserves" — set a precedent for the proud signature that Giovanni would later make his own. Ascani suggests that the demons and the damned of the Baptistery pulpit's *Last Judgement*, athletic and classicising but sometimes caricaturally distorted, with serpentine hair and beards, formed an important object of study for the young Giovanni in the crucial years of his stylistic formation.

Relative to the earlier Pisa Baptistery pulpit, completed by the same artist in 1260, the Siena pulpit presents important architectural, structural, and stylistic differences, while remaining a clear derivation from it.

The plan is octagonal rather than hexagonal, and the consequences are considerable. Eight sides yield seven narrative fields plus the opening for the stair, against the six of Pisa; the wider register admits both an expansion of the programme and a denser figural population. Nine columns carry the body — eight at the perimeter, one at the centre — of which four rest on stylophore lions with their prey, while the central shaft rises from a base ringed with figures. The columns alternate granite and porphyry shafts with white marble, a chromatic play absent from the Pisa scheme.

Above the capitals, trilobed arches spring across the intervals, their spandrels filled with seated figures; the narrative panels are separated not by colonnettes, as at Pisa, but by figures set at the angles, so that the sculpted body begins to take over the articulating function that had belonged to the architectural member. This is the step that Giovanni would carry to its conclusion four decades later at Pisa Cathedral, where columns become caryatids outright.

An eagle lectern projects from the parapet, and the corner figures — prophets, evangelists, angels — form a continuous chorus around the narrative.

The Siena Cathedral pulpit (1265–1268) is the first work in which Giovanni's hand can be sought, for here Nicola relied on a workshop of increasingly autonomous personalities — beside the less clearly distinguishable Lapo and Donato, an already formed Arnolfo di Cambio, and Giovanni himself. The unresolved difficulty that twentieth-century criticism faced in attributing individual figures derives, as Ascani observes, from the very working method of the shop: even where individual pupils executed particular figures, they followed the master's overall compositional and iconographic design, probably fixed beforehand through drawings and cartoons, and their work was then reabsorbed into the whole by Nicola's finishing interventions. This is why one finds figures in which the drapery reveals Arnolfesque or Giovannesque idioms while the faces betray the master's hand, as in the group of Magi in the *Adoration*.

The encyclopedic tradition draws the sceptical conclusion directly: on the whole the pulpit is distinguished by a unity of style and execution that does not permit a division between master and workshop collaborators. This is a position, not a neutral summary, and it should be reported as one pole of the debate.

The passages most securely given to the young Giovanni are the lean and intense *Mystic Christ* on one of the little pillars between the narrative panels and the fragmentary *Annunciate Angel* now in Berlin. A special case is the relief of the *Massacre of the Innocents*, where the dramatic subject and the crush of figures anticipate Giovanni's later tragic compositions; the central group of women, with their bold movements turned inward and their fluid linear energy, the rapid passage of planes, and the very high relief with passages worked *à jour*, points, in Ascani's judgement, to an energetic and youthful hand.

Compared with his father's sculpture, Giovanni's early work already shows a greater adherence to formal and psychological realism and to the linearism of the mature Gothic sculpture of northern France. Seidel, whose doctoral research concerned the relations between central-Italian and north-French art in the thirteenth century, has argued forcefully — against the tradition of rupture — for reading the Siena pulpit as the very crucible in which Giovanni's style emerged under his father's guidance, so that continuity, not break, is the governing category.

It should be noted that the attribution of specific figures on the Siena pulpit to Giovanni remains genuinely contested: some specialists have treated the ensemble as a mature and self-contained work of Nicola alone, while others detect Giovanni's style explicitly in the tibicine angels, in the *Last Judgement*, and in the Berlin *Annunciate Angel*. The debate is unlikely ever to be fully resolved, precisely because the shop's method was designed to submerge individual authorship in a unified whole.

The pulpit does not stand where it was built, and the difference matters for any reading of its programme.

The monumental structure was set in the right-hand zone of the space beneath the dome — as may be seen, draped in cloths, in the Biccherna panel of the *Offering of the Keys of the City to the Virgin* (Biccherna ex Gabella, no. 41). It originally stood beneath the hexagon of the dome, to the right as one looks toward the choir from the central nave. It was moved in 1506, when it was entirely dismantled and remained in abeyance pending its present placement, completed in 1543. A Renaissance staircase and bridge were added, carved by Bernardino di Giacomo in 1536–1539, probably to a design by Baldassarre Peruzzi.

The recent restoration recovered something the monument had lost to view: beneath the dust and the smoke of votive candles, both the general patina and the remains of the colouring and gilding were retrieved. The pulpit was never the white marble object that photography and museum convention have made of it, and any account of its original effect must reckon with polychromy and gold.

Raised at the meeting-point of the ecclesiastical and civic poles of Perugia — the Cathedral of San Lorenzo on one side, the Palazzo dei Priori on the other — the Fontana Maggiore was built to celebrate the completion of the aqueduct that brought water from Monte Pacciano to the summit of the city. It is the last work of Nicola Pisano documented by an inscription and the first in which Giovanni appears as an independent artist. The Latin epigraph names all the protagonists of the enterprise: Fra Bevignate, the Silvestrine monk who directed the works on behalf of the commune; Nicola and Giovanni as sculptors; and Boninsegna of Venice, the hydraulic engineer responsible for the audacious feat of carrying water uphill without pumps. The epigraph is dated 1278, while the bronze basin crowning the fountain is signed by the metalworker Rubeus and dated 1277. Since the project was still under discussion in the autumn of 1277, the sculptural campaign must have been executed with remarkable speed — within roughly a year — which in turn implies a large and tightly organised workshop.

The monument consists of two superimposed polygonal basins of slightly unequal facetting, set on a circular stepped base; the discrepancy in the number of faces produces a subtle rotational effect, a sensation of movement as the eye passes from one register to the next. The whole is surmounted by a smaller bronze cup with caryatid figures, from which the water springs. The stone is the white and pink limestone of the Assisi quarries, whose alternation reinforces the horizontal articulation of the two basins.

- Lower basin

Fifty reliefs — two to each of the twenty-five faces — carry the encyclopaedic programme. The twelve months, each paired with its zodiacal sign and its characteristic labour, run alongside cycles of biblical and Roman history: Adam and Eve, Samson, David, Romulus and Remus. To these are joined the Liberal Arts and a series of animal groups, several of them drawn from the Aesopic tradition, which articulate the political and moral references of the ensemble. The programme is thus neither purely sacred nor purely civic but deliberately universal, a chronicle in which sacred history, Roman origins, the ordering of knowledge and the ordering of the agricultural year converge.

- Upper basin

Twenty-four statues alternate with plain panels, arranged around four cardinal figures: Solomon to the east, Rome to the west, the mythic founder Euliste to the north, and the personified Augusta Perusia to the south. Around these cluster saints, personifications and contemporary officials — the podestà Matteo da Correggio, the capitano del popolo Ermanno da Sassoferrato, the local patrons St Herculanus and St Lawrence. The living magistrates of 1278 are placed on the same plane as biblical kings and legendary founders: the fountain becomes a figured universal chronicle centred on the city itself, an encyclopaedic monument of civic self-representation, and its water the visible sign of the commune's capacity to govern nature as well as men.

Because of the vast quantity of work executed in so short a time by a large workshop, the individual hands of the two masters are here especially difficult to isolate; they must, however, have supplied the overall scheme and the models for the individual scenes. The unity of the whole is itself a product of the medieval shop's practice of setting several craftsmen successively to a single piece, so preserving the coherence of the ensemble at the expense of individual signature. Criticism has consistently assigned to Giovanni alone the Months — especially the later ones, updated on French prototypes — some of the statues, and above all the diptych of the two eagles, where the expressive violence and palpitating vitality of the plumage are matched by the signature he inscribed there.

- The diptych of the two eagles

The pair sits at a hinge point in the iconographic sequence. One account places it at the close of the Liberal Arts cycle, following the reliefs of the Perugian griffin and the Guelph lion; another locates it precisely at the juncture between the Liberal Arts and the Expulsion of Adam and Eve. These are compatible: the eagles mark the seam between the cycle of the sciences and the cycle of sacred history.

The image is doubled: an eagle with wings spread, claws firmly gripping a rock, head turned back in the act of uttering its cry.

The signature is the decisive point. It is on this panel that Giovanni Pisano placed his signature. The expressive force of the figure and the meticulous study of detail are read as justifying the pride with which he signed here, asserting his own individuality against the overwhelming reputation of his father.

#### Why the eagle, and why here

Three readings circulate, and they are not mutually exclusive:

1. Imperial eagle: the traditional identification, but difficult to sustain given Perugia's alignment with the papacy. Some popular guides still repeat it uncritically; one lists the fountain's animal emblems as griffin for the city, lion for the Guelph party, and eagle for the Empire. In a monument that celebrates a Guelph commune, an imperial emblem placed beside the Guelph lion would be incoherent.

2. Pisan heraldry: the eagle is the heraldic emblem of Pisa, which is why the signature falls on this panel. The sculptor marks his own city within a programme otherwise devoted to Perugia's.

3. The Evangelist: the eagle is the symbol of Giovanni's namesake evangelist, making the panel an act of self-celebration and an assertion of individuality. This reading is now the one favoured by the Perugia municipal museum service.

The second and third together explain the doubling: two eagles, two claims — *Pisanus* and *Iohannes*.

The eagles carry more argumentative load than any other relief on the fountain. Carli (1977, pp. 22–23) held that the eagle diptych alone, being signed by Giovanni, can be recognised as direct Pisano work, the inferior quality of the remaining forty-eight reliefs betraying execution by a team following Nicola's compositions and drawings. Cavallucci's distribution is less severe but points the same way: he assigns to Giovanni the statuettes of the Roman Church, Theology, the Liberal Arts, the Eagles, and the labours of October and December.

## The French Question

No problem in Pisano studies has generated more inconclusive debate than the question of Giovanni's relation to French Gothic. That his mature style is deeply marked by the formal and psychological realism and the linearism of mature northern French sculpture is not in doubt; the question is how that knowledge reached him. Ascani frames the possibilities judiciously: such knowledge came not only through the importation of portable objects — illuminated manuscripts and goldsmiths' work — but also, very possibly, through the direct observation of monumental sculpture beyond the Alps, "as cannot have been difficult for a Tuscan of his generation," or through drawings and sketchbooks carried by itinerant artists, a practice common since the first half of the thirteenth century but one that has left, for these decades, no explicit written trace in the areas Giovanni frequented. A journey to France, sometimes proposed on stylistic grounds, cannot be documented and is not accepted by all scholars; it must be flagged as a hypothesis, not a fact.

What can be said with confidence is that the ivory *Madonna and Child* now in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Pisa, plausibly carved on Giovanni's return from Siena, exploits the curve of the tusk to produce the *hanchement* of the most recent French Gothic statuettes — objects he certainly knew at first hand — and that the openwork gabled crown of the Baptistery presupposes intimate familiarity with the vocabulary of French Rayonnant architecture. The foundational modern authority for the French Gothic background against which this question must be posed is [Willibald Sauerländer](/scholars.html#sauerlander-willibald), whose *Gotische Skulptur in Frankreich, 1140–1270* (1970; English edition *Gothic Sculpture in France, 1140–1270*, 1971) broke the Chartres-centred account of the genesis of medieval sculpture and is credited with having rewritten the history of early French Gothic sculpture.

Sauerländer's contextual, transnational method — which emphasises continuous artistic exchange across regions rather than isolated national traditions — provides the framework within which the current consensus situates Giovanni, in preference to the older model of a single formative "trip to France." The specialist literature on the sculpted façades of the French cathedrals, above all the studies of the west front of Reims by [Peter Kurmann](/scholars.html#kurmann-peter), furnishes the comparative material for understanding what Giovanni did at Siena, though the comparison must be handled with care and without overclaiming: Giovanni did not transplant a French façade to Tuscany but radically rethought its premises, as the next section shows. Seidel's contribution has been to insist that the French component in both Pisani be treated in depth and as integral to their development rather than as an external veneer, and his 2012 monograph accordingly devotes sustained attention to the Pisani's relation to France and Rome.

### The *Madonna del Colloquio*: Giovanni Pisano and the Invention of the Exchanged Glance

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-04.jpg" alt="Marble sculpture of a crowned, veiled Virgin holding the Christ Child on her left arm; mother and child turn their heads toward one another, their faces meeting at close range, the drapery falling in long diagonal folds. Photographed in studio light against a dark ground.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, known as the Madonna del Colloquio, marble, c. 1290–1302. Originally on the external lunette of the western portal of the south transept, Pisa Cathedral; removed to the Camposanto in 1829, now Pisa, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. Ministry of Culture catalogue image, ICCD 0900235648.</figcaption>
</figure>

Among the marble Madonnas that Giovanni Pisano produced for the Pisan cathedral fabric, the group conventionally known as the *Madonna del Colloquio* is the one on which the sculptor's mature achievement in the intimate mode rests most securely. It is a marble sculpture, attributed to Giovanni Pisano, catalogued by the Ministry with a date-range of 1290–1299, and now displayed in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Pisa. The museum arranges it in the sixth room alongside two further Madonnas by the same hand; it presents a half-figure format rare in medieval sculpture, and its particular effect lies in the emotional charge generated by the exchange of looks and the disposition of the two bodies.

The image reproduced here is the Ministry's own catalogue image (ICCD 0900235648), which records the work in raking studio light against a dark ground — a lighting convention of the twentieth-century soprintendenza photo campaigns that flattens the marble's surface warmth but renders the carving of the drapery folds with exceptional clarity.

The Virgin is shown crowned and veiled, the crown a light open circlet of foliate fleurons set over a veil that falls in a broad, heavy sweep across the right shoulder and down the back. She carries the Child on her left arm, her right hand raised to support him at the chest, her left gathering the drapery beneath his weight. Her head inclines slightly forward and downward; his turns up and back toward her. The two faces meet at close range, and it is this meeting — not the frontal address to a worshipper conventional in Duecento Madonnas — that governs the whole composition.

The drapery is organised in long, unbroken diagonals: the mantle descends from the right shoulder across the torso in a single dominant fold, countered by the vertical cascade of the veil on the opposite side. The linear treatment is fine and highly controlled, yet it never dissolves the underlying mass; the figure retains an imposing volumetric presence beneath the surface incision. A horizontal joint is visible across the lower body in the image, below which the block continues in plain vertical folds.

The name derives from the iconographic novelty: mother and son are joined in a wordless conversation of glances. Giovanni founds his image on the motif of the divine colloquy between adult and child, dear to art north of the Alps but not unknown to classical antiquity either — the comparison offered is with the Hermes and Dionysos of Praxiteles at Olympia, of which Giovanni may have seen some derivation.

The formal mechanism is one Giovanni had developed at scale elsewhere. The technique of correspondence at a distance — of counterpoint between separated elements, of gestures and lines of force carried across intervals — which he had used to coordinate the individually unbalanced Prophets and Sibyls of the Siena Cathedral façade, is here converted into a correspondence of looks, hints, and smiles. What results is the gentle variant of the dramatic capacities revealed in the Sant'Andrea pulpit at Pistoia: a fresh lightness attentive to the nuances of amorous, maternal, and filial sentiment as they emerge through minimal expressive variations in the features of the face.

That such effects were legible to a cultivated contemporary is not a modern inference. Dante, describing the reliefs of humility rewarded and pride punished on the terrace of the proud in *Purgatorio*, demonstrates that an educated observer of the period expected precisely this order of effect from sculpture.

The displacement history is unusually well documented and materially affects how the work should be read.

The group remained for a long period — from the beginning, perhaps — on the external lunette of the small western portal in the south transept of Pisa Cathedral. Lasinio removed it to the Camposanto in 1829, during the cathedral restorations, replacing it with a statue of a saint. In the Camposanto it was placed beneath the *Vintage of Noah*, mounted on a fifteenth-century frieze with two putti bearing a festoon; after 1909 it was moved beneath the *Departure of Abraham and Lot for Palestine*, still in the north corridor. It entered the first Museo dell'Opera in 1935; after exhibition at the Mostra della Scultura Pisana del Trecento it passed into the Opera's storerooms, and from 1960 into the hall adjoining the Camposanto, from which it moved in 1986 to the new Museo dell'Opera.

Two consequences follow. First, the work was conceived for exterior placement in a lunette, seen from below and at a distance, and its present eye-level museum display reverses the intended viewing conditions. Second, its long exposure on the cathedral's flank accounts for the weathering of the surface, which the studio lighting of the catalogue image partly conceals.

The attribution to Giovanni has never been seriously contested. Quality and style impose the name of Giovanni for this Madonna; the question of dating, however, remains highly intriguing, and the most diverse solutions have been proposed. The sequence of the debate runs as follows.

| Stage | Position |
| --- | --- |
| From the nineteenth century | Beginning with Milanesi's notes to Vasari, the group was identified with the Madonna of Henry VII, which Vasari indeed places on a lunette of the cathedral transept. All datings resting on this identification point to 1313. |
| 1930 | Weinberger's intervention, confirming the identification of the *Pisa* figure as the surviving element of the Henry VII group, disposed of this unjustified tradition. |
| 1957 | Barsotti proposed identifying it with the Madonna for whose execution the Opera del Duomo of Pisa purchased a block of marble in 1302. |
| Current range | Setting aside the older solutions based on the Henry VII identification, opinion oscillates between Giovanni's first independent works and the date suggested by the 1302 document, with numerous intermediate hypotheses. |

The Ministry's own compiler favours the later end: on balance the most probable hypothesis appears to be a dating to the early period of the Pisan sojourn at the head of the cathedral workshop, after the contract concluded with Borgogno di Tado, since the Madonna in its triumphal equilibrium appears almost as the culmination of the cycle of variations on the coordination at a distance of intrinsically unbalanced figures that Giovanni achieved with the Siena Prophets and Sibyls.

However, this reasoning sits in tension with the same record's own date-field of 1290–1299, since the contract with Borgogno di Tado belongs to 1302. The record is internally inconsistent, and the inconsistency should be reported rather than resolved by choosing one field over the other. It also matters that a dating of "around 1280", which circulates widely, represents the early extreme of the range and not a settled position. Popular sources compound the confusion: at least one widely consulted guide dates the work to the mid-thirteenth century, which is impossible, Giovanni not yet having been born.

### The Façade of Siena Cathedral: Giovanni Pisano and the First Monumental Statuary Front in Italy

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-05.jpg" alt="Polychrome marble Gothic cathedral façade with three deeply splayed portals below and, above, a large circular window flanked by loggias, crowned by three gabled cusps filled with gold-ground mosaics; free-standing statues project from the wall at the level of the upper cornice and along the flanking turrets.">
  <figcaption>Siena, Cattedrale di Santa Maria Assunta. West façade. Lower register by Giovanni Pisano, 1284–c. 1297, with the three splayed portals, lateral turrets, and the statue programme of prophets, patriarchs, sibyls, and philosophers (originals in the Museo dell'Opera della Metropolitana; the figures in situ are copies). Upper register attributed either to Camaino di Crescentino, c. 1299–1317, or to Giovanni di Cecco, after 1376. Cusp mosaics executed in Venice, 1878. White marble with red Siena marble and green serpentine of Prato.</figcaption>
</figure>

The front of Santa Maria Assunta in Siena is not one design but two, imperfectly joined. The lower half belongs to Giovanni Pisano and to the last two decades of the thirteenth century; the upper half, with its rose window, its two orders of loggette and its three gilded cusps, belongs to a later and separate campaign whose authorship and date remain genuinely disputed. The junction is not concealed: the two halves follow different formal rules, as is visible in the failure of the piers flanking the rose window to align with those below. Any reading of the façade must begin from this disjunction, because the coherence of Giovanni's conception is legible only in the lower register, and the celebrated statuary belongs entirely to it.

From 1285, following Nicola's death (probably in the preceding year), Giovanni took over the enterprise of the Siena cathedral façade, whose walls had been carried nearly down to the level of the front. In 1284 the commune had granted him Sienese citizenship, specifying — as the *Costituto* records — that for his whole life he should be exempt from all communal burdens, taxes (*dazi* and *colte*), exactions, and military service; the privilege appears to have stood until his death, his name being cancelled from the relevant registers only in 1319. He is named capomaestro of the cathedral works in a document of 3 April 1287.

Here a genuine scholarly divergence should be recorded: while the Italian encyclopedic tradition treats the 1287 document as already naming him capomaestro, other scholarship regards a document of 17 July 1290 — which calls him *magister Operis beate Virginis Marie et caput magistrorum dicte opere,* adding that the work could not be well completed without him — as the first fully secure attestation of the title. The distinction does not affect the substance of his responsibility for the façade, but it is a useful caution against precision where the documents do not support it.

However, an anonymous fourteenth-century chronicle gives 1284 as the year of the laying of the first stone of the façade, and government documents attest Giovanni at work as capomaestro on the lower part from 1284 to about 1297. His departure was abrupt. He left Siena suddenly, probably on account of criticisms levelled at him by the commune for waste and disorganisation — a characterisation that circulates widely in the literature and should be reported as the traditional explanation rather than as documented fact, since it belongs to the same fund of anecdote as the Pistoia and Pisa disputes.

At Siena, Giovanni made the façade his masterpiece, conceiving the whole front as a great "speaking" polyptych. Less interested than French sculptors in concentrating the figurative message around the portals, he devised, with the patrons, a bold iconographic plan that treated the entire façade as a field within which, around the Virgin and Child, prophets, patriarchs, philosophers, and sibyls announce — willingly or unwillingly — the coming of Christ and the virginal conception. The architectural scheme derives from late-Romanesque and proto-Gothic Tuscan and French prototypes: the triple portal recalls the west front of Chartres, while the lateral towers simplify and revise the model of the French "harmonic" façades, reducing the towers and shifting them off the axis of the lateral portals.

To this phase belong the three splayed portals with their lunettes and gables and the two lateral turrets. The central portal carries a round arch, the lateral ones slightly pointed; the jambs are articulated by slender twisted colonnettes with foliate capitals, which carry archivolts likewise twisted, their keystones cut as satyr heads. That last detail is worth pausing over: the satyr masks at the crown of a cathedral portal declare, at the threshold, the same confidence in the usable antique that will justify the presence of Plato and Aristotle above.

The truly revolutionary stroke was in the placement of the statues. Instead of ranging his figures within the splays of the portals or confining them within galleries as at the northern cathedrals — where sculpture is strongly subordinated to the architecture, treated almost as column or niche-bound — Giovanni set them at the structural nodes of the façade, free-standing before the marble wall, definitively detached from the architecture behind them, including the now-empty pedimented aedicules from which they seem to be walking out, and thus free to rotate, advance, and twist according to the emotional tension of each personage. With the Siena statues Giovanni effectively liberated sculpture from architecture, and made the cathedral front the first monumental statuary façade in Italy.

The prophets and patriarchs — Isaiah, Habakkuk, Moses, David, Solomon — are joined by Miriam (Mary sister of Moses), Balaam, the Erythraean Sibyl, and, most surprisingly, Plato and Aristotle, who by the exercise of reason are imagined to have sensed the approaching divine maternity of Mary; other figures include Simeon, Daniel, Jesus son of Sirach, and Ezekiel. The human figures number fourteen, of which eight stand on the front, three on the left flank and three on the right.

The reconstruction of the original arrangement, dependent in large part on inscriptions preserved by the niches, was worked out in the earlier twentieth century (by Lusini and by Keller) and places David and Solomon above the central portal, each with a lion, with the remaining prophets and philosophers distributed across the flanks and buttress faces, animals beneath them — a bull beneath the Erythraean Sibyl, a griffin beneath Moses, horses symbolising the Church beneath certain of the prophet pairs.

### Seen from the Wrong Place: Giovanni Pisano's Façade Statues for Siena Cathedral

  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-09.jpg" alt="Eight over-lifesize weathered marble figures standing in a receding rank against a dark ground, lit obliquely from above: bearded and youthful men in heavy classical drapery, their necks long and heads thrust forward, several leaning markedly out from the hips.">
  <figcaption>Giovanni Pisano, prophets, patriarchs, sibyls and philosophers from the lower façade of Siena Cathedral, marble, c. 1285–1297. The originals of the façade figures were removed from the exterior and replaced by copies; eight of the fourteen now stand in a single receding rank in the Museo dell'Opera della Metropolitana, on a low continuous plinth against a dark wall. The two images reproduced here record that rank from its opposite ends, and read together they give the full sequence.

The display conditions are themselves part of the subject. These figures were conceived to be seen from the piazza, from perhaps fifteen metres below, at oblique angles, against the sky and the polychrome marble of the façade. Here they are seen at close range, at nearly eye level, in raking artificial light, in a dark room. Nearly every judgement the modern beholder forms about them is formed under conditions the sculptor did not anticipate and would not have accepted.

- The optical corrections

The problem of viewing *di sotto in su* is fundamental to their design. Giovanni calculated the elongations, the forward inclinations of the heads, and the turns of the bodies precisely so that, seen from below, the figures would appear to lean out and speak. This calculation for a specific viewpoint is among the most sophisticated aspects of his art, and it is the principal reason why the statues, examined at eye level, can appear disconcertingly distorted.

The distortions should be described rather than apologised for. The necks are long and thrust forward; the heads are proportionally large; the torsos are compressed and the legs correspondingly extended; the shoulders are drawn back so that the chest reads as a broad surface catching light from above. Several figures lean so far forward from the hips that at eye level they appear to be pitching off the plinth. From below, each of these corrections resolves.

The profile view offered by the second image is in one respect more informative than a frontal one, since it shows that the forward lean is not a matter of the head alone but of the whole body, articulated from the ankles upward. Giovanni was not adjusting a face for legibility; he was designing a figure for a line of sight.

Seen in the round, the figures are also complete sculptures, with backs, with shoulders that carry the turn of the torso, and with drapery that continues behind. Nothing about them is relief, and nothing depends on the wall — which is the museum's one genuine advantage over the façade, and the clearest confirmation of what the placement at the structural nodes had achieved.

### A Chorus in a Row: Eight Façade Statues of Siena Cathedral in the Museo dell'Opera

  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-08.jpg" alt="Eight over-lifesize marble figures standing in a receding rank on a low plinth against a dark wall, lit from the right: the two nearest are pale and comparatively well preserved, while those behind are discoloured, deeply pitted and abraded. All lean forward from the hips with necks extended and heads carried in advance of the chest.">
  <figcaption>Giovanni Pisano, eight prophets, patriarchs, sibyls and philosophers from the lower façade of Siena Cathedral, marble, c. 1284–1297. Siena, Museo dell'Opera della Metropolitana, installation view. The originals were removed from the façade and replaced by copies.</figcaption>
</figure>

The second image carries information that is material rather than iconographic. The rank falls into two visibly distinct groups. The two figures nearest the camera are pale, close-grained, and comparatively even in surface: the drapery folds retain their edges, the modelling of chest and shoulder is legible, and the marble reads as marble. Behind them, from the third figure back, the surface changes abruptly — discoloured to a mottled ochre and grey, deeply pitted, and in places scaled away, with heads abraded to the point where features survive only as masses, and hands and forearms broken or lost. Six centuries in the open air preceded the transfer indoors.

Three explanations are possible, and all three may be operating together:

1. Differential exposure according to position on the façade, figures on the sheltered flanks weathering less than those on the front and buttress faces
2. Differential cleaning and consolidation in successive restoration campaigns
3. Differences in the marble itself, the blocks being drawn from more than one quarry or bed

Which of these predominates cannot be settled from the images I gathered, but what is certain is that the modern beholder's impression of individual figures is heavily conditioned by conservation history: a statue that reads as vigorous and one that reads as ruined may have been carved to the same standard by the same hand.

- The rank as an accident and as evidence

Set in a row, the figures make visible something the façade dispersed across its width: they were designed to relate to one another. The heads turn at different angles, some to the left and some to the right; the gestures are not repeated — a hand raised to the breast, an arm extended and open, a fist closed on a lost attribute. Conceived for viewing from below and at a distance yet carefully finished in their visible parts, they form a chorus of unified expressive intensity, a continuous exchange of glances and words designed to lead the eye toward the central Marian group.

The museum rank is of course not the original disposition, and the correspondences it appears to reveal are accidents of display. But the capacity for correspondence is not accidental: it is what the technique of coordination at a distance was designed to produce.

Among the individual figures, the Miriam has been especially admired for the slender uplift and lightness of its body and drapery, distinct from the more massive, earthbound solidity of the others.

- Reception

It is before these figures that the twentieth century's estimate of Giovanni as a modern master was formed. The prophets of Siena are the works usually associated with Henry Moore's celebrated tribute — a claim that circulates widely and should be verified against Moore's own text before it is quoted. The circumstance is worth registering with some irony in any case, since that estimate was formed precisely in the museum, under the conditions that misrepresent them. What the twentieth century admired as expressive distortion, and read as an anticipation of modern sculpture, was in large part a set of optical corrections functioning exactly as designed, seen from the wrong place. The reception history and the original intention are not the same thing, and both belong in the account.

What stands on the façade today is a modern replacement set. The image therefore documents the intended optical effect — the figures read correctly from the piazza — while the sculptural quality survives only in the museum, under conditions that defeat it.

#### The upper façade: an unresolved attribution

The completion of the front is the point at which the literature divides, and the division is not a matter of nuance.

- The traditional account

Construction of the upper part began in 1376 under the direction of Giovanni di Cecco, who worked to a new and elaborate design inspired by the cathedral of Orvieto. This is placed in the aftermath of the abandoned "Duomo Nuovo" of 1339, begun under Lando di Pietro, which the economic collapse following the plague of 1348, political upheaval, and above all grave structural miscalculations obliged the Sienese to abandon; the old building was then brought to completion, and in the years after 1376 Giovanni di Cecco finished the upper façade on the Orvietan model.

- The revision

The upper part is now assigned to Camaino di Crescentino, father of the sculptor Tino di Camaino, who worked on it between about 1299 and 1317. The earlier attribution to Giovanni di Cecco rested on a document referring to works "at the Façade in the piazza del Duomo, loggia of the bishop," which was taken to concern the cathedral front but in fact refers to the façade of the Bishop's Palace, standing on the same square and likewise the bishop's residence. The discovery of a document of 1310 urging the execution of a mosaic for the central cusp, together with three previously overlooked fourteenth-century chronicles dating the completion of the façade to 1317, clarified the question; it was Camaino di Crescentino who gave the façade its present tricuspidate form.

The consequence deserves emphasis, because it inverts a familiar line of transmission. If the Sienese upper façade was substantially complete by 1317, Orvieto — whose façade Lorenzo Maitani directed from about 1310 — cannot have been its model; the dependence runs the other way, and at least one account states plainly that Maitani took the Siena front as the model for Orvieto. The two positions cannot both stand. The English-language literature has not generally absorbed the revision: reference works still describe the work as stalled by 1317, taken over by Giovanni di Cecco at an uncertain date in the mid-fourteenth century, completed around 1370, and modified by him toward a more Gothic appearance with high gables and pinnacles under the influence of Orvieto.

The façade is faced in white marble, the red marble of Siena, and the green serpentine of Prato. The chromatic banding, carried around the whole building, refers to the *balzana*, the black-and-white arms of the city.

Several conspicuous elements postdate both medieval campaigns:

- The three gilded mosaics of the cusps — from left to right the Presentation of Mary in the Temple, the Coronation of the Virgin, and the Nativity — were executed in Venice in 1878. They occupy fields that had awaited decoration since the fourteenth century; the 1310 document urging a mosaic for the central cusp shows how long the intention outlived its fulfilment.
- The busts at the centres of the three gables over the portal arches were added only in the seventeenth century.
- The oculus carries a stained-glass Last Supper, visible from within.
- The bronze central door is a twentieth-century addition; the wooden lateral doors are likewise not medieval.

At the summit stand statues of angels and a figure of the Virgin, for which the great oculus appears to serve as a halo — a placement that reconciles the two campaigns retrospectively, since it completes the Marian ascent Giovanni had begun below.

## The Break with Siena and the Return to Pisa (1297)

In 1297 Giovanni left Siena abruptly, following grave disputes with the cathedral overseers. The archival scholar [Peleo Bacci](/scholars.html#bacci-peleo) (not to be confused with the Fribourg medievalist [Michele Bacci](/scholars.html#bacci-michele), discussed below) was the first to establish, in his documentary studies later gathered in *Documenti e commenti per la storia dell'arte* (1944), the circumstances of the rupture: Giovanni was accused of delays, serious defaults, and poor organisation of the works. (A charge sometimes repeated, that he removed marble or materials, is not confirmed by the documents as reported and should not be asserted without direct consultation of Bacci's archival findings.) He returned to Pisa, where he had evidently kept a residence and where he had not yet finished his work on the upper registers of the Baptistery loggias. The façade at Siena was left with its statues awaiting installation, and it was completed after Giovanni's departure by Sienese sculptors who worked to a partly autonomous programme; the upper façade would later be rebuilt, from 1376 under Giovanni di Cecco, to a wholly different design inspired by Orvieto, so that the division of the upper front no longer corresponds to Giovanni's lower one.

By 14 December 1297 Giovanni had relocated to Pisa, in all likelihood already as master-builder of the cathedral; in March 1298 he appears among the experts consulted in an inquiry into the leaning campanile. His standing had by now been formalised in the very titles the documents give him — he had been "caput magistrorum" at Siena — and the pattern of his last two decades was set: a return to his native city, a great new pulpit, and a series of prestigious commissions that carried his art across Italy.

### Qui res non egit inanes": Giovanni Pisano and the Pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-03.jpg" alt="Hexagonal white marble pulpit on slender columns, its parapet carved with five deeply undercut relief panels of crowded figures; lions and a crouching male figure support the columns at the base.">
  <figcaption>Pistoia, Pieve di Sant'Andrea. Pulpit, 1298–1301 (dated by inscription "sub primo mille trigentis"). Marble. Hexagonal parapet with five narrative reliefs of the life of Christ, carried on seven columns resting on stylophore lions, a telamon, and a base of eagle, griffin, and winged lion.</figcaption>
</figure>

Commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301, the pulpit for the parish church (*pieve*) of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia is, in the near-unanimous judgement of criticism, Giovanni's most fully achieved masterpiece and one of the highest moments of Italian medieval art — carved in the years around 1300 that saw elsewhere the mature activity of Arnolfo di Cambio, Giotto, and Duccio. Hexagonal like Nicola's first pulpit and of relatively contained dimensions to suit the narrow Romanesque church, it is of almost exclusively autograph execution.

The commissioning circumstances are known only through the monument itself. Giovanni appears to have visited Pistoia in person during 1297 or 1298, when he inspected Sant'Andrea, took its measurements, and signed a written contract with the patron — a document that has not survived. Vasari places the commission in 1297, while the inscription records completion in 1301; Vasari also states that the work took four years, owing to the exacting nature of the carving.

Giovanni set beneath the panels a proud signature-inscription that reads in part: *"Sculpsit Ioh(ann)es qui res no(n) egit inanes. Nicol(a)i nat(us) sensia meliore beatus. Que(m) genuit Pisa doctu(m) sup(er) omnia visa"* — "Giovanni carved it, who wrought no empty works, son of Nicola and blessed with higher skill, whom Pisa bore, learned above all things seen." The inscription also records the patron, the canon Arnoldo, and the two treasurers, Andrea Vitelli and Tino di Vitale. There is no false modesty here; the verses convey both Giovanni's character and his consciousness of his own worth, and they testify to the social and cultural elevation that the greatest artists could attain in the late-medieval Italian city.

The text runs along the cornice between the pointed arches and the parapets. The Ministry catalogue transcribes the whole in capitals, incised *a sgraffio*: it opens with praise of the Trinity and joins the work begun to its completion *sub primo mille trigentis*; names the *plebanus* Arnoldus as principal of the work and provider of funds; identifies Andrea Vitelli and Tino, son of Vitale, as the best of dispensers; and closes with the sculptor's own verses. Two points follow. First, the date 1301 is embedded in the epigraph itself and is therefore documentary rather than inferential. Second, the patron is designated *plebanus* — parish priest of the pieve — which is not quite the same office as "canon"; the two designations circulate interchangeably in the literature and should be reconciled.

The metrical scheme is leonine hexameter throughout, and the rhetorical structure is worth noting for its own sake: Trinity, patron, treasurers, sculptor. Giovanni's claim occupies the final and climactic position, the place the epigraphic convention reserved for the most weighty name.

The structure develops the schemes of Nicola's pulpits, especially the Sienese one, to which it makes more than one iconographic reference. Seven columns support the hexagonal body: two rest on stylophore lions with their prey, one on a bowed telamon (an Atlas-Adam of self-portrait-like realism), while the central column rises from a base of three winged creatures — an eagle, a griffin, and a winged lion — in springing, opposed, rotating tension. These three creatures, as the medieval interpretation had it, allude to the twofold nature of Christ: the eagle as queen of the sky and the lion as king of the earth united in the griffin, symbol of Christ as God and man.

The columns carry trilobed arches, more sharply vertical than at Siena in keeping with the greater Gothic tension of the whole and the narrower proportions. In the spandrels stand prophets, Saints Peter and Paul, and the kings David and Solomon; on the little pillars are sibyls; between the panels stand the *Mystic Christ*, Saint Andrew, Saint Stephen, and groups of angels and apostles, with the tetramorph originally beneath the lecterns.

The proportional logic deserves emphasis, since it is easily lost when the pulpit is reproduced in isolation. Sant'Andrea is a narrow twelfth-century *pieve* with a low nave; the pulpit had to be legible from close quarters and from below, in a confined space. The contraction of scale relative to Siena, and the compensating verticality of the arcade, are responses to that setting, not merely stylistic preferences.

#### The five narrative panels

The five narrative panels of the parapet unfold the story of salvation. The first combines the *Annunciation*, the *Nativity* with the *Bath of the Child*, and the *Annunciation to the Shepherds*, concentrating in a single field three episodes of the mystery of the Incarnation. There follow the *Dream* and the *Adoration of the Magi*; the *Massacre of the Innocents*; the *Crucifixion*; and the *Last Judgement*.

### The Reaching Hand: Giovanni Pisano's *Nativity* at Sant'Andrea, Pistoia

  <figcaption>Annunciation, Nativity and Annunciation to the Shepherds, first relief of the parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia. Marble, 1298–1301. The Annunciation at the upper left with Joseph seated below; the reclining Virgin at the centre reaching toward the swaddled Child in the manger, with the ox and ass above; the Annunciation to the Shepherds and the flock at the right; the preparation of the Child's bath at the lower centre. The *Nativity* is the first of the five narrative slabs of the hexagonal parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia, commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301. It opens a cycle that continues with the Dream and Adoration of the Magi, the Massacre of the Innocents, the Crucifixion, and the Last Judgement.

The image is unusually valuable for documentary reasons: the incised inscription band is visible running along the cornice beneath the relief at the lower right, beginning with a cross and the words **LAUDE DE TRI[NI]** — the opening of the leonine hexameters that praise the Trinity, name the *plebanus* Arnoldus and the treasurers Andrea Vitelli and Tino di Vitale, date the work *sub primo mille trigentis*, and close with Giovanni's own famous claim. The image therefore records both the panel and the epigraphic evidence for its date in a single frame, and it confirms the identification of the pulpit beyond question.

The panel is bounded at each angle by standing figures rather than by colonnettes: at the left a full-length draped figure with a book, at the right the corner group with the beginning of the following relief beyond. Where the Pistoia parapet is separated into discrete panels by corner figures, the Pisa Cathedral reliefs of a few years later would press toward a continuous, teeming narrative frieze curving around the whole structure.

The first panel combines the *Annunciation*, the *Nativity* with the *Bath of the Child*, and the *Annunciation to the Shepherds*, concentrating in a single field three episodes of the mystery of the Incarnation.

- Upper left: the Annunciation

The angel leans in from the left, wings folded back, one hand raised; the Virgin stands before a slender fluted architectural member, her head inclined and her hand brought up to her breast in the gesture of startled acceptance. The pair occupies its own recessed zone at the top of the field.

- Lower left: Joseph

A bearded man sits below the Annunciation, his cheek propped on his hand, in the posture of meditative withdrawal that the Byzantine tradition assigns him. His placement directly beneath the Annunciation is pointed: he broods below the event he did not witness.

- Centre: the Nativity

The Virgin reclines diagonally across the field, veiled, raised on one elbow, her body turned inward. Her free arm reaches across to the swaddled Child in the manger, the hand extended toward his face. Behind and above, the ox and the ass lean their heads down over the crib; further back, angels crowd the upper register.

- Right: the Annunciation to the Shepherds

Angels occupy the upper right; below, shepherds in short tunics stand among trees, one with a pipe raised to his lips, another turning upward. The lower right corner is filled with sheep, goats and a ram, deeply undercut and closely observed.

- Lower centre: the Bath of the Child

Two women attend a large footed basin. One bends over it and pours from a jug, testing the water; the other, seated, holds the naked infant on her lap, waiting. The Child is not yet in the water.

#### Three departures from Nicola

The panel is best read against the two treatments of the same subject by Giovanni's father, at the Pisa Baptistery in 1259 and at Siena in 1265–68. Giovanni retains the multi-episode syntax entirely; what he changes is the emotional logic within it.

1. The Virgin turns toward the Child: at Pisa the reclining Virgin gazes upward and away, her scene juxtaposed with the manger but not connected to it. At Siena she inclines her head downward toward the bathing group, and the episodes begin to be linked. Here she turns her whole body inward and reaches out to touch the swaddled infant. The maternal relation has become the panel's subject rather than one of its components — the same discovery that governs the *Madonna del Colloquio* and that Giovanni would spend the rest of his career developing.

2. The Bath is shown as preparation, not as event: Nicola places the Child standing in the basin, the act already under way. Giovanni shows the moment before: the water being poured, the naked body held ready. The choice converts a narrative episode into a passage of ordinary domestic attention, and it is characteristic of his interest in what surrounds an event rather than the event itself.

3. The relief is deeper: The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. In this panel the shepherds' flock at the right, the ox and ass, and the bending woman at the basin are all cut nearly clear of the block, and the shadows they cast are part of the design.

Among the innovations of the Pistoia style is a more supple plasticity, joined to the customary descriptive power and linear concentration, that lends the figures mobile and even questioning expressions, virtuosically varied according to the angle of view; and a recovered classicising sweetness quite unlike that of his father.

Both qualities are present here. The Virgin's face is broad, calm and classical in cast, yet the head is set at an angle that changes markedly as the beholder moves along the pulpit. The bending woman at the basin is a passage of pure supple plasticity: a single continuous curve from the shoulder through the back to the trailing hem, with no linear incident to interrupt it.

### Warned in Sleep: Giovanni Pisano's *Dream and Adoration of the Magi* at Pistoia

The *Adoration of the Magi* is the second of the five narrative slabs of the hexagonal parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia, commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301. It follows the *Nativity* and precedes the *Massacre of the Innocents*. The panel presents the *Dream* and the *Adoration of the Magi* together in a single field.

The field divides into two unequal halves along a diagonal, and the division corresponds to the two episodes.

1. Left: the Dream: three Magi lie asleep side by side along the lower left, fully clothed and crowned, their heads on a single long bolster, a heavy coverlet drawn across them; the foremost lies with his face turned upward and his hands resting on the cloth. Above and behind them an angel bends forward from the waist, one arm extended over the sleepers in the gesture of warning — the dream of Matthew 2:12, in which the Magi are told not to return to Herod. Above the angel three horses' heads crowd the upper left corner, their bridles and manes deeply cut, one seen almost frontally.

2. Right: the Adoration: the three Magi appear again, awake and standing. The eldest kneels at the centre-right, bearded, his crown removed and held low, his hands raised toward the Child; behind him stand the other two, one crowned and bearded in profile, the other younger and beardless, both holding covered vessels. At the extreme right the Virgin sits enthroned, veiled, the Child on her knee reaching forward toward the kneeling king. A female figure stands between the standing Magi and the throne. Behind the throne, at the right edge, further heads appear in shallow relief.

The two episodes are separated by nothing except the angel's body, which serves as the hinge: the arm that warns the sleeping Magi points back across the field toward the encounter that has already occurred.

This is the panel's most consequential decision, and it should be stated plainly.

The Dream follows the Adoration in Matthew's account: the Magi come, they offer, they are warned in sleep, they depart by another way. Giovanni places the Dream at the left and the Adoration at the right, so that the field is read against the chronology. The beholder moving along the parapet from the *Nativity* encounters the consequence before the cause.

Two explanations are available:

1. The first is compositional: the sleeping bodies are horizontal and low, the Adoration group vertical and high, and the arrangement fills the field more effectively than the reverse would.

2. The second is theological: the panel's terminal position is the throne, and every reading of the field, in whichever direction, resolves upon the Virgin and Child. Giovanni has subordinated sequence to destination.

The group of the three kings asleep is among the most remarkable passages of the pulpit, and it has no equivalent in Nicola's treatments of the subject at Pisa or Siena, where the Dream is either absent or reduced.

Three bodies are laid parallel under one cover, distinguished only by the angles of their heads and the disposition of their hands. The drapery of the coverlet is carved as a single broad expanse of shallow, sweeping folds — a passage of the supple plasticity that marks the Pistoia style, with no linear incident to interrupt the surface. Against it the angel's body is cut deeply and at a sharp diagonal, so that the contrast between the inert horizontal and the intervening diagonal carries the whole meaning of the episode without any gesture of alarm on the part of the sleepers.

That restraint is characteristic. Giovanni is representing the moment at which the warning is delivered but not yet received.

The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. Here the horses' heads, the angel's shoulders, and the kneeling Magus are cut nearly clear of the block, while the attendant heads behind the throne are barely raised — five or six planes are legible across the field.

Among the innovations of the Pistoia style is a more supple plasticity, joined to the customary descriptive power and linear concentration, that lends the figures mobile and even questioning expressions, virtuosically varied according to the angle of view; and a recovered classicising sweetness quite unlike that of his father. The heads of the standing Magi exemplify the first quality — the bearded king in profile has a face that reads differently from every position along the parapet — and the young Magus and the female figure beside the throne the second.

### The Field Without a Centre: Giovanni Pisano's Massacre of the Innocents at Pistoia

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-12.jpg" alt="Marble relief packed edge to edge with violently contorted figures: a crowned man leans forward from a throne at the upper right; young soldiers in short tunics raise their arms to strike and wrench infants from women's grasp; mothers clutch living children or bend double over dead ones; small nude bodies lie heaped along the lower edge; at the upper centre one veiled woman has drawn her hands across her eyes.">
  <figcaption>Massacre of the Innocents, third relief of the parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia. Marble, 1298–1301. Herod enthroned at the upper right, the sole seated figure; the executioners in short tunics moving through the centre and left; the mothers filling the greater part of the field, shown simultaneously at every stage from seizure to bereavement; the slaughtered children heaped along the lower edge.</figcaption>
</figure>

The *Massacre of the Innocents* is the third of the five narrative slabs of the hexagonal parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia, commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301. It stands at the centre of the cycle, between the *Adoration of the Magi* and the *Crucifixion*, and it is by common consent the most harrowing narrative relief of the Italian Middle Ages.

The inscription band running beneath the relief along the cornice (not visible here), shows the words **PRINCEPS EST OPERIS PLEBANUS VEL DATOR ERIS / ARNOLDUS DICTUS QUI SEMPER SIT BENEDICTUS**: the verses naming the parish priest Arnoldus as principal of the work and provider of the funds. The panel that records the most violent act in the Infancy narrative is thus set directly above the name of the patron who paid for it.

The relief abandons every organising device Giovanni had used in the two preceding panels. There is no division into episodes, no architectural framing, no reading direction. The field is filled edge to edge with a single continuous mass of struggling bodies, and no ground surface remains visible anywhere.

- Upper right: Herod

The king sits enthroned in the upper right corner, crowned, bearded, leaning far forward with one arm thrust out and downward in the gesture of command. Behind and beside him stand counsellors, one bearded and hooded, one shown in profile with a hand raised. He is the only figure in the panel who is seated, and the only one at rest.

- Upper centre and left: the executioners

Young soldiers in short belted tunics move through the middle of the field, their bodies twisted into violent diagonals. At the upper left one raises both arms above his head, gripping an infant by the leg to swing it downward; at the centre another, in a mail sleeve and turban-like headcloth, seizes a child from a mother's arms with one hand while raising a blade with the other; at the right a third pulls a naked infant away from a woman who grips its other side. Their poses are athletic and classicising, and the exposure of their bare limbs distinguishes them sharply from the enveloping drapery of everyone else in the panel.

- Centre and lower left: the mothers

The largest part of the field is given to them. They are shown at every stage of the action: one at the centre still holding her living child against her cheek, her face lifted; another clutching an infant while a soldier's hand closes on it; others in the lower register bent double over small bodies already dead, their faces pressed to them. A woman at the lower left crouches with her hands over a child's head. Behind them, at the upper centre, a row of older women stand with hands raised to their faces, one covering her eyes entirely.

- Lower edge

Along the base lie the slaughtered children, small nude bodies heaped and overlapping, several with limbs projecting beyond the frame.

The panel is bordered on the left by a standing full-length figure, draped in heavy draperies, holding a child upside down in his left hand. He is depicted in the act of killing him.

#### The composition of an atrocity

The relief's achievement is a compositional one, and it can be stated precisely.

1. Herod's isolation: the single seated figure occupies the corner from which the command issues, and everything else in the field is its consequence. His stillness is not dignity but detachment: he is the one person present who is not touching anyone.

2. The absence of a focal point: unlike the *Nativity* and the *Adoration*, which resolve upon the Virgin and Child, this panel offers nothing to which the eye can settle. Incidents multiply without hierarchy; every quadrant contains an act of violence carried out in full. The beholder cannot organise the scene, and that inability is the representational point.

3. Grief in every register at once: Giovanni does not sequence the emotions. Within a single field he shows anticipation, the moment of seizure, the act itself, and the aftermath of bodies already cold, disposed not in narrative order but simultaneously and adjacently. The mother still holding her living child and the mother bent over her dead one are separated by a hand's breadth.

4. The withheld face: among the standing women in the upper register one has drawn her veil across her eyes with both hands. In a panel where every other face is turned toward some part of the horror, the one figure who refuses to look is given the highest position. It is the relief's single moment of restraint, and it functions as a direction to the beholder.

In each of the reliefs Giovanni pursues an expressive intensity that is only occasionally softened, as observers have noted, by accents of aching melancholy. This panel contains no such softening.

The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. Here the undercutting is at its most extreme: the executioner at the upper left is detached from the ground across the whole width of his raised arms; heads in the middle register project their own shadows onto figures behind them; the dead children along the base are carved fully in the round and simply laid against the block.

Among the innovations of the Pistoia style is a more supple plasticity, joined to the customary descriptive power and linear concentration, that lends the figures mobile and even questioning expressions, virtuosically varied according to the angle of view. The mothers' faces demonstrate it: each is caught at a different point in the passage from disbelief to knowledge, and each alters as the beholder moves along the parapet.

#### Genealogy

The panel has a direct ancestor and a direct descendant.

Nicola had introduced the subject at Siena in 1265–68, in a field likewise filled edge to edge with executioners, mothers and slaughtered children — a subject absent from the Pisa Baptistery cycle and admitted at Siena only because the octagonal plan afforded two additional panels. It has been argued that the central group of women there, with their bold movements turned inward, their fluid linear energy, the rapid passage of planes, and the very high relief with passages worked *à jour*, points to an energetic and youthful hand, that is, to Giovanni himself.

Whether or not that attribution stands, the compositional problem was posed at Siena and solved here. And it was here, in the view of the critic whose formulation the standard account preserves ([Cesare Gnudi](/scholars.html#gnudi-cesare)), that the opposed tensions of Giovanni's art suddenly fused into a unity of moved interior grandeur, the pulpit representing the stylistic apex of his production.

Giovanni would return to the subject a decade later at Pisa Cathedral, where the *Massacre* occupies its own panel within a nine-relief cycle. That treatment is more crowded and more architecturally continuous; it is not more terrible.

### The Wound Given and the Wound Felt: Giovanni Pisano's *Crucifixion* at Pistoia

  <figcaption>Crucifixion, fourth relief of the parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia. Marble, 1298–1301. The dead Christ at the centre, feet crossed on a suppedaneum above the skull of Adam; at the left the Virgin collapses into the arms of the holy women with St John standing apart; below the cross Longinus drives the lance upward; at the right a crowd of individuated witnesses, and at the upper right an angel thrusting a small figure downward.</figcaption>
</figure>

The *Crucifixion* is the fourth of the five narrative slabs of the hexagonal parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia, commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301. It follows the *Massacre of the Innocents* and precedes the *Last Judgement*.

Its place in the cycle is not merely sequential. The *Massacre* had shown a field without a centre, an atrocity in which no figure could organise the beholder's attention. Here Giovanni restores an axis — a single vertical body around which everything else is disposed — and the recovery of order after the preceding panel is part of the meaning.

Christ occupies the centre, arms stretched wide along a cross whose members are cut with deliberate plainness. The head has fallen forward and to his right, the eyes closed, the mouth slightly open; the beard and hair are drilled into separate locks. The body is emaciated and explicitly dead: the ribs stand out in a hard arc, the abdomen is drawn in, the pelvis twists, and the legs descend to feet set one above the other on a small suppedaneum — the three-nail Gothic type, not the four-nail arrangement of the older tradition. The loincloth is knotted at the hip and falls in a single diagonal sweep. Beneath the foot of the cross, at the base of the field, lies the skull of Adam.

The two thieves occupy the extreme upper corners of the field, one at each edge, their crosses set obliquely and partly cut by the framing moulding. Both are shown nude to the waist, the torsos twisted, the arms stretched along their cross-arms at angles that break the vertical discipline of Christ's body at the centre. The consequence is a composition spanning the full width of the slab: three crosses, the central one frontal and stable, the flanking two canted inward and pressed against the boundaries of the relief.

Giovanni has used the frame itself as a compositional instrument, letting the lateral crosses run out of the field so that the beholder infers a scene larger than the panel can contain. The device also solves a practical problem peculiar to a hexagonal parapet, where each slab is narrow: a symmetrical three-cross Calvary of the conventional kind would have required either shrinking Christ or omitting the crowd, and Giovanni has refused both.

The contrast in bodily treatment carries the theological distinction that the iconography requires. Christ hangs dead and still, the head fallen forward, the limbs composed; the thieves are alive and writhing, their bodies wrenched into sharp diagonals, shoulders dislocated by their own weight, heads thrown to one side.

This is not merely a difference of narrative moment — Christ has died and they have not — but the standard means by which medieval sculpture distinguished a sacrificial death from a punitive one. Crucifixion as an execution deforms; crucifixion as an offering does not. The thieves also serve a formal function that the *Massacre* panel had made necessary: after a field in which no figure could organise the eye, Giovanni restores an axis, and the two contorted flanking bodies exist partly to measure the stillness of the one between them.

The traditional distribution places the penitent thief, Dismas, at Christ's right, and the impenitent, Gestas, at his left, following the same right-hand-of-grace convention that governs every Last Judgement of the period. The confirmation comes from face expression: Dismas is portrayed as turning his head towards the cross, while gazing directly at Longinus; conversely, Gestas' expression is a mixture of rage and hopelessness.

Now, to the winged figure at the upper right: the figure bends, with head bowed. The wing behind is large and feathered rather than membranous. This reads as Synagoga expelled by an angel — the aged figure, resembling a woman, with the tablets of the Law, driven from the scene at the moment the Old Covenant is superseded.

Two things support it:

1. Every attribute fits: gender, age, the tablets, the lateral expulsion, the position at Christ's left, opposite the corner where Ecclesia would stand.

2. This is exactly the motif reported for Nicola's Siena Crucifixion, where the sources describe an angel at the upper right driving away an aged woman.

At the right of the cross, the Virgin collapses backward into the arms of the holy women, her body given over entirely to their support, her head fallen and her face upturned — the swoon that Giovanni's father had introduced at Pisa from Franciscan meditative writing, and that here becomes the organising event of the whole left half. Two women bear her weight while a third grips her hand. Immediately left of the cross stands a solitary draped figure with the head bowed and turned inward, a full vertical column of the composition given to him alone: St John. Behind and above, further mourners press upward, one raising a vessel, others with arms extended toward the cross. Angels lean in from the upper left corner.

The right half is a dense press of individuated heads at every angle — bearded and clean-shaven, young and aged, hooded and bare. At the centre-right a soldier in a short belted tunic and boots turns his back to the beholder and looks up toward Christ, one arm raised; beside him a beardless youth in a short tunic stands with hands loose at his sides. Further right the heads crowd four and five deep, several with mouths open, one shouting, one turning away.

Below the cross, at the centre, a soldier drives the lance upward into Christ's side, his head thrown back and his mouth open, both hands on the shaft: Longinus, and the panel's second focal incident after the swoon.

Giovanni has placed two acts at the same height on either side of the shaft, and the pairing is the composition's argument.

On the left the Virgin falls, and the women who catch her convert grief into a physical burden borne by three bodies. On the right Longinus drives the lance, and the act converts violence into an equally physical exertion of two arms and a braced back. Both are labour; both are directed at the same body; both occur at the moment of death. The panel sets the wound that is inflicted against the wound that is felt, and gives them equal weight and equal space.

That symmetry is Giovanni's own. Neither of Nicola's Crucifixions, at Pisa or at Siena, isolates Longinus as an actor, and neither gives the swoon a group of this scale.

The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. Here Longinus, the standing soldier, and the swooning group are cut nearly clear of the block; the heads at the right recede through five distinct planes; the ground has been annulled entirely.

Among the innovations of the Pistoia style is a more supple plasticity, joined to the customary descriptive power and linear concentration, that lends the figures mobile and even questioning expressions, virtuosically varied according to the angle of view; and a recovered classicising sweetness quite unlike that of his father. The classicising sweetness is present in the body of Christ, which for all its emaciation retains an anatomical coherence and a smoothness of surface drawn from the antique. The mobile and questioning expressions belong to the right-hand crowd, where no two faces register the event alike.

In each of the reliefs Giovanni pursues an expressive intensity that is only occasionally softened by accents of aching melancholy. The Virgin's upturned face is one of those accents.

### The Cross at the Hinge: Giovanni Pisano's *Last Judgement* at Pistoia

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-14.jpg" alt="Densely carved marble relief: a bearded Christ sits enthroned at the upper centre with arms opened and chest bared; directly below his feet two bowed figures raise a cross; at the left clothed figures stand in tiered ranks with half-nude figures rising along the lower edge; at the right nude bodies are seized and inverted by horned demons, a coiled serpent and a snouted beast filling the corner.">
  <figcaption>Last Judgement, fifth relief of the parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia. Marble, 1298–1301. Christ enthroned at the upper centre displaying the wounds; beneath his feet angels raise the Cross at the exact axis of the field; the Elect in ordered ranks at his right with the resurrecting dead below; the Damned seized by demons and beasts at his left, a serpent coiling among them.</figcaption>
</figure>

The *Last Judgement* is the fifth and final narrative slab of the hexagonal parapet of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea in Pistoia, commissioned around 1298 and completed in 1301. It closes a cycle that runs from the *Nativity* through the *Dream and Adoration of the Magi*, the *Massacre of the Innocents*, and the *Crucifixion*.

The compression is the panel's governing condition and its clearest point of difference from the family precedents. Nicola had compressed the entire eschatological drama into a single slab at the Pisa Baptistery, then separated the Elect from the Damned into two full fields at Siena. Giovanni here returns to the single field, but with a population and a violence that the Pisan panel of 1259 had no room for. The hexagon offers five slabs; the Judgement gets one, and it is made to carry everything.

#### Composition

- Upper centre showing Christ the Judge: He sits enthroned, bearded, the chest bared and the arms opened wide in the *ostentatio vulnerum* — the display of the wounds by which the sentence is justified. The feet rest on a projecting ledge. He is the only figure in the field granted space around him, and the only one at rest.

- Upper register, flanking: Apostles and the company of heaven range on both sides in three superimposed tiers, bearded and hooded, several with hands extended toward the throne or gesturing outward. The background surface has been almost entirely annulled behind them.

- Centre, below the throne with the Instruments of the Passion: directly beneath Christ's feet, at the exact vertical axis of the panel, angels display the Cross. Two figures support it — one at each side, heads bowed, the arms of the cross laid across their shoulders — while further angels stand behind. The *arma Christi* thus occupy the physical centre of the composition, interposed between the Judge and the judged.

- Left half showing the Elect: The figures remain clothed and upright. Toward the upper left they stand in ordered ranks, one crowned figure with a hand raised in prayer, others in mitres and caps, faces lifted toward the throne. Toward the lower left the ranks give way to the resurrecting dead: half-draped and nude figures rising from below the frame, torsos bare, arms braced, heads turned upward.

- Right half showing the Damned: the order collapses entirely. Nude bodies are seized, inverted, and dragged; demons intervene among them with grotesque physiognomies, horned and shaggy heads, and hair drilled into serpentine masses. A great serpent coils through the group at the centre-right. At the far right a monstrous beaked or snouted creature and a broad-muzzled demon with bared teeth occupy the corner. Along the lower right the damned crouch and crawl, some gripped by the hair, one with hands pressed to the face.

#### The Cross at the centre

The Cross stands at the exact axis on which the field divides between salvation and damnation, and it is displayed by angels rather than merely present. Everything that happens to the right — the seizure, the serpent, the beasts — happens on one side of it; everything on the left, the ordered ranks and the rising dead, on the other. The instrument of the sacrifice is thus made the physical hinge of the sentence, and the composition states the theological argument spatially: the Judgement is executed by reference to the Crucifixion, which the beholder has just seen in the adjacent panel.

The relation between the two reliefs is the point. Walking the hexagon, one passes from the Cross erected on Golgotha to the Cross held up as evidence, from a body dying to a body enthroned displaying the same wounds. Giovanni has designed the fourth and fifth panels as a single argument delivered in two stages.

The field constructs its meaning through a contrast that is formal before it is iconographic.

- Salvation is legible as verticality and clothing

The Elect stand upright, their bodies enclosed in continuous drapery, their heads aligned in ranks. Even the resurrecting dead at the lower left, though nude, rise along a vertical axis.

- Damnation is legible as horizontality and nakedness

The Damned are inverted, splayed, dragged sideways; no figure among them holds an upright posture; every one is stripped.

This is the same device Nicola used at Pisa and developed across two panels at Siena, and Giovanni compresses it back into one — but with a marked intensification of the bestial. The demons here are not merely distorted men. The serpent, the snouted beast, and the broad-muzzled creature at the right introduce a zoological register that has no counterpart in his father's work, and the right margin of the panel edges toward the monstrous rather than the caricatural.

The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. In this panel the angels bearing the Cross and the demons at the right are cut nearly clear of the block, while the ranks behind Christ recede through four or five planes to a surface barely raised.

In each of the reliefs Giovanni pursues an expressive intensity that is only occasionally softened by accents of aching melancholy. The bowed heads of the two angels supporting the Cross are such an accent — the one moment of quiet in a field otherwise given over to acclamation and violence.

All in all, in each panel Giovanni pursues an expressive intensity that is only occasionally softened, as observers have noted, by accents of aching melancholy. The reliefs are strongly differentiated, the figures carved at varying depths, some standing almost free of the ground and even breaking beyond the upper frame, so that the scenes are violently contrasted and the light plays across them in intense chiaroscuro. The *Massacre of the Innocents*, with its tangle of soldiers, mothers, and slaughtered children, is among the most harrowing narrative reliefs of the Middle Ages.

It was here, in the view of the critic whose formulation the standard account preserves ([Cesare Gnudi](/scholars.html#gnudi-cesare)), that the opposed tensions of Giovanni's art suddenly fused into a unity of moved interior grandeur — the pulpit representing the stylistic apex of his production. Among the innovations of the Pistoia style is a more supple plasticity, joined to the customary descriptive power and linear concentration, that lends the figures mobile and even questioning expressions, virtuosically varied according to the angle of view; and a recovered classicising sweetness quite unlike that of his father.

Set within the sequence of the four great Tuscan pulpits, Pistoia occupies a position of particular evidential value. It shares much with Nicola's Pisa Baptistery pulpit of 1260 and with the Siena Cathedral pulpit of 1268, on which Giovanni had assisted; these works draw on Roman sarcophagi and other sources to form a style representing an early revival of classical sculpture while remaining Gothic, and drawing also on such sources as French ivory carving, though Giovanni's manner moves away from his father's strong classicism toward one more marked by northern Gothic art.

Its evidential importance lies in its condition. Where the Pisa Cathedral pergamo of 1302–1310 survives only as a twentieth-century reconstruction assembled from fragments dispersed for three centuries, the Pistoia pulpit has come down substantially as Giovanni left it. Any argument about the master's own hand, about the intended sequence of a salvation narrative, or about the relation of sculptural to architectural form in his work must therefore begin here and reason outward. Pistoia is the control against which the reconstructed Pisan monument is read.

### The Eagle That Left Pistoia: Giovanni Pisano's Gospel Lectern in New York

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-15.jpg" alt="White marble eagle standing with wings raised and spread, head erect and turned to one side, talons grasping a book; a hexagonal bookrest with a projecting lower ledge rests across the raised wings. The plumage is carved in overlapping scale-like feathers on the breast and long striated flight feathers at the wing edges.">
  <figcaption>Eagle lectern from the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia, c. 1301, marble, Overall 70.5 × 61.5 × 40.6 cm; 93 kg. New York, The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.</figcaption>
</figure>

The dating aligns the piece exactly with the completion of the pulpit of Sant'Andrea at Pistoia, recorded by the inscription on the parapet cornice: a Latin inscription along the bottom band of panels gives 1301 as the date of completion and the names of the artist, the patron Canon Arnoldo, and the treasurers Andrea Vitelli and Tino di Vitale.

The lectern takes the form of an imposing full-size eagle grasping a book in its talons, standing with head erect, facing to its left, wings held poised as if ready for flight. A marble bookrest, hexagonal in form with a protruding ledge at the bottom, meant to hold the Gospels for liturgical reading, rests on the bird's wings.

The plumage is organised in three distinct registers of treatment: broad overlapping scale-like feathers across the breast and shoulders; longer lanceolate coverts descending the wings; and, at the outer edges, flight feathers rendered as fine parallel striations cut almost as incision rather than relief. The legs and feet are worked with a rougher, shaggier texture, the talons splayed wide across the volume of the book. The bird stands on a shallow hexagonal plinth whose geometry answers that of the bookrest above.

The pose is a considered compromise between two demands. As an image the eagle must appear on the point of flight, wings raised and beak lifted; as a piece of furniture it must remain absolutely stable and present a level surface at the correct angle for a deacon reading aloud. Giovanni resolves this by canting the wings back and outward so that they read as spread while functioning as a cradle, and by anchoring the whole on the mass of the book beneath the talons.

#### A caution the Met itself supplies

The head is a later replacement. This is the single most consequential fact about the object and it should govern any stylistic argument built upon it. The eyes, the beak, the crest, the treatment of the feathers around the face are not his.

What remains original is the body, the wings, and the plumage, and it is there that the comparison with the documented Pistoia carving must be made.

The eagle served as the crowning element of a tetramorph, a sculptural composite of the symbols of the four Evangelists. The Met's records for two related pilasters in its own collection make the arrangement explicit: the pilaster supported an eagle lectern for the reading of the Gospels, and the eagle of John thus completed a representation known as a tetramorph.

The logic is liturgical before it is decorative. The Gospel book physically rests on the symbol of the Evangelist whose vision reaches highest; the eagle, which alone among creatures was held to look directly into the sun, carries the text of the Word. In the Pistoia pulpit's original disposition the tetramorph stood beneath the lecterns, and the eagle crowned it — so that the four symbols ascended from the parapet to terminate in the object from which the Gospel was actually read.

#### Dispersal

The lectern is not in New York by accident of collecting but as a consequence of a documented liturgical reordering. Originally the pulpit stood in front of the presbytery. It was removed from there, probably around 1619 under the parish priest Bartolomeo Cellesi, in order to adapt the church to the liturgical guidelines issued by the Council of Trent. The new placement required modifications to various parts: some corner groups of the parapet had to be moved and the two lecterns were removed. The lectern with the Eagle of Saint John the Evangelist is now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York; the other lectern, the Angel Pietà, is in the Gemäldegalerie of the Staatliche Museen, Berlin.

Two points follow:

- first, the Pistoia pulpit — which I have described in the preceding entries as substantially intact, and which is indeed far better preserved than the Pisa Cathedral pergamo — is not wholly intact. It lost both its lecterns and had elements of its parapet rearranged. The claim of integrity needs qualifying, and the two entries on the pulpit should be corrected accordingly.

- second, the loss was caused by the same force that dismantled and reordered so much Italian church furniture in the decades after Trent: not iconoclasm, not neglect, but liturgical reform. The pulpit was moved because it stood where the reformed rite required clear sightlines to the altar, and the lecterns were casualties of the move.

### Held Up for Reading: Giovanni Pisano's Angel Pietà Lectern from Pistoia

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-16.jpg" alt="Squared marble block carved on its face with a shallow recess: at the centre the dead Christ rises half-length from the tomb, head fallen forward on the chest, arms crossed at the wrists; two winged angels in short tunics stand at either side, each raising one arm to hold a cloth arched above his head, the right-hand angel steadying his forearm. The upper surface of the block is flat and framed by a narrow lip.">
  <figcaption>Angel Pietà lectern from the pulpit of Sant'Andrea, Pistoia, c. 1300, marble, 44 × 45 × 36 cm; kg. 70, Berlin, Skulpturensammlung, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.</figcaption>
</figure>

It is the companion to the eagle lectern now in New York. The pulpit of Sant'Andrea carried two: the lectern for the reading of the Gospels, with the Eagle of Saint John the Evangelist, now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the other lectern, the Angel Pietà, in Berlin. Both were removed around 1619, when the pulpit was moved from its position before the presbytery to meet the liturgical requirements of the Council of Trent, a relocation that also displaced some corner groups of the parapet.

The block is essentially a shaped bracket. Its upper surface is flat, framed by a narrow raised lip, and forms the rest on which the book was laid. The sides taper inward toward the bottom, and the lower edge is cut into a pair of concave curves that spring from a central point — a profile that allowed the block to be seated on a supporting pilaster while presenting a broad, stable reading surface above.

Everything sculptural is therefore contained within a field of some 45 centimetres square. The comparison with the eagle is instructive: that lectern converts the whole support into an image, so that the book rests on a bird. Here the support remains an architectural block and the image is set into its face. Two entirely different solutions to the same problem, made for the same pulpit, probably within a year of one another.

The subject is the **Man of Sorrows** — *Imago Pietatis*, the Byzantine *Akra Tapeinosis* — supported and displayed by two angels.

Christ occupies a shallow rectangular recess at the centre. He is shown half-length, rising from the tomb, the body nude to the waist and the lower part concealed by a drapery drawn across the block. The head has fallen forward onto the chest, the eyes closed, the long hair falling to either side of the face and the beard divided. The arms are crossed at the wrists over the abdomen in the standard gesture of the type, the hands hanging slack. The body is neither living nor laid out for burial: it is dead and upright, sustained rather than resting.

The two angels stand at either side, each in a short belted tunic, wings raised behind. Each lifts one arm high above Christ's head, and between them they hold a cloth that arcs over him in a shallow canopy. The angel at the right reaches down with his free hand to touch or support Christ's forearm; the angel at the left holds his free hand closed at his waist, though damage makes the gesture hard to read.

The image belongs to a devotional type that entered Italy from Byzantium in the later thirteenth century and spread rapidly under Franciscan and Dominican promotion. Its sculptural monumentalisation in the years around 1300 is comparatively rare, and this may be among the earliest such treatments in Italian marble. That claim should be verified before publication.

The eagle lectern carried the Gospel; this one, by the ordinary arrangement of a two-lectern pulpit, would have carried the Epistle. If so, the pairing of image and text is exact: the reader of Paul, whose letters are sustained by the argument from the sacrifice, stood at a desk supported by the image of the dead Christ displayed for contemplation. The Man of Sorrows is not a narrative image but a devotional one — it shows no moment in the Passion, but the fact of the Passion made permanently present — and that is precisely the mode of an epistle rather than of a Gospel narrative.

The angels' function reinforces the reading. They do not mourn; they **present**. The cloth held above Christ's head is a canopy of honour, the gesture with which a relic or the Host is displayed, and the angel who touches his arm steadies rather than grieves. The image asserts an offering made available for veneration at the moment the text was read aloud.

The carving shows the qualities of the Pistoia campaign. The angels' drapery falls in the supple, continuous folds that mark the pulpit's reliefs, with no linear incident to break the surface; the raised arms are deeply undercut and cast their own shadow across the recess. Both angelic faces are broad and calm, in the recovered classicising sweetness quite unlike his father's that characterises Giovanni's Pistoia manner. Against this, the head of Christ is worked with a different intensity — the hair drilled into separate strands, the features slackened, the neck given over entirely to the weight of the fallen head.

The surface has suffered. The block is chipped along all four edges and across the upper lip; the marble is discoloured to a mottled ochre in the recess and along the lower drapery; the left angel's face is abraded and both angels' hands are damaged or broken. The losses are consistent with an object detached from its mounting, stored, and moved more than once between 1619 and its acquisition in Berlin.

### Pergamo of Pisa Cathedral, 1302–1310 (reassembled 1926)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-02.jpg" alt="Circular marble pulpit on porphyry columns and carved figure-supports, its curved parapet densely carved with crowded relief scenes, an eagle lectern projecting at the upper right.">
  <figcaption>Pisa, Cattedrale di Santa Maria Assunta. Pergamo, 1302–1310/11. Carrara marble, porphyry and marmo africano, h. 4.61 m. Dismantled after the fire of 1596 and reassembled in 1926; original location in the presbytery. View from the south-west, showing the convex narrative panels, the caryatid and telamon supports, the stylophorous lions and the eagle lectern.</figcaption>
</figure>

Soon after finishing at Pistoia, Giovanni was charged by the Chapter of Pisa Cathedral with a new and far grander pulpit, carved between 1302 and 1310 and dated, in the Pisan calendar, to December 1311. A contemporary vernacular inscription on the third pillar of the outer south side records: *"In nomine Domini Amen Borghogno di Tado fece fare lo perbio nuovo lo quale è in duomo cominciosi corente ani Domini MCCCII: fu finito in ani Domini corente MCCCXI del mese diiciembre"* — naming Burgundio di Tado (Borgognone di Tado), the works-director (Operaio) under whom the pulpit was made, and giving the dates of beginning and completion. The pulpit replaced the twelfth-century pulpit of Guglielmo, judged inadequate and obsolete and sent to Cagliari Cathedral, a see then dependent on the archbishop of Pisa; in 1312 the older monument was dismantled, carried by sea and reassembled in its new setting, a rare and technically demanding operation for the period.

The date requires a word of caution that bears on the whole bibliography. The *stile pisano* anticipated the common era by one year from 25 March, so an inscription reading December MCCCXI in Pisan reckoning corresponds to December 1310 in modern style.

A second, metrical inscription in Latin, in leonine hexameters, runs in two parts — the first beneath the narrative reliefs, the second along the step below the pulpit. It opens:

> LAUDO DEUM VERUM PER QUEM SUNT OPTIMA RERUM / QUI DEDIT HAS PURAS HOMINEM FORMARE FIGURAS

Vasari transcribed the opening lines and declined to reproduce the remaining thirteen verses, judging them tedious; he read the completion date as 1320, an error compounded by a variant in the numeral verse (*undenis* for *ventenis*), and offered the epigraph less as evidence of the pulpit's beauty than as a specimen of what he took to be the general crudity of the age. His testimony is valuable as a sixteenth-century reading of the monument, and useless as a chronology.

The pergamo stands 4.61 metres high and combines Carrara marble with porphyry and *marmo africano*; its original location was in the presbytery, not the nave arcade it now occupies. The plan is circular, or very nearly so, and this governs everything else. Where the Pistoia parapet is separated into discrete panels by corner figures, the Pisa reliefs press toward a continuous, teeming narrative frieze curving around the whole structure; the enrichment of form, as the scholarship notes, here reaches dimensions never equalled.

Two structural innovations follow from the change of plan. The first is the presence of true caryatids and telamones — figures carved in place of plain columns — carrying various symbolic meanings; the second is the adoption of volute corbels in place of small arches to support the raised floor. To these must be added the extraordinary sense of movement produced by the sheer number of figures filling every void. The supporting apparatus is correspondingly dense: stylophore lions and telamons at the base, columns (some of porphyry) symbolising the sacraments, statues of the theological and cardinal virtues, sibyls, prophets, evangelists, and personifications of the liberal arts, together with figures of Saint Michael and Hercules. Among the caryatids the most celebrated is the group of Pisa–Ecclesia–Caritas, nursing two infants at her breast — city, Church and Charity condensed into a single body that physically bears the weight of the Word preached above.

The effect is a building that has become sculpture. The human figure no longer decorates the architecture; it performs the architecture's work.

#### Narrative programme

The Pisa pulpit is the most complex of the whole Pisano series. Its iconographic richness is unmatched: the nine reliefs of the parapet narrate the history of salvation in some twenty-four scenes, from the *Annunciation of the Birth of the Baptist* to the infernal *Last Judgement*. The panels, slightly curved to follow the roughly circular plan, present the *Annunciation, Visitation and Birth of the Baptist*; the *Nativity and Annunciation to the Shepherds*; the *Journey, Adoration and Dream of the Magi*; the *Presentation in the Temple, the Magi before Herod, and the Flight into Egypt*; the *Massacre of the Innocents*; the *Passion of Christ*; the *Crucifixion*; and the *Last Judgement*, divided between the saved and the damned — the last subject occupying two fields rather than one, which is what brings the count of eight subjects to nine reliefs.

Compression of time within a single field is systematic here, following medieval convention: the three Magi appear simultaneously roused by the angel, searching for the newborn Messiah, and already arrived at the hut of the Holy Family. The beholder cannot read these as a series of discrete tableaux; the ring must be walked, and the narrative delivers itself in a continuous unrolling rather than in framed instalments.

Against the disruptive dynamism of the Pistoia pergamo the language of the Pisa reliefs is somewhat more measured, while the architectural novelties are the more consequential; the dynamic character of the work emerges instead from the great variety of poses among figures and animals and from the typically Gothic presence of elegant curvilinear rhythms. The pathos nonetheless reaches its extremity in the Crucifixion, with the swooning Virgin, an image drawn from Franciscan literary sources.

The monograph on the Pisa pulpit by [Roberto Paolo Novello](/scholars.html#novello-roberto-paolo) — a member of the research group on the signed works of the Italian Middle Ages, and directly concerned with the signature-inscriptions of the Pistoia and Pisa pulpits — is the standard modern study of this work.

#### The dispute of 1304 and the self-defensive inscription

During execution a dispute arose in 1304 between Giovanni and the Operaio Burgundio di Tado, occasioned by payment that did not conform to Giovanni's contract; in July 1307 the supervision of the works passed to Nello Falconi (Nello di Falcone). This friction is traditionally connected with a second, self-defensive inscription on the pulpit, in which Giovanni complained of the hostility and unjust treatment he had suffered — a text whose exact reading has been debated but which is consistent with the proud, aggrieved temper of the man revealed at Pistoia.

It is worth stressing, methodologically, that the modern arrangement of the Pisa pulpit is a reconstruction, and that the sculptor's own words about his sufferings survive on a monument that was for centuries a heap of dispersed fragments. Even so, the projecting head of the Christ Child in the *Nativity*, so vivid as to occur only in Giovanni's best work, has been treated by the scholarship as a principal argument for the master's own hand in the reliefs.

#### Fire, dispersal, reconstruction

The pergamo survived the great fire of 25 October 1596, but during the restoration campaign of 1599–1601 it was dismantled and its pieces distributed to various locations. The dispersal was prolonged, and the monument's members entered the ordinary furniture of the cathedral: in 1626 two stylophorous columns, four statuettes (the Genealogy of Christ and three prophets), the Ecclesia and several corbels were reused in the new cathedral pulpit by Chiarissimo Fancelli, the corbels serving as steps; in 1689 the group of the Evangelists, stripped of its Christ, was pressed into service as the support of a holy-water stoup in the south transept; in 1707 the eagle lectern was placed on the left side of the choir on a base by Giuseppe Bambi. Between the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries further pieces were transferred to the Camposanto, by then understood as a museum of Pisan artistic memory; in 1828–30 the Ecclesia was removed from Fancelli's pulpit, along with the narrative reliefs, for a project of two symmetrical ambones flanking the choir that was subsequently abandoned.

The work was recomposed in the cathedral only in 1926, in its present configuration, which is perhaps not wholly faithful to the original. Because it was taken apart in 1599 and reassembled in 1926, there is no absolute certainty that the carved slabs occupy their correct positions. The sequence of the salvation narrative given above must therefore be presented as the reconstructed order, not as a documented original arrangement — a distinction that matters precisely because the programme's meaning depends on its sequence. Certain elements never returned; some fragments are today in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.

### Marble Beside Fresco: Giovanni Pisano's *Madonna* in the Scrovegni Chapel

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-18.jpg" alt="Marble statue of a crowned and veiled Virgin standing frontally, the Christ Child on her left arm turning his head back toward her and raising a hand to her shoulder; her mantle falls in long vertical folds gathered by a knotted cord, with traces of blue on the lining and gold on the hem and veil. The base is inscribed in Gothic letters. Behind rises a carved tomb with a recumbent effigy.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, c. 1305, marble with polychromy and gilding, 129 x 24 cm, Padua, Cappella degli Scrovegni.</figcaption>
</figure>

In the years of the Pisa Cathedral pulpit campaign, Giovanni supplied for the Scrovegni (Arena) Chapel in Padua a marble group that constitutes a *unicum* in his oeuvre: a *Madonna and Child* flanked symmetrically by two candle-bearing figures, carved in fine-grained Apuan marble, probably from Carrara, and still preserving conspicuous traces of polychromy and gilding that compose motifs of great refinement on the garments.

Enrico Scrovegni<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-scrovegni-enrico" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, the wealthy Paduan banker who had commissioned Giotto to fresco the chapel between about 1303 and 1305, summoned Giovanni to furnish the sculpture for the presbytery — the liturgical heart of the space, which also held Scrovegni's tomb and Giotto's painted Crucifix (now in the Musei Civici agli Eremitani). The group stands today crowning the high altar.

The convergence is exceptional. In the Scrovegni Chapel the painting of Giotto is in dialogue with a sculptural work of equal extraordinariness. Two of the supreme artists of the age worked, if not side by side, then in the same small space and under the same patron.

The Virgin stands frontally, crowned over a veil whose edges retain their gilded borders, the Child carried on her left arm. The mantle is fastened at the breast by a circular clasp and falls in long unbroken vertical folds to the ground, gathered at the waist by a cord that descends in a knotted tassel to the knee — a passage of virtuoso undercutting, the cord standing entirely free of the drapery behind it. The hem is bordered with a wide gilded band, and the mantle's lining preserves extensive traces of the original **blue**, clearly visible down the right side of the figure and along the fall of the drapery.

Her right hand extends forward and open at waist height. The Child, robed and with hair carved in tight curls, turns his head sharply back toward his mother and raises his right hand to her shoulder.

The base of the Virgin carries the inscription in Gothic characters:

> DEO GRATIAS + OPUS / JOH[ANN]IS MAGISTRI NICOLI / DE PISIS,

which attests Giovanni's authorship and, by extension, that of the flanking figures.

The Padua Madonna is among the most tender of Giovanni's realisations of the theme. The head inclines slightly, as if to focus on the child, who returns the gaze with a lively, almost restless joy; it is a dialogue rather than a pose, and in that quality of the gesture caught in an instant, sculpture and painting spoke to one another across the chapel — Giovanni doing in marble what Giotto was doing in colour, restoring a human face to the sacred.

The formulation is worth pressing. The motif is the same one Giovanni had developed in the *Madonna del Colloquio* at Pisa and would carry into the Prato *Madonna della Cintola*, but here it operates within a figure of exceptional stillness. Where the Prato Madonna generates a compressed spiral that discharges in the exchanged glance, the Padua figure is almost columnar: the drapery falls straight, the pose is frontal and static, and all the movement in the group is confined to the two heads. The tenderness is achieved against the grain of the body rather than through it.

The survival of the colour makes this group a rare document of how Giovanni's marbles were meant to be seen — coloured, gilded, and integrated into a total decorative environment.

This is the most transferable point. The Pisano sculptures throughout Tuscany are habitually described, photographed, and reproduced as white marble; the Siena pulpit's recent cleaning recovered colour and gilding beneath centuries of candle smoke, and the Prato Madonna retains substantial gilding on its hem. The Padua group, protected indoors and never dismantled, preserves more than any of them. Any account of the Pisa or Pistoia pulpits' original appearance should be written with this figure in view.

The 2022 ICR campaign studied the sculptures above the altar and assessed their conservation condition, extending its assessment to the Scrovegni funerary monument in the apse and to the statue of the patron in the adjacent sacristy. Recent conservation campaigns, conducted with multispectral and non-destructive investigation and three-dimensional photomodelling, have begun to clarify Giovanni's technique both in the carving of the stone and in the subsequent painting of its surfaces.

### Ministers at the Altar: Giovanni Pisano's Scrovegni Group and Its Two Deacons

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-19.jpg" alt="Three marble figures standing on a ledge above an altar: at the centre a crowned Virgin holding the Christ Child, who turns his head toward her; at either side a wingless young man in a long tunic with a vertical orphrey band, holding a tall candlestick. Behind and above, a recumbent effigy lies beneath a canopy whose curtains are drawn back by winged figures at the corners.">
  <figcaption>Th entire group, c. 1305, marble with polychromy and gilding, Madonna: 129 x 24 cm; left candle-bearing deacon: 124 x 24 cm; right candle-bearing deacon: 126 x 24 cm, Cappella degli Scrovegni, Padua.</figcaption>
</figure>

The two candle-bearing deacons (angeli cerofori) are young men with tightly curled hair, bare-headed, standing frontally. Each wears a long tunic with a broad vertical orphrey running from shoulder to hem, and each holds a tall candlestick upright in one hand at chest height, the fingers of the other steadying the shaft. Gilded shoes appear beneath the hems. Nothing rises behind the shoulders; the backs are clear against the marble.

The vestment is the **dalmatic**, and the office is that of the deacon serving at the altar. The Padua Museums' designation is correct, and the ICR's should be treated as loose usage. The distinction is not pedantic: it moves the group from a celestial register to a liturgical one. These are not angels attending the Queen of Heaven but ministers attending Mass, and their candles are the ones actually lit at the altar over which they stand.

One observation from this image deserves recording, because it is counter-conventional.

The deacons are **not** subordinated in scale. Their heads rise to approximately the height of the Virgin's crown, and they stand on the same level. Medieval convention would normally diminish attendant figures relative to the sacred image; here they are her equals in height, and the Madonna's prominence is secured only by her forward position and central placement.

Two explanations suggest themselves. The first is functional: figures serving as candle-bearers at a real altar must reach a usable height, and their scale answers a liturgical requirement rather than a hierarchical one. The second is that the three statues may not have been conceived for their present positions, and that the equality of height is an accident of a later arrangement. The Padua Museums' phrase — the group is *today* crowning the high altar — leaves this open.

The disposition itself, however, is deliberate at least in its symmetry: the two deacons mirror one another almost exactly, differing only in the hand that holds the candlestick, so that the group reads as a stable triangle with the Virgin at its apex.

The deacons are carved with a broad simplicity quite unlike the nervous surface of the Pistoia reliefs. The drapery falls in long unbroken verticals with minimal incident; the faces are calm, full, and classicising; the hair is the one passage of drilled elaboration. This restraint is consistent with figures designed to be read at a distance in a small, brilliantly frescoed chapel, where fussy carving would compete with Giotto's colour rather than complement it.

Whether the deacons are autograph or workshop remains open. The signature is on the Madonna's base and attests the Madonna; nothing extends it to the flanking figures, and the difference in handling between the Virgin's drapery — with its free-standing knotted cord and its deep folds — and the deacons' plainer surfaces is at least consistent with different hands.

### The Sign Above the Relic: Giovanni Pisano's *Madonna della Cintola* at Prato

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-17.jpg" alt="Small marble statue of a crowned and veiled Virgin standing with the Christ Child held high on her left arm; her mantle is drawn across the body in a long diagonal ending in a broad gilded and fringed hem. The Child turns his head back toward her and reaches one arm up toward her crown.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, known as the Madonna della Cintola, marble with gilding, dated variously between c. 1300 and c. 1315, h. 69 cm. Prato, Cattedrale di Santo Stefano, Cappella del Sacro Cingolo, on the altar containing the relic of the Virgin's Girdle. The Child reaches toward his mother's crown, a gesture read as prefiguring the Coronation. Photographed before the statue's removal to the Opificio delle Pietre Dure, Florence, for restoration; its return to Prato is anticipated for 2027.</figcaption>
</figure>

To Giovanni's late maturity belongs the small and elegant marble *Madonna and Child* long venerated in the Cathedral of Santo Stefano at Prato, set on the altar of the Chapel of the Holy Belt (the Sacra Cintola). The work is bound up with the most important civic-religious cult of Prato: the relic of the Virgin's girdle, which according to tradition Mary let fall to the doubting apostle Thomas at her Assumption, and which was the religious and civic symbol of the city.

The relic was given, by tradition, to the pieve of Prato around 1172, and became — attestedly from at least the second half of the thirteenth century — the fulcrum of Prato's religious life and the ground of the city's claims to political independence from neighbouring Florence and Pistoia. The statue therefore stands at the meeting point of devotion and civic self-assertion, and it should be read in that double register.

The scholarship on the cult and its artistic consequences — assembled most fully in the exhibition and catalogue dedicated to the Prato relic curated by [Cristina Gnoni Mavarelli](/scholars.html#gnoni-mavarelli-cristina) — provides the devotional context for the statue's function as the crowning image of the altar where the relic was displayed.

The Virgin stands, crowned over a veil, the Child carried high on her left arm. The crown is an open circlet of foliate fleurons, and the veil falls in a broad mass behind the shoulder. The mantle is gathered across the body in a single sweeping diagonal that passes beneath the Child and descends to the left, its hem finished with a wide band of gilded ornament — bordered, punched, and fringed — which survives in remarkably good condition and runs the full length of the visible edge.

Her right hand hangs low at her side, holding a small object now hard to identify. The Child, robed and with hair carved into tight drilled curls, turns his body outward while his head turns sharply back toward his mother; his left arm reaches up and behind, toward her crown.

The Virgin is enveloped in an ample mantle whose dense and nervous folds withdraw substance from the figure, creating an ascending motion, almost a compressed spiral, which finds its release and conclusion in the colloquy of glances between Mother and Child — a motif dear to Giovanni Pisano.

That formulation identifies the statue's structural principle exactly. The drapery does not describe a body; it generates a torsion that rises through the figure and is discharged at the top in the meeting of two faces. The *Madonna del Colloquio* in Pisa had discovered the exchanged gaze; here Giovanni makes the whole standing figure a mechanism for delivering it.

The statuette may suggest a *Coronation of the Virgin*. The reading rests on the Child's gesture: held in Mary's arms, he performs the childlike and curious action of touching her crown, prefiguring the future coronation.

The statuette was made around 1300 for the earlier altar of the Cintola, and was conceived perhaps for an isolated position, as the innumerable diversified and dynamic views that characterise it suggest. The point is important and consistent with the treatment of the drapery: a compressed spiral is a form designed to be walked around, not addressed frontally.

The present setting reverses that. The concave silver step creates a space converging on the figure; beyond the Renaissance grille by Maso di Bartolomeo one glimpses, isolated, the rich eighteenth-century altar in precious marbles, with its imposing silver step that seems to form a guard of honour to give prominence at the centre to a marble statue of the Madonna and Child, of contained dimensions but a great masterpiece of Giovanni Pisano. The altar frontal is a bronze relief of the *Dormitio Virginis* by Emilio Greco, executed in 1983. The chapel is entirely frescoed by Agnolo Gaddi with the Stories of the Virgin and of the Girdle.

The consequence is that a figure conceived for multiple viewpoints is now framed, centred, and seen from one. The sculpture is not attached to any prodigious event, unlike other venerated Marian images around which sanctuaries arose, but is nonetheless very dear to the people of Prato, because from the beginning it has constituted the visible element signalling the presence of the relic of the Sacred Girdle, enclosed in the altar below and shown only a few times a year. It is, in the strictest sense, a marker: the sign above the reliquary.

There is no secure documentation for the date of the Prato Madonna, which is ascribable to Giovanni's late activity, probably after the second decade of the fourteenth century and within his lifetime (by about 1315); the movements of the statue within the cathedral, however, attest its association with the altar of the Holy Belt from the outset.

These are not variants of one dating but three distinct placements within Giovanni's career: contemporary with Pistoia, contemporary with the Pisa Cathedral pergamo, or later than both.

The work exercised a wide influence and was taken as a model by leading Italian sculptors for decades.

Its recent removal for conservation and study — the Madonna della Cintola was transferred to the laboratories of the Opificio delle Pietre Dure in Florence, as announced by the institute itself, for restoration under a team led by Tommaso Castaldi, with its return to Prato anticipated for 2027 — was the first time in seven centuries that the statue had left Prato, and it is a reminder that the material study of Giovanni's sculpture is still very much a live enterprise.

The gilded hem visible in the image makes the point concretely. Traces of original polychromy and gilding survive on this figure to a degree unusual among Giovanni's works, and a modern campaign with current analytical methods may establish how much of the surviving gold is medieval, how much is later regilding, and what colour accompanied it. Since the recent cleaning of the Siena pulpit recovered patina, colour and gilding that had been invisible beneath centuries of candle smoke, the Prato campaign may prove similarly consequential — and any account of the statue's original appearance written before its results are published should be marked as provisional.

### Raised, Not Laid Out: Giovanni Pisano's Monument to Margaret of Brabant

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-20.jpg" alt="Black-and-white photograph of three marble figures on a metal display mount: at the centre a woman in a pierced coif and wimple, head tilted back and eyes lifted, rising from a mass of drapery; at either side a figure bent steeply forward, face hidden within a deep hood, arms extended toward her.">
  <figcaption>Elevatio of Margaret of Brabant, white Carrara marble, c. 1313. From the funerary monument commissioned by Henry VII for San Francesco di Castelletto, Genoa; dismembered before 1602, dispersed at the demolition of the church in the early nineteenth century, the "Elevatio corporis" rediscovered in 1874. Genoa, Museo di Sant'Agostino. H. c. 150 cm. Photographed on a modern metal mount by Paolo Monti (BEIC 6356347), before the restoration completed at the Opificio delle Pietre Dure in 2024.</figcaption>
</figure>

Giovanni's last great commission was a funerary monument, the only essay in tomb sculpture to survive from his hand. Margaret of Brabant<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-margaret-of-brabant" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>, wife of the Holy Roman Emperor Henry VII, died in Genoa on the night of 13 December 1311, aged thirty-six. Her grieving husband commissioned from Giovanni, the most important sculptor of the day, a monument for the Franciscan church of San Francesco di Castelletto, executed in white Carrara marble between 1312 and 1313 and originally installed in the apse. Later, toward the end of the sixteenth century, the tomb was dismembered and reused within the same church; with the demolition of the building between 1798 and 1810 the monument was reduced to the fragmentary state in which it survives.

What remains — now held in the Museo di Sant'Agostino in Genoa — includes a celebrated group representing the empress raised toward heaven by two attendant figures (the *elevatio*, the raising of the queen's body or soul), together with a Madonna and personifications of Justice and Temperance (the latter surviving only as a head). The fragments pose acute problems of reconstruction and interpretation, and no contemporary description of the original appearance is known.

The image, a Paolo Monti studio negative, records the three figures mounted on separate pins on a hexagonal metal table — a modern conjecture about their relation, not a documented disposition.

Margaret rises at the centre, half-length, in a pierced openwork coif over a wimple, the head tilted back, the lips parted, the eyes lifted. Below the waist the body dissolves into drapery: she is not standing but being lifted. The two attendants bend steeply inward from either side, faces entirely lost within deep hoods, arms extended to grasp her. The composition is built as a rising diagonal across three separate blocks — two downward pressures converging on a single upward axis.

From these fragments emerges a new and moving conception of the sepulchre, in which the queen turns to heaven an intense gaze full of hope, without precedent in art except in the verses of Dante's *Commedia*. The comparison is the museum's own and it is exact: what is represented is not the body's condition but its expectation.

#### Reconstruction: the historiographical sequence

| Stage | Position |
| --- | --- |
| 1874 | Rediscovery of the *Elevatio corporis*. |

| 2024 | Return from the Opificio delle Pietre Dure and reunion with the *Justice*. |

The tomb has been read as a unified programme illustrating the ascent of Margaret's soul immediately after death, and it introduced an innovative, multi-level, wall-mounted design that marked a decisive step toward greater monumentality in Italian Gothic funerary sculpture — a type whose development would run through the tombs of Tino di Camaino and beyond.

The scholarly reinstallation of related Genoese medieval sculpture, and the partial reopening of the Museo di Sant'Agostino where the Margaret fragments are held, have been served in recent years by the work of [Francesca Girelli](/scholars.html#girelli-francesca), co-author of a 2020 monograph on Giovanni Pisano and a specialist in the reuse, reworking, and re-display of medieval sculpture; the older authority on medieval art in Genoa is [Colette Dufour Bozzo](/scholars.html#dufour-bozzo-colette). The funerary and devotional dimensions of such patronage have been illuminated more broadly by the work of Michele Bacci on artistic patronage and the medieval theology of the afterlife.

In this late work the violent plasticism characteristic of Giovanni's earlier sculpture gives way to a broader, calmer modelling; the *elevatio* group of Margaret of Brabant is today among the most famous of all medieval sculptures, and it is a fitting final statement of Giovanni's art — grief, transcendence, and formal daring fused in fragments.

### *Sub Petri Cura*: Giovanni Pisano's Lunette Group for the Pisa Baptistery

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-21.jpg" alt="Three weathered marble figures standing in a shallow vaulted niche: at the left a gaunt bearded man in a shaggy fleece garment rests his hand on the head of a small kneeling figure with joined hands; at the centre a crowned and veiled woman on an inscribed plinth holds a headless child on her left arm; at the right a youthful figure holds a book against his chest.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child between St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist, with the donor Pietro, marble, 1300-06. From the lunette of the main portal of the Baptistery of San Giovanni, Pisa; now Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. The Baptist presents the kneeling donor at the left; the Evangelist holds his book at the right; the Madonna's base carries the inscription naming Peter as supervisor and Giovanni, son of Nicola, as sculptor.</figcaption>
</figure>

While he was engaged on the pulpit for the cathedral, Giovanni Pisano returned to work for the Baptistery. The Madonna and Child originally stood high in the lunette of the main door of the Pisan Baptistery, in the company of three further figures: the two Saint Johns — the Baptist and the Evangelist — and the smaller kneeling donor, Peter.

The group therefore stood on the exterior of the building, above the principal entrance, and formed the sculptural climax of the west portal. It is the third distinct campaign the Pisani conducted on that building: Nicola's pulpit within, completed in 1259; the crowning of the upper exterior zone with pierced gabled tabernacles and colossal half-figures in the 1270s and 1280s; and this lunette group, executed while Giovanni was simultaneously directing the cathedral pergamo of 1302–1310.

The base of the Madonna carries an incised inscription in two lines, legible in the image. It records both the supervisor of the work and the sculptor, and names the kneeling donor whose figure appears at the Baptist's feet.

The text reads:

> SVB PETRI CVRA FVIT HEC PIA [SCVLPTA FIGVRA] / NICOLI NAT[O] SCVLTORE IOHA[N]E VOCAT[O]

— "Under Peter's supervision this pious figure was carved, the sculptor being called Giovanni, son of Nicola."

The formula is characteristic. Giovanni names himself, as at Pistoia and Pisa, in the possessive relation to his father — *Nicoli nato*, son of Nicola — and simultaneously as an independent master. The rhyme scheme is leonine, as in all his signatures.

#### The figures

- St John the Baptist stands at the left, bearded and gaunt, wearing the camel-hair garment rendered as a dense mass of drilled tufts over a short mantle. His right hand rests on the head of the kneeling donor beside him — the gesture of presentation, by which the patron is commended to the Virgin. The Baptist is the Baptistery's titular saint, and his primacy in the group is proper to the building.

- The donor, Pietro, kneels at the Baptist's feet, hands joined, at markedly reduced scale. He is presumably the *operaio* or supervisor named in the inscription. His inclusion at this date is notable: a named lay figure kneeling within a monumental portal group is not yet common in Italian sculpture, and the group belongs to the early history of the donor portrait in that setting.

- The Virgin stands at the centre, crowned over a veil, on a separate inscribed plinth. The Child, now headless and heavily damaged, is carried on her left arm. Her mantle is gathered high across the body and falls in a broad cascade to the left, the drapery deeply cut but the whole figure comparatively flat in section.

- St John the Evangelist stands at the right, youthful, holding a book against his chest with both hands. The surface here is the most eroded of the four.

The Madonna, the most monumental of those created by Giovanni, having to find a place within a lunette of limited depth, is in reality little more than a high relief, and its plastic and constructive events unfold almost exclusively according to a frontal view.

This is the most important formal point, and it inverts what one might expect from the same years. At Siena, Giovanni had detached his figures entirely from the wall, so that they stand free and rotate; at Prato, the Madonna is a compressed spiral designed to be walked around. Here the architecture imposes the opposite constraint. A lunette is shallow, and the figures had to be legible from directly in front and from below.

The figure accordingly takes on a movement full of energy from the relation between the decisive — the passage breaks off, but the direction is clear: the vitality is generated within the frontal plane rather than through it, by opposition of masses rather than by torsion of the body.

The four figures are consequently designed as a frontal frieze, not as a group in the round. What the museum's present arrangement in a shallow vaulted niche preserves is at least the frontality; what it cannot preserve is the height and the outdoor light.

Six centuries in the open air have taken a heavy toll. The Child has lost his head and much of his body; the Evangelist's surface is pitted and eroded across the drapery; the Virgin's face survives comparatively well but the hands are damaged. The Baptist has fared best, his fleece garment retaining its drilled texture.

### Twenty Centimetres of Agony: Giovanni Pisano's Ivory Christ at the Musée de Cluny

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-22.jpg" alt="Small ivory figure of the crucified Christ, gold in colour with the head falling onto the right shoulder with eyes open beneath contracted brows, long striated hair framing a curled beard; the torso is attenuated with the ribcage sharply articulated, and a knotted loincloth falls in deep folds. The arms are broken away at the shoulders and the legs below the calf.">
  <figcaption>Christ crucifié, elephant ivory with traces of gilding, Tuscany, 1270–1280, 20.5 x 6.3 cm. Made for a cross now lost; arms and lower legs missing. Paris, Musée de Cluny – Musée national du Moyen Âge, acquired 2023 as a work of major heritage interest, the first sculpture by Giovanni Pisano to enter a French public collection.</figcaption>
</figure>

The figure survives from the crown of the head to just below the knees; the arms are lost at the shoulders, the legs below the calf, and the cross itself entirely.

The Christ is shown in agony, the eyes still open beneath brows contracted with suffering; in an attitude of resignation and abandonment, the head inclines onto his right shoulder. This detail is the iconographic key. A dead Christ has closed eyes; a triumphant Christ stands upright and open-eyed. Here the eyes are open and the body has already given way — the figure is caught at the moment of dying rather than after it, and the ambiguity is deliberate.

The hair falls in a heavy mass of long parallel striations to the shoulders, framing the face; the beard is worked in tight drilled curls. The torso is markedly attenuated, the ribcage articulated in a hard arc, the abdomen drawn in beneath a taut diaphragm, the flank showing the wound. The loincloth is knotted at the hip and falls in deep, agitated folds, its lower edge breaking into pointed cascades.

The surface bears a network of fine vertical striations across the chest and torso. These are the natural cracks of aged ivory following the grain of the tusk, not tooling, and should not be described as carved detail.

The Cluny record notes traces of gilding, and the V&A's comparable figure gives a fuller picture: that figure was originally partly painted on the loincloth and gilded on the hair and beard. If the same scheme applied here — and the surviving traces suggest it may — the object as first seen was a gilded head and beard above a coloured cloth, against the wood or metal of a cross. The present monochrome ivory is not what a thirteenth-century beholder saw.

It's worth noticing that the attribution to Giovanni Pisano rests on quality and style, not on documents. The power of expression in the face, the delicacy of the anatomical rendering, and the virtuoso treatment of the ivory are the qualities that invite the attribution.

This small statuette is rightly considered a major milestone in the history of medieval art, with this Crucified Christ bearing witness to the influence of French Gothic art on Italian artists. It testifies to the way in which French Gothic art was received in Tuscany.

Moreover, what is French about the figure is the pathos: the *Christus patiens*, the sagging body, the head fallen to the shoulder, the loincloth broken into restless folds. What is not French is the anatomical seriousness — the ribcage and abdomen are constructed with an understanding of the body's mechanics that derives from the antique, and that Giovanni had from his father and from the Camposanto sarcophagi. The ivory is the meeting point of the two currents that define his art, compressed into twenty centimetres.

Its interest for the corpus is also material. Giovanni worked throughout his career on numerous commissions in marble, wood, stone, gold and ivory for prestigious patrons, and several wooden crucifixes by his hand are today preserved in the cathedral museums of Pisa and Siena. The Cluny Christ therefore belongs beside the wooden crucifixes rather than beside the pulpits: a class of small and portable devotional objects that is systematically underrepresented in accounts of his work, and that this acquisition brings back into view.

### Fifteen Centimetres of Passion: Giovanni Pisano's Ivory Christ in London

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-23.jpg" alt="Small ivory figure of the crucified Christ photographed against a grey ground: the head falls forward and to one side with eyes closed, long waved hair and a divided beard retaining traces of gilding; the torso is carved with sharply defined clavicles, ribs and abdominal channel; a loincloth knotted at the hip falls in broad planes broken by angular projecting folds. The arms are broken away at the shoulders and the legs below the knee.">
  <figcaption>The Crucified Christ, carved elephant ivory with traces of gilding, 1290–1310. 15.3 x 6 x 5 cm. London, Victoria and Albert Museum.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a celebrated small masterpiece of sublime quality. The attribution is one of association rather than of document, and the museum sets out its reasoning with unusual candour.

Giovanni Pisano is known to have carved a great ivory Virgin and Child in 1298, now in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Pisa, and scenes depicting the Passion of Christ belonged to the ensemble of which it formed a part. Although it is unlikely that this crucifix figure was made for that Pisan commission, its style and superb quality strongly suggest that the same sculptor made it. It is also closely comparable to figures on the pulpit that Giovanni Pisano made for Pisa Cathedral, which dates from the years 1302–1310.

The argument therefore runs: a documented ivory Passion ensemble by Giovanni existed; this figure is not from it; but its quality and its resemblance to the Pisa pergamo figures point to the same hand. The dating of 1290–1310 follows from that comparison, not from any independent evidence.

#### The relation to the Cluny ivory

The two ivories must now be treated together, and the entry on either is incomplete without the other.

| | Musée de Cluny | Victoria and Albert Museum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Height | 20.5–21.5 cm | 15.3 cm |
| Date given | c. 1270–1280 | 1290–1310 |

| Condition | Arms and lower legs lost, cross lost | Arms lost, cross lost; assembled from three parts |
| Acquired | 2023, by private treaty | Long in the collection |

The divergence in dating is a generation, and it cannot both be right if the two figures are by the same hand at the same moment. Three possibilities: the Cluny date is too early; the V&A date is too late; or the two ivories are genuinely separated by twenty years, which would mean Giovanni returned to the type across his career.

The difference in scale is also worth noting. At 15.3 cm the V&A figure is markedly smaller, which affects the plausible size of the lost cross and thus the kind of object each belonged to — a portable devotional crucifix in one case, possibly an altar cross in the other.

The figure survives from the crown of the head to just below the knees, the arms broken at the shoulders and the legs at the calf.

The head falls forward and to Christ's right, the chin nearly touching the collarbone, the eyes closed. The hair falls in long, deeply cut waves that sweep back from the brow and descend in heavy locks across both shoulders; the beard is divided and worked in the same continuous rippling strands. The gilding survives most conspicuously here — the darker warm tone across the hair and beard is the residue of the original gold.

The torso is treated with an anatomical seriousness that repays close attention. The clavicles are sharply cut; the ribcage is indicated by shallow parallel ridges beneath the pectorals; the sternum and the linea alba are traced as a continuous vertical channel down the abdomen; the navel is deeply drilled. The flanks are drawn in and the iliac crests project. This is not a schematic body but an observed one, and the observation is of a body under load — the ribs raised by the arms' suspension, the abdomen hollowed.

The loincloth is knotted at the right hip and swathes the pelvis in broad planes broken by sharp angular folds, several of which project as free-standing wedges. The lower edge falls into pointed cascades on both sides. The contrast between the smooth continuous surfaces of the flesh and the faceted, crystalline treatment of the cloth is the figure's principal formal effect.

A fine network of vertical cracks runs through the torso and drapery. These follow the grain of the tusk and are the natural ageing of the material, not tooling.

The figure is a *Christus patiens* of the developed Gothic type: dead rather than dying, the eyes closed, the head fallen, the body sagging from the arms. This distinguishes it from the Cluny ivory, where the eyes are still open beneath brows contracted with suffering — a difference of moment within the Passion, and one that may bear on the dating question, since the movement of the iconography across the later thirteenth century runs broadly from the dying to the dead Christ. It was originally partially painted on the loincloth and gilded on the hair and beard. The original appearance was therefore a gilded head above a coloured cloth, with the flesh left as polished ivory — a deliberate chromatic hierarchy, and one entirely lost in the present monochrome state.

Giovanni worked throughout his career in marble, wood, stone, gold and ivory, and several wooden crucifixes by his hand survive in the cathedral museums of Pisa and Siena. The two ivory Christs belong with those rather than with the pulpits: small, portable, made for private or chapel devotion rather than for public liturgy.

This class of work is systematically underweighted in accounts of Giovanni, which are organised around the four great commissions. Yet the ivories show him solving a different problem — how to carry the full pathos of a monumental Crucifixion at fifteen centimetres, in a material whose curvature dictates the pose and whose scale forbids undercutting. The answer here is concentration: everything the Pistoia *Crucifixion* achieves through a field of forty figures is compressed into one body and one fall of cloth.

### Behind the Grille: The Crocifisso d'Elci and Two Centuries of Invisibility

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-25.jpg" alt="Polychrome wooden crucifix on a white wall: the emaciated body of Christ hangs with arms stretched steeply upward, head fallen forward and to one side with eyes closed, dark waved hair and divided beard; the loincloth is gilded with a green border, and the cream flesh shows extensive paint loss exposing a brown ground. The plain cross carries a red tablet lettered INRI, and the shaft ends in a metal socket.">
  <figcaption>Crocifisso d'Elci, 1290–1299, polychrome wood, 87 x 47,5 cm, Pisa, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Crocifisso d'Elci is among the least studied objects in Giovanni's corpus, and the reason is documented rather than art-historical. The crucifix has always been scantly considered by the historiography — and the displacement history explains why.

The sources attest that it was kept first on the pulpit; subsequently, between 1569 and 1678, on the altar of the Santissimo Sacramento; and finally it is documented for more than two centuries, from 1742 until 1986, within a niche above the tomb of Archbishop Francesco Pannocchieschi d'Elci, where a dense grille precluded any view of it. It is precisely from this placement that the name "Crocifisso d'Elci" derives.

The object was therefore effectively invisible for two hundred and forty-four years. Scholars could not see it, and the literature developed without it. Since 1986 the work has been exhibited in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo at Pisa, in the Sala del Tesoro, having been restored for the occasion by the Soprintendenza di Pisa.

This is a case where the state of the scholarship is a function of physical access rather than of the object's quality, and the entry should say so plainly. Any assessment of the Crocifisso d'Elci written before 1986 is an assessment of something the writer had not examined.

The corpus is carved in wood and polychromed. The head has fallen forward and to Christ's right, the eyes closed, the mouth slightly open beneath a divided beard; the hair falls in dark waves to the shoulders. The body is markedly attenuated: the ribcage is articulated in shallow ridges, the abdomen drawn in, the flanks hollowed, the arms stretched to a steep angle by the suspension of the weight.

The loincloth is knotted at the left hip and swathes the pelvis, its lower edge falling in an angular cascade; it is gilded with a green-blue border. The legs descend nearly parallel with the feet set one above the other.

The flesh reads as a flat cream-white over which extensive losses expose a warm brown ground; the gilded loincloth with its coloured border has the character of a later campaign rather than a Duecento one. The ICCD's technique field describes the work as *legno intagliato con resa policroma* without specifying date.

The cross and mount are not original: the plain squared members, the painted INRI tablet, and the modern metal socket at the foot of the shaft belong to the museum installation or to an earlier remounting. The corpus alone is the medieval object.

The Crocifisso d'Elci belongs to a class of Giovanni's work that the standard accounts systematically underweight. He worked throughout his career in marble, wood, stone, gold and ivory, and several wooden crucifixes by his hand are preserved in the cathedral museums of Pisa and Siena. Alongside the ivory Christs in Paris and London, these wooden corpora constitute a body of Passion sculpture in soft and small-scale materials running in parallel to the four great pulpit and façade commissions.

The comparison with the ivories is instructive. At 87 cm this is roughly four times the height of the Cluny figure and nearly six times the London one, but the iconographic and formal choices are the same: the dead or dying Christ with head fallen to the right, the attenuated torso, the knotted loincloth breaking into angular folds. Giovanni appears to have carried a single conception of the crucified body across three materials and at least three scales.

### The Cross That Lives: Giovanni Pisano's Wooden Crucifix at Siena

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-26.jpg" alt="Polychromed wooden crucifix against a pale ground: the body of Christ hangs with arms stretched steeply upward, head fallen onto the right shoulder with eyes closed, long dark hair and a divided beard; the loincloth is reddish-brown with gilded edges. The cross is carved as a dark tree trunk with the stubs of cut branches, rising from a rocky mound at whose foot lies a skull, with a red titulus lettered in gold above the head.">
  <figcaption>Crucifix, c. 1285, polychromed and gilded wood, h.: 114 cm (Cross); 71 cm (Christ), Siena, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The dating of c. 1285 places this crucifix precisely at the opening of Giovanni's Sienese decade. In 1284 the commune had granted him citizenship with lifelong exemption from communal burdens, taxes, exactions and military service; from 1285, following Nicola's death, he took over the enterprise of the cathedral façade; and he is named capomaestro in a document of 3 April 1287.

The crucifix therefore belongs to the same years as the earliest work on the façade statues, and it now stands in the same museum as those figures. The juxtaposition is instructive: the prophets were carved over-lifesize for viewing from the piazza far below, with every proportion adjusted for that sightline; this corpus is seventy-one centimetres high and was made to be looked at closely. Giovanni was working at both extremes of scale simultaneously.

Christ hangs with the arms stretched to a steep angle, the shoulders drawn up by the suspension of the weight. The head has fallen forward and onto the right shoulder, the eyes closed, the mouth slightly open; long dark hair falls in heavy strands across both shoulders and the beard is divided. The torso is markedly attenuated — the ribcage articulated, the abdomen hollowed beneath a taut diaphragm, the flanks drawn in.

The loincloth is knotted at the left hip and wraps the pelvis in a broad diagonal sweep, its lower hem falling at an angle across the thighs; it is painted in a deep reddish-brown with gilding at the edges, and the gold is the only chromatic accent against otherwise pale flesh and a dark cross.

The legs descend with the knees slightly flexed and the feet placed one above the other on the shaft — the three-nail Gothic arrangement rather than the four-nail type of the older tradition. This should be verified from the object, since the angle of the photograph makes the reading probable rather than certain.

The hands are broken or eroded at the fingers; losses are visible on the right knee and along the shins.

The cross is not a squared beam but a tree: a dark trunk with the stubs of lopped branches projecting at intervals along the shaft and the arms, rising from a rocky mound at the foot of which lies a skull. A red titulus with gilded letters is fixed above the head.

This is the *lignum vitae*, the cross as the living Tree of Life — an image of considerable currency in the Franciscan milieu of the later thirteenth century, and one given systematic form by Bonaventure<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-bonaventure" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>'s *Lignum vitae* of the 1260s. The theology is compressed: the instrument of death is shown as a tree that lives, and it stands over the skull of Adam, whose fall the second tree undoes.

- Two cautions

1. The branch-cross type is genuine to the period, but the glossy dark finish, the naturalistic bark, and the rocky Golgotha base at the foot all have the character of a later campaign; the corpus may be Giovanni's and the cross a subsequent replacement.

2. The given dimensions imply an unusually tall cross relative to the corpus, and the excess is largely in the shaft below the feet. That is consistent with a processional or altar mounting, and possibly with a later extension.

In describing the Siena Cathedral pulpit *Crucifixion*, one source states that the cross there is formed of tree branches. If that is correct, and if the present cross is original, then the *lignum vitae* appears twice in the Sienese work of the Pisano workshop within twenty years — once in Nicola's marble relief of 1265–68 and once in this wooden crucifix of c. 1285.

Giovanni worked throughout his career in marble, wood, stone, gold and ivory, and several wooden crucifixes by his hand survive in the cathedral museums of Pisa and Siena. This example and the Crocifisso d'Elci at Pisa are two of them, and the entries should be read together.

| | Siena | Pisa (Crocifisso d'Elci) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date | c. 1285 | 1290–1299 |
| Height of corpus | 71 cm | 87 cm |
| Cross | Branch-form, with skull | Plain, later |
| Polychromy | Painted and gilded; reddish loincloth | Cream flesh, gilded loincloth; date uncertain |

Set beside the ivory Christs in Paris and London, these four objects show Giovanni returning to the crucified body across some four decades, in three materials and at scales from fifteen to eighty-seven centimetres, with a consistent conception: head fallen to the right, attenuated torso, knotted loincloth breaking into angular folds. The pulpits are where the reputation rests; the crucifixes are where the idea was worked out repeatedly and privately.

### The Twisting Body: Giovanni Pisano's Crucifix from San Cerbone

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Giovanni-Pisano/giovanni-pisano-27.jpg" alt="Painted wooden crucifix set in a shallow white niche on a modern conical base: the body of Christ hangs with arms stretched taut, head fallen forward, the chest pulled upward and the abdomen hollowed; blood runs from the wound in the right side; the knees bend sharply so that the legs swing to one side, the feet set one above the other. The cross is a dark stripped trunk with evenly spaced branch-stubs along shaft and arms.">
  <figcaption>Crucifix, carved and painted wood, c. 1300. H. 88 cm. From the Cathedral of San Cerbone; Massa Marittima, Museo di Arte Sacra.</figcaption>
</figure>

The crucifix comes from the cathedral of San Cerbone at Massa Marittima and is now displayed in the town's Museo di Arte Sacra, mounted on a modern conical base within a shallow arched niche.

Massa Marittima lies in the Maremma, within the Sienese sphere of influence, and a commission there around 1300 falls between Giovanni's departure from Siena in about 1296–97 and the Pistoia pulpit of 1298–1301. The work therefore belongs to a moment when he was moving between centres, and it raises a question the entry should pose rather than answer: whether the crucifix was carved in Siena or Pisa and sent, or whether Giovanni was present in Massa Marittima. The cathedral of San Cerbone had an active thirteenth-century sculptural programme, and the question is not idle.

At 88 cm the figure stands close in scale to the Crocifisso d'Elci at Pisa and well above the Siena crucifix of c. 1285. It is the most anatomically explicit of the three.

Christ hangs with the arms stretched taut and nearly straight, the shoulders drawn high by the load. The head has fallen forward and to his right, the face largely lost to abrasion, the hair carved in long parallel waves that sweep back from the crown and fall across the shoulders. The chest and abdomen are worked with an insistence on the mechanics of suspension: the pectorals are pulled upward and outward, the ribcage rises in a hard arc, the abdomen is drawn into a deep hollow beneath the diaphragm, and the flanks are sharply indented above the hips.

The wound in the right side is carved and painted, with a stream of red running down the flank toward the loincloth — one of the few passages where the original narrative polychromy survives legibly. Traces of red also appear at the feet.

The legs are the panel's most striking departure. They do not descend in parallel but bend sharply at the knees and swing to Christ's right, the body twisting so that the hips turn away from the plane of the shoulders. The feet are set one above the other on the shaft — the three-nail Gothic type. The result is a figure in torsion rather than in suspension alone, and it distinguishes this crucifix decisively from the more frontal and columnar corpora at Siena and Pisa.

The loincloth is short, knotted at the right hip with a loose end falling free, and its lower hem cuts across the upper thighs at a sharp diagonal. It is painted white with a blue-grey band at the upper edge and traces of rose and gilding in the folds — a scheme quite unlike the reddish-brown of the Siena loincloth or the gold of the Pisan one.

The surface has suffered severely. The flesh reads as a mottled ochre where the original paint survives, interrupted by extensive losses that expose the darker ground and bare wood; the face is heavily abraded and the features barely legible; the fingers of both hands are damaged.

The visible polychromy appears to be substantially original rather than a later campaign — the blood, the flesh tones, and the loincloth are of a piece, and the pattern of loss is that of a single aged layer rather than of overpaint flaking from an earlier surface. This should be confirmed against a conservation report before being asserted; but if it holds, the Massa Marittima crucifix is the best surviving witness among Giovanni's wooden Christs to what such an object originally looked like.

The cross is rendered as a **stripped trunk with the stubs of lopped branches** projecting in regular pairs along both the shaft and the arms, painted a uniform dark brown-black. The ends of the arms are cut square and the shaft continues well below the feet into the modern socket.

This is the same *lignum vitae* type as the Siena crucifix — the cross as living tree — but treated with a marked difference of handling. At Siena the trunk is naturalistic, irregular, with a rocky Golgotha and the skull of Adam at its foot; here the branch-stubs are regularised into an almost decorative rhythm, evenly spaced and uniform in size, and there is no mound and no skull.

Three now stand together in these entries, and the comparison begins to be productive:

| | Siena | Massa Marittima | Pisa (d'Elci) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Date | c. 1285 | c. 1300 | 1290–1299 |
| Height of corpus | 71 cm | 88 cm | 87 cm |
| Cross | Branch-form, naturalistic, with skull | Branch-form, regularised | Plain, later |
| Loincloth | Reddish-brown, gilt edges | White with blue band | Gilded, green border |
| Legs | Near-parallel | Sharply bent and twisted | Near-parallel |

The Massa Marittima figure is the outlier in pose, and it is also the latest. The torsion of the lower body corresponds to what Giovanni was doing at Pistoia in exactly these years, where figures are carved at varying depths and break beyond their frames. It may be that the wooden crucifixes track the same development as the marbles, and that this one belongs with the Pistoia campaign in conception as well as in date.

## The Uncertain End (c. 1314 – c. 1318/1319)

The circumstances of Giovanni's death are among the many uncertainties of his biography. The last secure documentary reference places him in Siena in 1314; the precise content of that record has not been fully clarified in the accessible literature and should be treated with caution. Encyclopedic tradition variously gives his death as "c. 1314," "after 1314," or "c. 1315," while the most precise scholarly reconstruction — that of Ascani — holds that he died in Siena at the end of 1318 and that in 1319 his name was struck from the city's revenue registers, and that he was buried at the base of the cathedral façade whose statues had been his greatest civic achievement. The place of death (Siena) is secure; the range c. 1315–1319 given in the present title reflects this well-supported uncertainty, and the reader should understand any single date as provisional.

One further work must be mentioned here only to be set aside. The tomb of the Dominican Pope Benedict XI<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedict-xi" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> (died in Perugia, 7 July 1304) in San Domenico, Perugia, was long attributed to Giovanni Pisano on the authority of Vasari. This attribution is now largely rejected. Current scholarship favours a later dating, into the late 1320s, and an attribution to the circle of Lorenzo Maitani (Lorenzo or his relation Ambrogio, documented in Perugia in these years), the monument being modelled on Arnolfo di Cambio's De Braye tomb in San Domenico at Orvieto; the Fondazione Zeri now files it under Lorenzo Maitani. It is an instructive case of a Vasarian tradition dissolved by modern documentary and stylistic study, and a caution against accepting the older attributions uncritically. Some specialists retain an earlier dating, so that the question cannot be regarded as entirely closed.

## Style, Workshop, and the Problem of the Autograph

Any account of Giovanni's art must reckon with the fact that much of it was produced by a workshop, and that the "autograph" is as much a critical construction as an observable fact. The method of Nicola's shop, described above, in which pupils executed figures to the master's design and the master then unified the whole, was carried over by Giovanni, and it accounts for the difficulty of attribution that has bedevilled criticism from [Cavalcaselle](/scholars.html#cavalcaselle-giovanni-battista) and [Crowe](/scholars.html#crowe-joseph-archer) onward. At Perugia the hands are nearly impossible to separate; at Siena the façade statuary includes work of collaborators; at Pisa the vast pulpit necessarily involved assistants. The Pistoia pulpit and the Padua and Genoa works are those in which Giovanni's own hand is most fully present, and it is on these that judgements of his personal style must principally rest.

The study of how such workshops were organised — how artists learned their craft, how they related to their guilds and patrons, and where their work was displayed — has been advanced for the Italian tradition by the work of [Bruce Cole](/scholars.html#cole-bruce), whose account of the artist at work from the Pisani onward provides the general frame for understanding Giovanni's practice; while the interpretation of Italian Gothic sculpture as possessing its own coherent visual language, rather than being measured against French or Renaissance yardsticks, is the contribution of Anita Fiderer Moskowitz.

Within this frame, the recurring diagnostic signs of Giovanni's own hand can be specified: the vividly projecting heads, especially of the Christ Child, whose liveliness the standard account treats as a principal argument for autograph status; the rapid passage of planes and the very high relief with pierced, à jour passages; the fluid linearism serving a strong expressivity; and the calculation of figures for a specific, often low, viewpoint.

The signatures themselves — proud, defensive, insistent on his Pisan birth and his superiority even to his father — are a further, documentary, sign of the personality behind the marble, and they distinguish Giovanni sharply from the anonymity of most of his contemporaries. Seidel's continuity thesis bears directly on the autograph question: if father and son are understood as engaged in a lifelong artistic dialogue rather than divided by a stylistic abyss, then the search for a sharp line between "Nicola" and "Giovanni" passages in the shared works is itself partly misconceived, and the emphasis falls instead on a developing, shared language within which Giovanni's distinct voice gradually emerges.

## Reception and Critical Fortune, from Vasari to the Present

The history of Giovanni's reputation is a subject in its own right, and it is best approached through the framework that Giovanni Previtali established in *La fortuna dei primitivi* for thinking about the reception of medieval art as itself a historical phenomenon shaped by the taste and ideology of successive ages. Giovanni's fortune is a textbook case of eclipse and rediscovery.

The earliest witness is Lorenzo Ghiberti, whose *Commentarii* (c. 1450), the first autobiography by an artist, mention Giovanni only briefly. Ghiberti names him as "figliuolo di maestro Nichola" and records, in a compressed notice, that "Maestro Giovanni fece il pergamo di Pisa di sua mano, il pergamo di Siena, e 'l pergamo di Pistoja" together with the fountain of Perugia. The notice is genuine but slight; Ghiberti's real interest lay in the Florentine painters descending from Giotto and in the Sienese masters, and Giovanni is little more than a name to him.

The decisive early-modern source is Giorgio Vasari, whose Life of Giovanni in the *Vite* (1550; 1568) must be used with great caution. Vasari is notoriously careless with dates and places and partial to Florence; in the first edition he even introduced Giovanni as a pupil of the much younger Andrea Pisano, and he attached to Giovanni's name works and dates that modern scholarship treats with reserve or rejects — the Benedict XI tomb among them. Yet Vasari also preserves genuinely useful information: the substance of inscriptions he reports, the date of 1297 for the Pistoia commission, and the memory of the original placement of the Pisa pulpit near the choir, testimony that proved valuable when the pulpit came to be reconstructed. Vasari's fundamental limitation was ideological: sharing his age's contempt for the "barbarous" Middle Ages, he could value Nicola for his supposed dependence on antique sarcophagi but had no framework within which Giovanni's Gothic pathos could be understood as anything but a stage on the road to better things.

  <figcaption>First page of Vasari's "Vite" related to Nicola and Giovanni Pisano.</figcaption>
</figure>

An important and often-overlooked figure in the recovery of the Pisani is the eighteenth-century Franciscan friar and art writer [Guglielmo Della Valle](/scholars.html#della-valle-guglielmo), whose *Lettere sanesi sopra le belle arti* (1782–1786) advanced the controversial thesis that the Sienese school was older and more autonomous than the Florentine, and several of whose insights proved remarkably prescient. Della Valle recognised the influence of Giunta Pisano on Cimabue and, crucially for the present subject, the influence of Nicola and Giovanni Pisano on Giotto, emphasising too the Roman component in Giotto's formation. This recognition — that the sculptural revolution of the Pisani stood behind the pictorial revolution of Giotto — anticipated a central theme of modern scholarship, and it deserves explicit acknowledgement in any reception history.

The most consequential rediscovery, however, came from outside Italy and from artists rather than scholars. Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the Pisa Cathedral pulpit lay dismembered, its fragments collected in the Camposanto by its conservator Carlo Lasinio in the early nineteenth century and awaiting a decision on their fate. It was foreign travellers, amateurs, and artists who first grasped their modernity. Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ingres-jean-auguste-dominique" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a> drew elements of the pulpit; Paul Delaroche<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-delaroche-paul" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a> included the sculptor — Giovanni, not his father — in the hemicycle mural of the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris (1836–1841); and the English critic [John Ruskin](/scholars.html#ruskin-john) recognised in Giovanni's sculpture an expressive force ahead of its time.

Ruskin's estimate was extraordinary: he called Giovanni the "Canova of the thirteenth century," the sculptor to whom we owe "the grace of Ghiberti, the tenderness of Raphael, the majesty of Michelangelo." It is a striking fact, symptomatic of how the rediscovery ran ahead of scholarship, that three fragments of the pulpit acquired in this milieu — a tetramorph lectern-support and two little pillars with playing angels, now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York — were purchased before they had even been recognised as Giovanni's work or as belonging to the Pisa pulpit. In 1875 a monument to Giovanni by Salvino Salvini was erected in the Camposanto.

Against this rising nineteenth-century appreciation stands the ambivalence of the great positivist surveys. Adolfo Venturi, in his monumental *Storia dell'arte italiana*, is credited with the influential image of an "abyss" separating Giovanni from Nicola — a rupture between the classicism of the father and the Gothic pathos of the son. It was precisely this image that Max Seidel's 2012 monograph was written to refute, arguing instead, on the basis of extensive new archival research, for unbroken continuity between the two artists and for their relationship as a unique cultural phenomenon: a dialogue between a genius father and an equally gifted son.

[Pietro Toesca](/scholars.html#toesca-pietro)'s *Storia dell'arte italiana* provided a more measured foundational treatment. The twentieth-century rehabilitation was carried forward by a series of scholars and, importantly, by artists: the enthusiasm of Henry Moore and Marino Marini, and the monograph by Michael Ayrton (with Moore's introduction) of 1969, completed the transformation of Giovanni from Nicola's shadowy son into an autonomous master and, in the modernist imagination, a precursor. The 2025–2026 exhibition at the Palazzo dell'Opera del Duomo in Pisa, "Giovanni Pisano. Memoria di uno scultore" (13 December 2025 – 8 March 2026, Pacini editore, 2025), staged for the centenary of the 1926 reassembly and bringing back to Pisa the three fragments held by the Metropolitan Museum, took precisely this reception history as its theme, tracing the nineteenth-century birth of the artist's myth up to his rediscovery by Moore and Marini; its scientific committee included the allowlisted scholars Roberto Paolo Novello and Max Seidel.

## The Dismantling, Dispersal, and 1926 Reassembly of the Pisa Pulpit

The fate of the Pisa Cathedral pulpit deserves separate and detailed treatment, both for its own sake and for the methodological lessons it carries. The pulpit does not preserve either its original placement or its original state. When first made it stood, according to canonical usage, between the altar and the assembly on the south side of the presbytery, near the first column of the south-eastern arm beneath the dome, facing the right side of the choir stalls — a location confirmed by Vasari's testimony. After the fire that struck the cathedral on the night of 25 October 1595, the pulpit was dismantled and removed during the reconstruction, an operation carried out between December 1599 and May 1601 under Archbishop Carlo Antonio Dal Pozzo, partly to meet the new liturgical requirements of the Council of Trent and partly to repair fire damage in the presbytery.

The consequences for the monument were drastic. Its elements were dispersed: some were placed in the Camposanto, some walled up within the cathedral, and some reused in the Baroque pulpit of the Fancelli workshop of 1630. Further losses followed in the early nineteenth century. When at last, in the earlier twentieth century, the decision was taken to reconstruct the pulpit, the enterprise fell to Peleo Bacci, who directed the reassembly completed and installed in the cathedral in 1926, on the basis of documentary research — including manuscript sources — and of the surviving fragments. Bacci's reconstruction — a monument of its own moment, produced under the Fascist regime and presented in a lavish photographic volume, a copy of which was inscribed to Mussolini — restored the circular base, the basin's floor slab, and all the cornices, and set the pulpit at the end of the central nave.

The methodological significance of this history is considerable, and it must be stated frankly. The present arrangement of the Pisa pulpit is a modern reconstruction, and it is generally acknowledged to contain errors that disrupt the original narrative continuity of the reliefs; documentation of the pulpit's original form was lacking, so that the sequence of scenes and the disposition of the figures cannot be regarded as certainly Giovanni's own. Elements were left out or replaced, and three of the finest surviving fragments — the tetramorph lectern and the two pillars with music-making angels — are not in Pisa at all but in New York. Any stylistic argument about the Pisa pulpit as a total composition must therefore be advanced with the caveat that the whole we see is a twentieth-century assemblage of a work destroyed as a coherent object four centuries ago. The 2025–2026 exhibition, and the temporary return of the Metropolitan fragments, were conceived precisely to make this history visible and to mark the centenary of Bacci's labour — a labour that, whatever its imperfections, rescued one of the supreme monuments of medieval sculpture from oblivion.

## Legacy and Diffusion

Giovanni's influence radiated across Italy through his pupils, his collaborators, and the sheer authority of his works. The most direct heir was Tino di Camaino, the Sienese sculptor who was his follower and probably his pupil during the years of the Siena façade, who contributed to the Pistoia pulpit (notably to the relief of the Magi), and who became capomaestro of Pisa Cathedral in 1315, charged with the tomb of the Emperor Henry VII — the husband of the very empress whose Genoese tomb Giovanni had carved. Tino carried the multi-level wall tomb type forward through a distinguished career as a funerary sculptor in Pisa, Siena, Florence, and, from the 1320s, Naples. The reconstruction of Tino's oeuvre is the achievement of a connoisseurial tradition running from the early studies of [Wilhelm (William R.) Valentiner](/scholars.html#valentiner-wilhelm-r) — whose grand, Bode-school attributions have since been narrowed or rejected and should be treated as foundational but dated — through the more disciplined work of [Gert Kreytenberg](/scholars.html#kreytenberg-gert) and [Enzo Carli](/scholars.html#carli-enzo), the latter of whom devoted his degree thesis to Tino and became the leading authority on medieval Sienese and Pisan art.

A second channel of diffusion ran northward. Giovanni di Balduccio, an expatriate Pisan, carried the Tuscan Gothic to Bologna and above all to Milan, where his shrine of St Peter Martyr in Sant'Eustorgio (signed 1339), modelled on the Bolognese Arca di San Domenico but enriched with Gothic improvements including a fine tabernacle on the lid, became the fountainhead of a Lombard school. The classic study of this transplantation of Pisan Gothic into Lombardy is the late-nineteenth-century monograph of [Alfred Gotthold Meyer](/scholars.html#meyer-alfred-gotthold) on Giovanni di Balduccio and the Campionesi (1893), complemented by the mid-twentieth-century survey of Lombard Gothic sculpture by [Costantino Baroni](/scholars.html#baroni-costantino-nicola) (1944); the Genoese dimension of the diffusion, and the reworking and re-display of these sculptures in later centuries, are the subject of recent work by Francesca Girelli. A third line leads to Orvieto, where Lorenzo Maitani directed the façade whose sculptural programme extends and transforms the conception Giovanni had pioneered at Siena; the Orvieto façade reliefs are treated in the scholarship of Anita Fiderer Moskowitz.

It is important, finally, to frame this diffusion correctly. The migration of Pisan sculptors and forms from the Tuscan centres to Lombardy, Umbria, the Veneto, and the Angevin south is a paradigmatic case of the artistic geography that [Enrico Castelnuovo](/scholars.html#castelnuovo-enrico), with Carlo Ginzburg, reconceived under the rubric of "centre and periphery" — not a simple radiation of quality from a superior core to passive provinces, but a structured field of relations in which forms are received, adapted, and transformed according to local conditions and demands. Giovanni's art was not merely copied; it was reworked, and the history of its diffusion is the history of those reworkings. The relation of the Pisani to the beginnings of Giotto — first glimpsed by Della Valle — belongs to this larger story of how the sculptural revolution of the late Duecento fed the pictorial revolution that followed, and it is the reason Giovanni Pisano occupies a pivotal place not only in the history of sculpture but in the whole trajectory of Italian art toward the Renaissance.

## Concluding Assessment

What emerges from this survey is an artist whose achievement is inseparable from a set of problems the surviving evidence never fully resolves, and it is worth stating at the outset that this is not a deficiency of the present account but a condition of the material itself. No birth date, no single death date, no undisputed autograph passage in any collaborative work of his early years, no certainty as to how the knowledge of French Gothic sculpture reached him, no confidence that the reconstructed sequence of the Pisa pergamo reflects Giovanni's own ordering: the documentary skeleton assembled above is real and, for a thirteenth-century artist, unusually rich, but it is a skeleton, and the flesh has had to be supplied, work by work, from stylistic argument, inscriptional evidence, and the testimony of later and often unreliable witnesses. A synthesis that suppressed this condition in favour of a smoother narrative would be easier to read and would misrepresent the state of knowledge.

The historiographical axis identified at the outset — Venturi's abyss against Seidel's continuity — has proved, on the evidence gathered here, more of a lens than a verdict. The two pulpits at Siena and Pistoia, read side by side, show both things at once: a workshop method inherited intact from Nicola, in which the master's compositional and iconographic design absorbed and unified the contributions of pupils, and, within that inherited method, an unmistakable shift in what the finished work asks of the beholder — from the *Massacre of the Innocents* at Siena, energetic and youthful but still organised as incident, to the same subject at Pistoia, where incident is abolished and the field becomes, deliberately, illegible as narrative. Continuity of method, discontinuity of address: the formula is not elegant, but it is what the objects themselves support, and it explains why the debate has proved so durable rather than settling in either scholar's favour.

The French question resists closure for a structurally similar reason. Every material trace Giovanni left — the ivory Christs, the Baptistery's Rayonnant tracery, the *hanchement* of the Pisan Madonnas — attests a working knowledge of mature northern Gothic so intimate that it is difficult to credit to secondhand transmission alone, and yet no document places Giovanni north of the Alps, and Sauerländer's transnational model of continuous exchange, rather than a single formative journey, remains the more defensible frame precisely because it does not require the missing document. The right conclusion is not that the question has been answered but that it has been correctly reframed: from "did he go?" to "by what channels, of the several available to a well-connected Tuscan of his generation, did this knowledge travel?" — a question the evidence can address without needing to settle the first.

The Siena façade statuary supplies the clearest instance in the whole corpus of a further, recurring difficulty: the near-total substitution, in modern reception, of viewing conditions for the object itself. Figures calculated with extraordinary sophistication for a single, distant, upward sightline were transferred to eye level in artificial light, and it was under those altered conditions — not the ones Giovanni designed for — that the twentieth century, through Moore and Ayrton, arrived at its estimate of him as "the first modern sculptor." That the judgment may still be right does not make the route to it sound, and the entry has tried throughout to keep the two apart: what a work was built to do, and what a later century, looking at it wrongly, decided it did. The same caution applies, in a quieter register, to the polychromy recovered at Siena and preserved almost intact at Padua, which should unsettle any reading of Giovanni's sculpture as an art of pure form realised in white marble; it was, in its own time, a coloured and gilded art, and the monochrome record that dominates photography and museum display is a modern artifact of survival, not the sculptor's intention.

A further pattern, visible only once the entries on the ivories and the wooden crucifixes are read together, concerns the shape of the corpus itself rather than any single work. The literature Giovanni has generated is organised, almost without exception, around four public commissions — the two pulpits, the Siena façade, the Pisa Cathedral programme — because these are the works that carry signatures, documents, and civic weight. But the same conception of the suffering body recurs, with only minor variation, across the Cluny and London ivories and the three surviving wooden corpora at Siena, Pisa, and Massa Marittima, worked in three materials and at scales from fifteen to eighty-eight centimetres over perhaps four decades. This is not a minor addendum to the public oeuvre; it is evidence of a private, repeated, and evidently unresolved formal problem that Giovanni carried alongside his commissioned monuments, and an account of his art that omits it, as most surveys still do, is incomplete in a way that has nothing to do with the objects' comparative fame.

The physical fate of the two great pulpits offers a final, cautionary frame for everything said above about Giovanni's style. The Pistoia pulpit's comparative intactness has made it, rightly, the control against which the Pisa pergamo's twentieth-century reconstruction is measured — but even Pistoia, it must be recalled from the account of its dispersed lecterns, is not wholly as Giovanni left it, only far closer than Pisa. Any stylistic claim resting on the Pisa pulpit as a unified composition — on the sequence of its nine reliefs, on the relation of one scene to its neighbour around the ring — is therefore a claim about Peleo Bacci's 1926 assemblage as much as about Giovanni's design, a distinction the entry has tried to keep visible rather than let the seams of restoration disappear into a seamless narrative of "the pulpit."

None of this uncertainty diminishes the case, made repeatedly across the individual entries above, for Giovanni's centrality to the sculpture of his age. It is, if anything, sharpened by it. The *Massacre of the Innocents* at Pistoia remains a field organised around the deliberate refusal of a focal point; the Siena statuary remains the first monumental façade statuary in Italy, whatever the conditions under which it is now seen; the Scrovegni Madonna remains a marble object standing in genuine dialogue with Giotto's fresco cycle in the same small room; and the *elevatio* of Margaret of Brabant remains, in its fragmentary state, one of the most affecting inventions in medieval funerary sculpture. These judgments do not depend on resolving the birth date, the French journey, or the original sequence of the Pisa reliefs. They depend on the surviving stone, wood, and ivory, examined as carefully as the documentary record allows and reported, where that record runs out, as uncertain rather than as settled.

The durable achievement, finally, is the one Giovanni himself asserted in his signatures and that the historiography, for all its disagreements, has never seriously contested: the liberation of the sculpted figure from its subordination to the architectural frame, first accomplished at Siena and carried, by different means, through Pistoia, Pisa, and Padua to the fragmentary tomb at Genoa. Whatever remains unresolved about the man — and a great deal does — the figures he freed from the wall continue to lean out, turn, and address the viewer exactly as he calculated they should, and it is on that calculation, verifiable in the marble itself, that his historical importance ultimately and securely rests.

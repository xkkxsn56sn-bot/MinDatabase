## Introduction: a painter without a name

The painter whom Italian scholarship calls the *Maestro della Santa Cecilia* (in German the Cäcilienmeister, in French the Maître de Sainte Cécile) occupies an awkward position in the history of Florentine art. He is too important to be relegated to the crowd of minor Giotteschi. He is too elusive for the monographic treatment reserved for documented masters. For more than a century he has served as a hinge in the scholarly imagination.

He connects the Upper Church of San Francesco at Assisi with the churches of Florence, the late Duecento with the early Trecento, and the monumental language of [Giotto](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html) with what has been called a narrative, miniaturist inclination in Florentine painting. Every one of these connections has been questioned at some point. The account that follows separates what is documented from what is securely attributed, what is reasonably hypothesised, and what remains speculative, and it corrects a number of errors that circulate in print.

[Richard Offner](/scholars.html#offner-richard) gave the painter his first rigorous definition, in a 1927 article in the Burlington Magazine and then in the first volume of the Critical and Historical Corpus of Florentine Painting (1931). [Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos) expanded and reorganised the catalogue from the 1980s onwards, edited the second edition of Offner's volume (1986), and lent decisive support to the [Gaddo Gaddi](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Gaddo-Gaddi.html) hypothesis. [Sonia Chiodo](/scholars.html#chiodo-sonia) has recently given the most comprehensive synthesis of the painter's career in the Uffizi's online journal Imagines (no. 10, April 2024), alongside the publication of new technical evidence on the eponymous panel. Early writers — Lorenzo Ghiberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lorenzo-ghiberti" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, Giorgio Vasari and the chroniclers Dino Compagni and Giovanni Villani — are cited as primary sources.

## Critical history and the construction of the corpus

The painter was not discovered so much as distilled, slowly, from the larger argument over Giotto and Assisi. Nineteenth-century critics testing Vasari's attribution of the Franciscan cycle to Giotto came to recognise that a second personality was active in the first scene of the sequence and in the three concluding episodes. An early attempt to give the cycle to [Spinello Aretino](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Spinello-Aretino.html) and his son nonetheless had the merit of separating two hands. A great Anglo-Italian survey of the 1860s defended Giotto's authorship of the cycle.
On one point the sources disagree. The state catalogue credits the decisive recognition of the Uffizi panel's author in the last three Assisi scenes to a German scholar writing in 1903. Chiodo credits the link between the Assisi frescoes and the Cecilia panel to an earlier and influential German reading of the Assisi cycle from the 1880s. The two accounts are not necessarily incompatible, since the connection was probably sharpened in stages, but no single "discoverer" can be taken as settled.

What followed was a phase of rapid accretion and some confusion. Around 1903 English connoisseurship characterised the painter of the last Assisi scenes as having "a sense of elegance, almost of affectation". The same circle recorded the attribution of the two large panels in Santa Margherita a Montici to the same hand. According to the state catalogue record, a further step added the dated *Saint Peter Enthroned* from San Pier Maggiore, now in San Simone, together with a proposal to identify the painter with the shadowy "Stefano Fiorentino" of the sources.

An American connoisseur grouped these works in 1909 under the label "Giotto's Assistant A"; in a 1908 letter he had extended the hand to scenes 20–28 at Assisi. The most damaging intervention came between 1919 and 1924, in a series of Burlington Magazine articles presenting the painter as "a great contemporary of Giotto" and identifying him with [Buonamico Buffalmacco](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Buonamico-Buffalmacco.html). This followed from attributing to him frescoes at the Cistercian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-cistercian-order" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> Badia a Settimo and a panel from the same church, which the sources give to Buffalmacco. Chiodo judges the proposal unsustainable on philological grounds, yet it lingered in the literature well beyond the mid-century.

Offner's 1927 article ended the Buffalmacco hypothesis and set out a corpus that was deliberately narrow. According to Chiodo's reconstruction of Offner's position, it comprised the eponymous panel, the two Montici panels (the *Maestà* and the *Saint Margaret and Stories of her Life*) and the 1307 *Saint Peter*. To these Offner added, "with decision", Scenes XXVI–XXVIII of the Assisi cycle, and a work no one had previously considered: the *Madonna* of San Giorgio alla Costa. This last attribution has not survived. The San Giorgio alla Costa *Maestà* was recorded as Giotto's by Lorenzo Ghiberti in the mid-fifteenth century and is assigned to the young Giotto by [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni), Boskovits and [Luciano Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano), though not without dissent. Offner never accepted this, even as he wrote one of the most penetrating analyses the painting has received.

Offner's real achievement lay elsewhere. In the 1931 volume, *The School of the S. Cecilia Master*, he made the painter the head of a "miniaturist tendency" in Florentine painting: an inclination towards minute, approachable narrative, which he set beside the elevated manner of Giotto. He described the painter's style as "narrative in kind, mystical and lyrical in essence; and independent of the monumentalism of Giotto", and traced an evolution from the eponymous panel to the Saint Margaret dossal. Around the master he grouped other anonymous painters, among them the Master of the Corsi Crucifix and the [Master of the Horne Triptych](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/MaestroHorne.html). A second edition, edited by Boskovits, appeared in 1986 as *The St. Cecilia Master and his Circle*.

The Mostra Giottesca of 1937 brought the painter before a wider public. Practically all the works Offner had given him were exhibited, together with two further *Maestà* panels, one then at Pescia and one from the oratory of Santa Maria Maddalena at Pian di Mugnone, still labelled as anonymous Florentine. The leading Italian critics of the mid-century were cool towards the painter but decisively abandoned the idea of a Roman training, bringing him back into a strictly Giottesque orbit. In his final lists the American connoisseur who had called him "Assistant A" granted him an autonomous name, describing him as a "follower of the youthful Giotto; attractive in small figures, but less so in larger ones". He added the Pescia *Maestà*, the *Madonna and Child* from the Contini Bonacossi collection now in the J. Paul Getty Museum, and the Museo Horne triptych.

In the second half of the twentieth century, as Chiodo observes, the study of the painter is "substantially linked to the name of Miklós Boskovits". His Corpus volume on the painters of the miniaturist tendency (1984) gave the master the Apostles on the intrados between the fifth and sixth bays of the nave of [Santa Maria Novella](/Content/Churches/Basilica-Santa-Maria-Novella.html), frescoes in the chapel of Saint Catherine in the same church, and twelve historiated initials in the Gradual D from the Cistercian abbey of Settimo. He cautiously suggested that a small group gathered around the Horne triptych represented the painter's late activity in the third decade of the century, and later attached to this reconstruction a decided endorsement of the identification with Gaddo Gaddi.

## The eponymous dossal

### Saint Cecilia and Stories from her Life

The panel that gave the Saint Cecilia Master his name came from the small Florentine church of Santa Cecilia, which stood beside the future Piazza della Signoria and served, before the Palazzo dei Priori existed, as a meeting place for the city's governing bodies. Vasari saw it on the high altar and gave it to [Cimabue](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Cimabue.html); it entered the Florentine galleries in the nineteenth century and now hangs in the Uffizi among the founders of Tuscan painting.

Eight episodes from the saint's *Passio* flank the enthroned titular in two registers, each originally accompanied by a Latin inscription in gold on blue, now largely effaced but recently recovered by technical imaging. The same diagnostic campaign, conducted by the Opificio delle Pietre Dure in 2021, revealed that the central figure's face and drapery were substantially repainted by a later hand, perhaps within a generation or two, while the eight lateral scenes remain untouched — a finding that has reopened the dating question and strengthened the case for placing the original work close to 1300, in the immediate orbit of the young Giotto. The scenes read from top to bottom and left to right on each wing, tracing not the macabre spectacle of martyrdom favoured by popular legendaries but the career of a Christian orator: Cecilia persuading, instructing, converting and disputing, until the final cauldron seals her witness.

#### Saint Cecilia Enthroned

The titular saint sits rigidly frontal on a monumental throne of pale rose and cream, a piece of fictive architecture as ambitious as any of the buildings in the lateral scenes. Its tall gabled back rises against the gold like a church façade, flanked by slender colonnettes and crowned by finials; the seat projects towards the viewer in confident foreshortening, its platform and moulded arms establishing a shallow but legible stage. This throne, with its inlays and sculptural ornament, belongs to the experimental furniture of Giotto's circle around 1300, and recent criticism has argued that the Cecilia Master took a leading part in precisely this research into the painted construction of space.

Cecilia herself wears a rose-red robe under a dark mantle, its edges gathered by red cords that cross her breast; her head is veiled in white beneath the halo. In her left hand she holds a small red-bound book, the emblem of the Gospel she carried "always in her bosom" according to the *Passio*, while her raised right hand displays a slender attribute usually read as the martyr's palm. Nothing in her dress or gesture alludes to music; the panel presents her instead as teacher and confessor.

The face the visitor sees today, however, is not the face the master painted. Radiography and X-ray fluorescence have shown that beneath the present broad, softly modelled features lies an earlier physiognomy with small, closely set eyes, a long sharp nose and a diminutive mouth — exactly the facial type of the figures in the eight side scenes and of the master's frescoes at Assisi. The repainting, which also deepened the drapery folds and added a gold border to the mantle, was a stylistic updating rather than a repair, carried out perhaps by the 1330s or 1340s; no underlying damage required it. Generations of critics who contrasted the "plastic and massive" central saint with the slighter narrative figures were therefore comparing two moments and probably two painters, and every stylistic argument built on this face must now be set aside.

#### The Wedding Feast of Cecilia and Valerian

The cycle opens, as the *Passio* does, with the wedding that Cecilia did not want. Behind a long table spread with a white cloth sit the bridal party, Cecilia distinguished by her halo among the guests, while servants move in the foreground with dishes and vessels. The painter sets the banquet within a tall, fantastical building of superimposed storeys, its upper levels opening into loggias and crowned by towers — one of those architectural inventions, laid out with incised lines before the figures were drawn, that remain his most personal signature. The structure does not so much contain the scene as announce it, a festive urban palace whose vertical extravagance matches the social weight of the occasion.

The narrative point of the scene is interior and invisible, and the painter trusts his audience to know it. While the wedding was celebrated, the legend says, and the instruments sounded, Cecilia sang in her heart to God alone, praying that her body be kept unstained — the phrase *cantantibus organis* that would, centuries later, transform her into the patroness of music. Here there is no organ and no concert: only the composed, slightly withdrawn figure of the bride amid the celebration, her abstraction conveyed by stillness rather than gesture.

As an opening image the feast performs the same function as the *Homage of the Simple Man* at Assisi: it plants the protagonist in the ordinary world — a world of tables, servants and social obligation — from which her vocation will progressively detach her. The lively genre detail of the attendants, observed with the quick, economical touch typical of the side scenes, gives the episode a worldliness against which the following chamber scene will strike its note of secrecy and conversion.

#### Cecilia Reveals her Vow to Valerian

The second scene moves from the public feast to the privacy of the bridal chamber, reduced by the painter to a spare, box-like interior whose receding walls and open doorway frame the two protagonists alone. Cecilia and Valerian stand face to face, and the whole drama is carried by the dialogue of their hands: she speaks, he listens. On their wedding night, the *Passio* recounts, Cecilia told her husband a secret — that an angel of God watched over her body, who would strike Valerian down if he touched her in carnal love, but would love him as he loved her if he respected her purity.

It is the first of the panel's many conversations, and it establishes the register in which this cycle understands sanctity. Cecilia's power is verbal; her weapon is the persuasive word, deployed first on her husband, then on his brother, then on officials and crowds, and finally on the Roman prefect himself. The painter gives her the upright, slender silhouette and small head characteristic of all his figures, but invests the exchange with a quiet urgency: the slight inclination of the bodies toward one another, the measured interval between them, the architecture closing around their secret.

Valerian's reply in the legend — that he would believe only if he could see the angel — sets in motion everything that follows, for Cecilia sends him along the Via Appia to Pope Urban, hiding among the tombs of the martyrs, to be instructed and baptised. The scene thus functions as the hinge of the entire left wing: from this whispered conversation flow the baptism, the angelic crowns and the conversion of Tiburtius in the scenes around it.

#### The Crowning of Valerian and Cecilia

The third episode delivers the proof Valerian demanded. Returning from his baptism at the hands of Pope Urban, he found Cecilia at prayer in her chamber, and beside her the angel he had been promised, radiant, bearing two crowns woven of roses and lilies gathered in paradise — flowers that would never fade and could be seen only by those of unstained body. The painter stages the miracle in another of his tall, ingeniously sectioned interiors, the winged messenger standing at the left of the chamber before the haloed Cecilia.

The handling of the crowns contains a small but telling departure from the written sources. In the *Golden Legend* and the *Passio* the angel crowns both spouses; here, as Chiodo's 2024 study of the scene has clarified, the angel crowns Valerian, who in turn extends the second wreath to Cecilia. The gesture turns a passive reception into an exchange between the couple, a chaste reciprocity that visualises the "love like his love for her" promised in the preceding scene — one of those quiet adjustments of the text that reveal a painter, or a theological adviser, thinking about the story rather than merely transcribing it.

The scene is also the panel's single irruption of the supernatural. Everywhere else the sacred works through speech, water and fire; only here does heaven become visible, and only to the purified. The painter keeps the miracle domestic in scale, without the clouds or glories of later art: the angel simply stands within the architecture of the house, as the legend insists the angel of the Lord dwelt with Cecilia. The quiet matter-of-factness is characteristic of his narrative temper, lyrical rather than ecstatic.

#### Cecilia Instructs Valerian and Tiburtius

The last scene of the left wing gathers its fruits. Tiburtius, Valerian's brother, entering the house, marvelled at the scent of roses and lilies out of season; Cecilia seized the opening and delivered the long catechesis that the *Passio* develops over several chapters — on the emptiness of idols that cannot feel or move, on the one God, on the Son and his redeeming death. The painter shows her in colloquy with the two young men within a blue-grey architectural setting whose towers and angular projections carry the eye upward, the small group bound together by the directed energy of her speech and the attentive stillness of her listeners.

The episode doubles the structure of the second scene: once again a private conversation, once again conversion by argument. But where the bridal chamber held two figures, here there are three, and the addition matters, for Tiburtius' conversion proves that Cecilia's word works beyond the special intimacy of marriage. The *Passio* gives him the cycle's most memorable objection — that whoever confesses Christ will be burned alive — and gives Cecilia the reply that this life is cheap and the next beyond price; the fresco of persuasion that the panel paints scene by scene reaches here its first full statement.

Within the economy of the altarpiece this scene closes the domestic half of the story. Everything on the left wing happens indoors, among family; everything on the right will happen in public, before the Church and the Roman state. The painter marks the shift architecturally: the sheltering interiors of the left give way, across the central throne, to baptistery, tribunal and place of execution.

#### The Baptism (Pope Urban Baptises Maximus)

The right wing opens under the sign of the sacrament. Within a domed exedra — a semicircular, niched structure that gives the scene an unmistakably ecclesial gravity — the aged Pope Urban, vested and nimbed, administers baptism to a figure at the font, while a group of haloed witnesses stands at the left. The architecture is among the most remarkable on the panel: the painter curves his fictive building around the rite, its concavity embracing celebrant and catechumen, an intuition of spatial enclosure that looks forward to the exedrae of fourteenth-century painting.

The traditional label for this scene, repeated in the older literature and still attached to it in catalogue records, is the baptism of Tiburtius. Chiodo's 2024 study of the panel, however, reading the images against the recovered inscriptions and the ancient *Passio*, identifies the baptised figure as Maximus — the officer of the prefect charged with guarding Valerian and Tiburtius on the eve of their execution, who was converted by their constancy and by Cecilia's nocturnal exhortation, and who saw their souls leave their bodies like virgins going forth to the bridal chamber. Behind him, in this reading, stand the already-converted Valerian, Tiburtius and Cecilia, their halos marking the company of the saved into which the jailer is being received.

Whichever name one attaches to the catechumen, the scene's function in the programme is the same: it shows the word becoming sacrament, the chain of persuasion begun in Cecilia's bedchamber issuing in the visible rite of the Church. That the panel's one strictly liturgical image is presided over by a pope was surely not lost on the panel's first audience in a church frequented by the priors and the great guilds of Florence — an image of legitimate, apostolic order at the heart of a cycle about public speech.

#### Cecilia Preaching

The second scene of the right wing presents the saint's word at its most public. Cecilia stands at the left beneath a projecting upper storey, her figure erect and her hand raised in the orator's gesture, addressing a compact audience of seated and standing listeners; among them a figure in vivid red occupies a bench with particular prominence. The architecture again does more than decorate: the overhanging balcony shelters the speaker like a pulpit's tester, while the open ground before her becomes the space of the assembly.

Older accounts call the scene simply the preaching of Cecilia to the people, and read it as the mass conversion — four hundred souls, the legend says — that she worked in her own house. Chiodo's 2024 rereading of the panel through the ancient *Passio* proposes something more precise: the moment when Almachius' officials, sent to bring her to sacrifice, are instead confronted and converted by her, urged to consider whether the orders they carry are just; the seated man in red would then be Gordianus, the sympathetic official who afterwards sheltered her household and her property. The difference is not trivial, for in this reading even the machinery of persecution is turned, for a moment, into a congregation.

Either way the scene is the panel's thesis made visible. Chosen over the miraculous and the macabre episodes that the legendaries offered in abundance, it shows Cecilia doing what the whole altarpiece claims for her: standing in a public place and changing minds by speech. For a panel displayed in a church where the city's magistrates gathered, the image of just authority persuaded rather than coerced carried an evident civic resonance — though this, like all iconological readings of the programme, remains an interpretation rather than a documented intention.

#### Cecilia before the Prefect Almachius

The trial scene brings the saint face to face with the power she has been quietly dismantling. Almachius sits enthroned beneath an aedicule at the right, elevated above the pavement, while Cecilia stands before him with her companions, her bearing unbowed, her hand once more lifted in argument. The *Passio* preserves their exchange as a genuine forensic duel: asked her condition, she answers that she is freeborn and noble; told that he holds power of life and death, she corrects him — he can take life but cannot give it, and is therefore a minister of death, nothing more.

The painter, or his adviser, makes one striking choice: the Roman prefect wears a crown and holds sceptre and orb, the regalia not of a provincial magistrate but of a king or emperor. The anachronism is surely deliberate in effect if not in intention, elevating the confrontation from a police matter to an emblem — the Christian confessor before worldly sovereignty as such. Enthroned at the right wing's lower register, Almachius becomes the structural counter-image of the enthroned Cecilia at the panel's centre: two seats of authority, one founded on the sword, one on the word, set in silent comparison across the gold.

It is the last conversation of the cycle, and the only one Cecilia loses — in the courtroom, at least. Condemned, she will be sent not to public execution but to the bath of her own house; yet the legend insists, and the panel's whole rhetoric confirms, that the defeat is the prefect's. His tribunal, for all its canopy and regalia, has been out-argued by a young woman, and the audience knows that the converts she has made throughout the preceding scenes already outnumber his court.

#### The Martyrdom of Saint Cecilia in the Boiling Bath

The cycle ends in fire and water. Condemned by Almachius to die in the *balneum* of her own house, heated beyond endurance, Cecilia stands in the cauldron-like bath with her arms raised in the ancient gesture of prayer, serene and unharmed, while executioners crouch at its base feeding the flames; dark billows roll upward above her. Uniquely on the panel, the painter abandons architecture altogether: the scene is set against bare, jagged rock, a wilderness that isolates the martyr from the civic and domestic world of all the preceding episodes and turns the private bathhouse of the legend into an elemental stage.

The *Passio* recounts that she remained in the scalding bath a day and a night as if in a cool place, singing; that the executioner then struck three blows at her neck and failed to sever her head; and that she survived three days more, preaching and disposing of her goods for the poor. Of all this the painter shows only the bath. The omission of the botched decapitation — the episode that dominates later imagery of the saint, down to Maderno's famous statue — is the panel's final editorial decision, consistent with its whole temper: the programme that preferred catechesis to spectacle to the end declines to dwell on the wound, and leaves Cecilia upright, praying, victorious over the element meant to destroy her.

The orant pose does double work. It is, literally, what the legend describes — she prayed in the flames — but it is also the immemorial attitude of the Church at prayer, familiar from catacomb painting and from the apse mosaics of Rome; the martyr in her cauldron becomes, in the last image, an icon of intercession. For the faithful of Santa Cecilia in Florence, lifting their eyes from this corner of the altarpiece to the enthroned saint at its centre, the sequence closed as a *Passio* read aloud would close: with the witness complete and the advocate installed in glory.

## The Assisi question: scene I and the last scenes of the Legend of Saint Francis (three or four, XXV/XXVI–XXVIII, according to the authors)

The master's place in the Upper Church of San Francesco is both the foundation of his critical existence and the most contested element of his catalogue. In the fullest form of the hypothesis, he painted scene I and the last scenes of the cycle, three or four (XXV/XXVI–XXVIII) according to the authors (south wall, on the left as one faces the altar), among the twenty-eight in the lower register of the nave. The first is the *Homage of a Simple Man*. The last scenes are the *Dream of Gregory IX* (Scene XXV, included by the authors who count four), the *Healing of the Man of Lérida* (Scene XXVI, the wounded devotee of Ilerda in Catalonia), the *Confession of the Woman of Benevento* (Scene XXVII, the woman raised from the dead) and the *Liberation of the Repentant Heretic Peter of Alife* (Scene XXVIII).
The layers of opinion are best kept apart. That the three final scenes differ in hand from the main body of the cycle is the common ground of most modern criticism. That their painter is the author of the Uffizi panel is the core of the traditional attribution. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale lists a long line of critics from the 1920s to the 1980s who accept it, including Offner in 1927 and the Corpus volume edited by Boskovits in 1984, usually with the qualification that he worked "probably under the watchful guidance of Giotto". Proposed for him only at times in the earlier literature (1908, 1924, 1967), scene I is now assigned to him by most critics. One handbook of the 1920s gave him the last four scenes plus two figures at the far right of the first.

Chiodo herself is more nuanced than any summary suggests. The captions in her 2024 essay attribute the *Homage of a Simple Man* and the *Confession of the Woman of Benevento* to "Giotto di Bondone and the Master of Saint Cecilia" jointly, a model of collaboration rather than separate authorship. In her Paragone article of the same year she goes further: the painter "assumes the direction of the work site, executing four Franciscan stories". She places his work at Assisi around the mid-1290s, when Giotto left him to complete the undertaking. These are interpretations argued from style and from the radiographic evidence of the Uffizi panel. No document places any named painter on the Upper Church scaffolding.

The study of the giornate published in 1962 showed that the *Homage of a Simple Man*, though first in the narrative, was painted last. The explanation that it was left for last because of the beam of the choir screen that crossed the wall at that point remains a hypothesis, plausible and widely held, but an inference from physical evidence.

Two dissenting positions show that the Assisi attribution is not unanimous. The first reassigns the last three scenes to an Umbrian painter. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale entry on the Maestro Espressionista di Santa Chiara reports that a critic writing in 1967 isolated, among Giotto's Umbrian collaborators, a painter "active in the last three Stories of Saint Francis … previously referred to the Florentine Master of Saint Cecilia". This collaborator was later merged with the Maestro Espressionista di Santa Chiara. That master has in turn been identified by several scholars with Palmerino di Guido, documented at Assisi in 1307, who on 4 January 1309 repaid a debt contracted jointly with Giotto. Each link in this chain is hypothetical, but it gives the Florentine attribution a serious competitor.

The second dissent is more radical. In a 2004 essay an eminent Giotto scholar called it "pathetic that many art historians continue to believe" in the master's presence at Assisi, according to the quotation reported by Chiodo, and maintained the position in later writings. Chiodo explains this refusal as part of his effort to give maximum weight to the originality of the Assisi solutions, which only Giotto's constant direction could explain. She calls it "as categorical as it is hard to understand" and notes that it found no following. It nonetheless comes from one of the most authoritative voices in Giotto studies.

The master's role is thus embedded in the larger "Assisi question". Chiodo draws a sharp line between the North American tradition, "until recently irreducibly denialist" about Giotto's authorship of the cycle, and the European tradition since the 1860s, which has tended to accept Vasari's attribution. A study of 1996 gave a large share of the cycle to a team associated with [Pietro Cavallini](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Pietro-Cavallini.html); it has not won general acceptance. For the Saint Cecilia Master the consequence is plain. His presence at Assisi is the majority view among those who see the cycle as Giotto's enterprise, and it stands or falls with the model of Giotto's workshop one adopts.

Two proposals extending his partnership with Giotto beyond Assisi have been judged unconvincing. One places him beside Giotto in the [Peruzzi Chapel](/Content/Churches/Cappella-Peruzzi.html) in Santa Croce; Chiodo rejects it because of the "sidereal distance" between those mature murals and the master's harsher, more conventional language. The other places him at Rimini; it was first proposed in the mid-century and revived in 2019, and Chiodo considers its 1965 rejection still valid.

The Legend of Saint Francis — the twenty-eight scenes that run around the nave of the Upper Church beneath the windows, and the most consequential fresco cycle of the Italian Middle Ages — begins not with a vision or a miracle but with a piece of street theatre. Bonaventure<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-bonaventure" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> opens the *Legenda maior*, the cycle's textual source throughout, with the episode: a simple man of Assisi, meeting the young Francesco di Bernardone<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> in the town square, spread his own cloak beneath the youth's feet, declaring that he was worthy of all reverence, for he would shortly do great things and be magnificently honoured by all the faithful.

The cycle thus opens under the sign of prophecy: before the renunciation, before the stigmata, before sanctity itself, an unlettered man sees what no one else can. And the painter has taken the theological point literally — Francis, still a layman in fashionable dress, his hair uncut, already wears the halo. The viewer, like the simple man, is asked to recognise the saint in the citizen.

What made the scene immediately famous, and keeps it famous, is where it happens. The square is the Piazza del Comune of Assisi, and the building at its centre is the Roman temple the town still possesses — the so-called Temple of Minerva, its six Corinthian columns and pediment rendered with an attention to a specific, standing, identifiable monument that has no real precedent in medieval painting. This is, by common consent, something very close to the first portrait of a place in Italian art.

It is not, however, a photograph: the painter has slimmed the columns, roofed the porch in red tile, filled the pediment with a Gothic rose window flanked by reliefs of angels, and generally baptised the pagan structure into the Christian present — the temple as thirteenth-century Assisi used and imagined it, not as archaeology would restore it. At the left rises the battlemented civic tower with its mullioned openings, shown — as has often been observed — still without its final crowning, a detail that has entered the dating debate since the actual tower was brought to completion only in the early years of the fourteenth century; at the right a building crowned by an airy wooden loggia closes the square. Between these three structures the town becomes a stage, and the civic space itself a witness to the prophecy.

The figure composition has the lapidary economy that governs the whole cycle. Francis advances from the left, slightly larger than life against the temple steps, his right hand opened outward in a gesture of mild, almost puzzled acceptance; the simple man kneels at the lower right, both hands stretching the cloak flat upon the pavement with the care of a servant preparing a king's path. Two pairs of commentators frame the event — at the left, two young men in red caps exchange glances and gestures over the strange scene; at the right, two older citizens, one in the red of civic dignity, mirror them. Their conversation is the picture's second subject: the town deciding what to make of its most unaccountable son. The symmetry of the witnesses, the frontal temple, the measured intervals between the figures give the opening scene the character of a solemn overture — deliberately more ceremonial, more static, than the narrative turbulence to come.

The scene's place in the cycle's internal history must be stated with care, for the Legend is the most disputed enterprise in all of Italian painting. The attribution of the cycle to Giotto rests on an old and weighty tradition — Riccobaldo of Ferrara<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-riccobaldo-ferrarese" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> noted Giotto's work in the basilica while the painter was alive, Ghiberti and Vasari consolidated the claim — and it remains the prevailing view of Italian scholarship; against it stands the line of criticism, classically formulated in Richard Offner's "Giotto, non-Giotto" of 1939, which finds the Legend irreconcilable with the documented Giotto of Padua and assigns it to a distinct, anonymous master with a large workshop.

That debate is set out in the entry on Giotto. But within the cycle itself connoisseurship has long distinguished several hands, and on one point most critics agree: scene I, together with the last scenes of the cycle, three or four (XXV/XXVI–XXVIII) according to the authors, belongs to the refined painter known as the Saint Cecilia Master — active at the two ends of the cycle, and perhaps the man who brought the enterprise to completion. The elongated elegance of the figures, the pale blond tonality, the ornamental precision of the architecture distinguish this fresco from the broader, graver manner of the scenes that follow, and the attentive visitor can measure the difference within a few steps of the nave.

The cycle is generally placed in the last years of the thirteenth century, in the pontificates between Nicholas IV — the first Franciscan pope, whose patronage reoriented the decoration of the basilica — and Boniface VIII, with some scholars extending the work into the opening years of the fourteenth; the unfinished civic tower in this very scene is among the arguments for an early terminus. Beneath the scene, as beneath each of the twenty-eight, ran a painted Latin titulus drawn from the *Legenda maior*, identifying the event for the literate; these inscriptions survive today only in fragmentary state.

The fresco shares the long conservation history of the Upper Church, most dramatically the earthquake of 26 September 1997, whose destructions however fell principally upon the vaults; the Legend itself came through, and the dark scar in the sky of this scene — the shadow of a hanging lamp burnt into the plaster — is among the oldest wounds the picture carries, almost a relic of the basilica's seven centuries of continuous use.

For the study of the Saint Cecilia Master the scene is a threshold in a double sense: it opens the Legend, and it opens the problem — the Assisi question — that every study of Giotto must traverse. Whoever painted it invented something that the [Bardi Chapel](/Content/Churches/Cappella-Bardi.html), a generation later, still remembered: the sacred story staged in the recognisable, inhabited spaces of an Italian town, before the eyes of citizens who look on and judge.

With the twenty-fifth scene the Legend enters its epilogue — the four posthumous miracles that close the cycle — and it opens where the doubt was most dangerous: in the breast of the pope who had performed the canonization. Bonaventure, in the appendix of Miracula attached to the Legenda maior, relates that Gregory IX, before he had fully committed his heart, harboured a hesitation over the wound in the side; and the saint resolved it in person, by night, in a dream.

The miracle deserves Bonaventure's own words, for the fresco follows them gesture by gesture (*Legenda maior*, *Miracula*, De sacris stigmatibus, 2):

> Dominus Gregorius papa nonus […] de vulnere laterali cordis gerebat aliquantulum haesitationis occultae. Nocte igitur quadam, […] apparuit ei beatus Franciscus in somnis, et elevato aliquantulum brachio dextro, detexit vulnus lateralis sui, petivitque phialam sibi dari, quae allata, usque ad summum sanguine de latere profluente impleri videbatur. Ex tunc igitur tanta coepit devotione sacra illa stigmata venerari, ut nunquam deinceps aliquem ea vana praesumptione impugnantem audire posset, quin zelo succensus argueret.

"The lord Pope Gregory the Ninth […] bore in his heart a certain hidden hesitation concerning the wound of the side. One night, therefore, […] the blessed Francis appeared to him in sleep, and, raising his right arm a little, laid bare the wound of his side, and asked that a phial be given him; which, being brought, was seen to be filled to the brim with the blood that flowed from the side. From that hour he began to venerate those sacred stigmata with such devotion that never afterwards could he hear anyone impugn them with vain presumption without rebuking him, kindled with zeal."

The cycle, which four scenes earlier had given the wounds their lay notary in the probing hand of Jerome, here gives them their supreme attestation — and the symmetry is exact and surely designed: the knight verifies by touching, the pontiff by receiving; the doubt of the learned layman is cut away before the citizens, the doubt of Christ’s vicar is healed in the secrecy of sleep, by a relic delivered from heaven’s side of the grave.

The scene is also the cycle’s last dream, and it closes the series of visions — the palace, the Lateran, the thrones, the Gargano — with the grammar they established intact: no cloud, no frame, no diminution; the saint simply stands in the chamber, full-scale, at the bed’s edge, while the dreamer sleeps on. Gregory lies in his red mantle with the tiara on his head — the office kept even in sleep, exactly as the mitred bishop of Assisi dreamed in Scene XXI, so that we may know not the man but the papacy to be the vision’s addressee.

Francis bends toward him with the two gestures that are the whole narrative: the right hand — the very arm Bonaventure’s text has him raise — draws open the habit at the breast, baring the way to the right side and its wound; the left extends the small phial along the bed toward the sleeping face. Below, the attendants of the chamber sleep in a frieze of varied abandonments — one slumped on his hand, one crouched by the bed-chest, one in white sunk forward at prayer-turned-doze, one robed figure with a crossed stole nodding at the left — the drowsing court that this cycle has set around its dreams since the night of Innocent III, here multiplied into a little anthology of sleep.

The setting marks the change of hands as clearly as any face. The principal workshop of the Legend built its interiors of architecture — arcades, vaults, aediculae; this chamber is built of cloth. Under a coffered ceiling, the room is a box of textiles: the canopy on its wrought-iron rods and branched suspension — a document of medieval bed-furniture rendered with antiquarian precision — the gold-diapered hanging walling the whole width of the field, the dark embroidered valance running like a line of script above it, the patterned coverlets and the inlaid chest.

This ornamental splendour, the elongated elegance of the standing saint, the pale refinement of the surviving heads are the signature of the Saint Cecilia Master, to whose orbit connoisseurship assigns the cycle’s final scenes; the design, with its long memory of the earlier dreams, remains the Legend’s own, and the handover of execution within a single designing intelligence — or the succession of masters, as the Offnerian line would have it — is part of the Assisi question discussed in the section on the Assisi question above.

The condition is a tale of two materials: the textiles, painted with the care of a goldsmith, survive magnificently — the diaper of the great hanging among the best-preserved ornamental passages of the whole cycle — while the faces have suffered, those of the two left-hand attendants effaced almost wholly, pale blanks in the midst of splendour, and the upper zone’s blue reduced to the usual bruised dark above the coffering. The titulus below is fragmentary, and the scene passed the earthquake of 1997 without structural loss. It remains the epilogue’s perfect overture: the Legend’s last dream, its last doubt, and its strangest relic — a pope asleep in his crown, and a dead man standing at the bedside, filling a phial with the proof.

The twenty-sixth scene is the epilogue’s miracle of the body. Bonaventure, in the appendix of Miracula that closes the Legenda maior, tells of John, a citizen of Lerida in Catalonia devoted to Saint Francis: set upon one night and savagely wounded, he was given up by the physicians, who judged the wounds past cure — the account dwells on their despair, and on the surgeon who would not even return by night; and the dying man, unable to do more, called upon the name of Francis. The saint answered as a physician answers: he came. In the night he appeared at the bedside, touched the wounds with those hands that bore the marks of Christ, unbound the dressings and anointed the flesh — and the man rose healed, so wholly that his tormentor’s work left no trace. The chapter is the epilogue’s thesis stated in flesh: the canonized saint keeps the habits of the living one, and the stigmatized hands, verified by Jerome and sealed in Gregory’s phial two scenes back, are now put to their work — instruments not of proof but of mercy.

The painter — the refined master of the closing scenes — has staged the miracle as a night-piece divided by a curtain, and the division carries the meaning. The chamber is of the family built in the preceding dream of Gregory: a loggia-like enclosure under a coffered ceiling on slender white colonnettes, framed plaques on the rear wall, teal night in the window-openings, vase-finials at the corners — and across its front, the great checked curtain, half-drawn and looped in a heavy sheaf. To its right, the revealed zone: the bed with its crimson coverlet, the wounded man propped on his pillows, and Francis bending low over him, both hands at the bandaged side, the healer’s bending back — not the orant of the visions but a body at work, the saint as medicus in the full clinical sense; behind the bed two attendants lean in, one haloed in pale green with joined hands, one bending with dark outspread wings — the angelic escort of the night visit, heaven assisting at an operation. To the curtain’s left, the concealed-from zone: three men — one in the long white of the physician’s art, one in a violet mantle and cap turning away, one in green — stand in grave conversation, their backs effectively to the bed. They are the world that gave the patient up: the doctors and kinsmen of the account, discussing a death that, behind the cloth, is being revoked. The Legend’s oldest device — the witnesses who do not see — here receives its last and driest formulation: the professionals of healing, talking, while healing happens.

The scene’s place in the epilogue is exactly calibrated. The four posthumous miracles run as a graded demonstration of the new saint’s reach — the pope’s doubt, the body’s wounds, the soul’s unconfessed sin, the prisoner’s chains — and Lerida, set in Spain, quietly makes a further point that the friars of the mother basilica cannot have missed: the reach is now geographic as well, the saint of Assisi operative at the far end of the Mediterranean, wherever his name is called. The execution belongs, with the rest of the closing scenes, to the orbit of the Saint Cecilia Master, whose signature is everywhere here — the elongated elegance of the standing figures, the ornamental precision of curtain and coffering, the pale blond key; the design’s continuity with the whole cycle, and the question of the designing mind’s name, Giotto for the tradition and prevailing Italian scholarship, an anonymous master for the Offnerian line, are discussed in the section on the Assisi question above.

The condition is worn in the manner of the final bay: the surfaces are abraded throughout, the night blues altered toward teal, the faces of the left-hand group and of the attendants thinned, and the reading of the winged figure — wings today darkened almost to silhouette — requires the close looking the modern visitor can rarely give; the titulus below is fragmentary, and the scene passed the earthquake of 1997 without structural loss. It remains the epilogue’s most humane invention: a sickroom at night, the experts in retreat beyond the curtain, and at the bed the founder of the order doing, with pierced hands, a nurse’s work.

The twenty-seventh scene carries the epilogue past the body into the economy of the soul — the furthest, the Legend implies, that intercession can reach. Bonaventure’s Miracula relate the case of a woman of the environs of Benevento, devoted to Francis, who died with a sin on her conscience that she had never dared confess. While the clergy kept the vigil and the household mourned, she suddenly sat up on the bier and called for a priest: Saint Francis, she declared, had obtained for her this return — that she might confess the sin whose silence was damning her; and having confessed, and received absolution, she composed herself upon the bier and fell asleep in the Lord in peace. The chapter is the epilogue’s boldest claim, and the medieval mind would have measured it exactly: healing restores the body, but this miracle reopens a closed judgement — the saint’s advocacy reaching into the interval after death, where no physician and no pope has writ, to retrieve one soul by the width of a single unspoken sentence.

The painter has built the theology as a house of three storeys of reality. At the upper left, outside the architecture, the transaction’s heavenly half: Francis kneels upon clouds before the half-figure of Christ, who leans from the sky’s corner with the gesture of granting — the intercession itself, painted as a courtroom aside between advocate and Judge; it is the cycle’s final apparition of Christ, closing the series that began over the sleeping youth of Scene III.

Below, the earthly theatre divides with the curtain-logic of the preceding scene into the zone of rite and the zone of miracle. At the left, under a porch, the apparatus of death interrupted: clergy with tall candles, the book, tonsured clerks and a group of friars pressing behind — the obsequies suspended mid-office, their candles still burning for a woman no longer dead. At the right, in the tall gold chamber, the event: the woman, raised on her bier in the pale blue of her pall, bends to the ear of the seated priest — the confession rendered as the oldest and most intimate of compositions, two heads inclined together, the secret passing between profile and profile; behind her the women of the house crowd in wonder, hands flung up; at the bier’s foot a servant kneels with a basin and a lady presses her cheek in lingering grief not yet overtaken by the news.

And across the centre of the field, between the two zones, the picture’s famous exclamation: the demon — dark, bat-winged, claws spread — in full flight, abandoning the house; while above the chamber a small angel sails inward. The soul’s contested interval, which the sermon literature described and the Arezzo scene painted as a city’s sky, is here domestic: one devil leaving, one angel arriving, over a woman whispering into a priest’s ear.

The scene belongs with the closing scenes to the orbit of the Saint Cecilia Master — the attenuated figures, the blond key, the ornamental architecture with its trefoil-arched chamber and inscribed cornice are his hallmarks — and connoisseurship has long counted this field among his most personal in the cycle; the design’s long symmetries, however, keep faith with the whole program: the fleeing demon answers the routed swarm of Arezzo, the interceding Francis answers the dreams’ standing visitations, and the confession completes the cycle’s anatomy of penance begun at the knight of Celano’s last-minute shrift. The question of the designing mind — Giotto for the tradition and prevailing Italian scholarship, an anonymous master for the Offnerian line — is discussed in the section on the Assisi question above.

The condition is that of the final bay, with its particular fortunes: the architecture’s golds and the figures survive in fair state, the blues have altered toward teal, abrasions wear the left-hand group and the inscribed cornice — whose painted lettering is now fragmentary — and the demon has darkened toward silhouette. The titulus beneath is fragmentary, and the scene passed the earthquake of 1997 without structural loss. It remains the epilogue’s most inward miracle: a house caught between candle and demon, and at its heart not a wonder at all — only a woman, given back the time to say one sentence, saying it.

### The Liberation of Peter of Alife (Legend of Saint Francis, XXVIII)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-08.jpg" alt="Fresco from the Upper Church at Assisi, the last of the cycle, showing Saint Francis flying down from the upper left through a blue sky toward a rose-coloured prison tower at the right, crowned by a tall column with spiral reliefs; the freed prisoner Peter steps out of its door, stretching his arms. At the centre two jailers examine the broken fetters; at the left the kneeling, mitred Bishop of Tivoli raises his hands in astonishment before a group of clergy and attendants, beside a white tiered tower. A dark timber structure with a ladder hangs suspended in the sky above.">
  <figcaption>Attributed to the Saint Cecilia Master and workshop, after the design of the Legend’s master, Saint Francis Liberating the Prisoner Peter of Alife, Legend of Saint Francis, Scene XXVIII, c. 1290–1300. Fresco, c. 270 × 230 cm. Assisi, Basilica of San Francesco, Upper Church, nave.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Legend ends on a liberation, and the choice is the program’s last word. Bonaventure’s Miracula relate that a certain Peter, of Alife, accused of heresy and imprisoned at Rome, was entrusted by Gregory IX to the strict custody of the Bishop of Tivoli: chained, shut in a dark cell, and — the account insists — treated to the full rigour that the suspicion of heresy exacted. On the vigil of the feast of Saint Francis, fasting and in tears, he called upon the saint with his whole heart; and Francis, moved by the pure faith of the man, came down into the cell, called him by name, and bade him rise: the fetters broke, the doors stood open, and the prisoner, free, was so beside himself with wonder that his liberator seemed still before his eyes. The bishop, summoned, recognised the power of God and did reverence; the broken chains were carried to the pope and hung — the sources add — as an ex-voto. The first scene showed a layman recognising the saint before anyone; the last shows the saint answering a layman — and not a devotee of standing but a suspected heretic, the least promising client in Christendom. The Legend’s final doctrine is mercy’s reach: the advocate of popes, two scenes back, is the advocate of prisoners now, and the order’s founder exits his own cycle not enthroned but in flight, working.

The flight is the picture’s glory. Francis enters from the upper left, horizontal, habit streaming, arms extended toward the tower — the Legend’s only fully airborne saint, closing the series of apparitions with its most kinetic: the cycle that began with dreams admitted bodily into rooms ends with the dreamer of all those dreams crossing open sky like his own answered prayer. Below him the narrative unfolds left to right as cause, consternation and effect. At the left, the custodian’s party: the Bishop of Tivoli, mitred and coped, has gone down on his knees, hands flung up — the gaoler-prelate converted on the spot into a witness — with his clergy and household pressing behind, one cleric gripping the episcopal staff, under a white tiered tower that rises like a campanile of astonishment.

At the centre, the picture’s driest invention: the two jailers, turned from the prison, examine the broken fetters between them — professionals of custody puzzling over hardware that has failed against heaven, the Legend’s long gallery of uncomprehending experts (the physicians of Lerida, the priests of the sultan) receiving its final portraits. And at the right, the prison itself: a rose-coloured drum whose door stands open, and from it Peter steps out, arms stretched wide in the first gesture of a man measuring his freedom. Above the drum rises the scene’s famous landmark: a tall column wound with spiral reliefs — an unmistakable quotation of Trajan’s Column, the Roman topography compressed into one antiquarian emblem, so that the viewer knows at a glance in whose city, and under whose law, this liberation has been worked. Suspended in the sky hangs a dark timber frame with its ladder — read as the prison’s beam and stocks, torn loose and carried off, the instruments of restraint exhibited in mid-air like trophies — a detail whose precise construal remains open.

The execution belongs, with the whole of the closing scenes, to the Saint Cecilia Master, and nowhere is his hand more celebrated: the attenuated, dancing elegance of the flying saint, the pale ornamental towers, the blond key of the palette are his signature, and this field has always served connoisseurship as his touchstone within the cycle — the same refined intelligence that opened the Legend in the piazza of Assisi closing it in the Rome of the popes, the two city-portraits bracketing the whole. The design’s symmetries, however, are the program’s: prophecy answered by miracle, the first recognition by the last rescue; and the question of the designing mind — Giotto for the tradition and prevailing Italian scholarship, an anonymous master for the Offnerian line — is discussed in the section on the Assisi question above, which this final scene leaves exactly, and fittingly, open.

The condition is uneven: the blue survives across much of the sky, mottled and darkened, keeping the flight legible; abrasions wear the left-hand group and the prison drum, the suspended timber has darkened toward silhouette, and the gilt details of the column’s reliefs have dulled. The titulus below is fragmentary, and the scene passed the earthquake of 1997 without structural loss. It remains the perfect last word of the most consequential fresco cycle of the Middle Ages: a tower, a broken chain, a kneeling bishop — and the saint of Assisi, airborne over Rome, going to the one who called.

### Saint Margaret of Antioch and Stories from her Life: The Montici Dossal

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-02.jpg" alt="Gabled altarpiece: Saint Margaret of Antioch standing at the centre in a gold brocade mantle against a red cloth of honour, flanked by six scenes from her life in three registers — tending sheep at the encounter with Olibrius, her interrogation, her torture, the prison with the dragon seen through an iron grate, the torment by fire and water, and her beheading with angels bearing her soul to heaven">
  <figcaption>Saint Margaret of Antioch and Stories from her Life, c. 1310s, tempera and gold on panel, Arcetri (Florence), church of Santa Margherita a Montici.</figcaption>
</figure>

In the small hillside church of Santa Margherita a Montici, above Arcetri on the southern rim of Florence, the Saint Cecilia Master's art survives in its original setting as nowhere else. The gabled panel of the titular saint — flanked in the church by the *Maestà* given to the same hand — has stood here since at least the seventeenth century, and quite possibly since it was painted; a documentary notice of a 1660 donation by one Anton Francesco Ferroni, reported in the literature but never verified, is the only shadow over its provenance.

Criticism has counted it among the master's most secure works since the panels were first attributed to him in the early twentieth century, and most scholars place it at the end of his known development, around or shortly after 1310, when the slender, conventional figures of the Uffizi dossal have grown softer, suppler and more at ease in landscape and simple architecture.

The cusped format tells us it stood upright above an altar, and the narrative is arranged accordingly for a viewer reading the whole field at once: six scenes in three registers, read boustrophedon — forward, back, and forward again, like the furrows of a plough — following the *Golden Legend*'s account of the shepherdess of Antioch from her encounter with the prefect Olibrius to her beheading. Where the Cecilia panel edited its legend toward oratory and civic argument, the Montici dossal embraces the fabulous and the terrible: fully four of its six compartments are given to prison, dragon, torture and death, a choice that speaks of a popular devotional audience and of the cult that made Margaret, delivered unharmed from the dragon's belly, the great protectress of women in childbirth.

#### Saint Margaret Standing

The martyr stands full-length at the centre of the panel, filling the tall compartment beneath the gable like a column. Over a red robe she wears a vast mantle of gold brocade, its surface worked with a minute all-over pattern that turns the figure into a single shimmering plane; a crown rests on her veiled head, and in her hand she holds the green palm of martyrdom. Behind her hangs a cloth of honour of deep red, gathered at the upper corners into two swags of patterned drapery, so that the saint appears not enthroned, as Cecilia does on the Uffizi panel, but presented, displayed to her congregation like a relic within its shrine.

The choice of the standing figure is itself significant. The frontal, iconic *Stehfigur* flanked by stories belongs to the oldest stratum of Tuscan hagiographic panels — the Berlinghieri Francis dossals, the Margaret and Catherine panels of the Duecento — and the master adopts the venerable scheme while quietly modernising everything within it. The body beneath the brocade has weight and stance; the mantle falls in long, logical cascades to break over the feet; the pale oval face, with the small close-set eyes and long nose of his secure figures, is modelled with a new softness that criticism has long read as the mark of his mature phase.

Her gaze meets the viewer directly, as the Montici *Maestà*'s Virgin also does — a frontality of address rather than of archaism. For the women who climbed to this church to pray for safe delivery, the image offered exactly what the surrounding scenes promise: the saint who passed through the dragon's body unharmed, standing whole, crowned and incorruptible at the centre of her sufferings.

#### Margaret Tending the Sheep and the Encounter with Olibrius

The story opens in the open air — the only landscape on the panel, and one of the freshest passages in the master's whole surviving work. Green hills rise in soft, folded ridges against the sky; sheep graze along the slopes; and at the right stands Margaret, the nobleman's daughter raised by a Christian nurse after her pagan father cast her out, keeping her foster-mother's flock. From the left ride the agents of her destruction: the prefect Olibrius and his escort on horseback, travelling, as the *Golden Legend* has it, from Asia to Antioch, his attention caught by the beautiful shepherdess at the roadside.

The *Legenda aurea* compresses the fatal exchange into a few brutal lines: seeing her, Olibrius burned with desire and sent his servants to seize her — if she were free he would marry her, if a slave he would take her as concubine. The painter renders the moment before the violence, as a meeting of two worlds across the width of the compartment: the mounted party, worldly and in motion, and the standing girl, still and alone among her animals. The compositional grammar is that of the Cecilia panel's conversations, transposed from chamber to hillside.

What is new is the landscape itself. The rocky stages of the Uffizi martyrdom were bare and emblematic; here the hills are green, inhabited and almost genial, closer in spirit to the backgrounds of the master's late Assisi miracle scenes than to anything in Florentine panel painting of the moment. Criticism has repeatedly singled out this growing integration of figure and setting as the clearest measure of the distance travelled since the eponymous dossal — the "miniaturist tendency" at its most lyrical, telling a terrible story with the lightness of a romance.

#### Margaret before Olibrius

The narrative crosses the central figure to the upper right compartment, where the shepherdess, seized by the prefect's men, is brought to formal interrogation. The painter stages the scene within one of his characteristic urban assemblages — buildings in pale rose and cream, pierced by doorways and stepped rooflines, enclosing a shallow forecourt in which the confrontation unfolds. Margaret stands before the seated Olibrius, guarded, while he questions her from his place of authority.

The *Passio* and the *Golden Legend* cast the examination in the same forensic mould as Cecilia's trial: asked her name, lineage and religion, Margaret answers that she is noble by birth and Christian by faith, and to the prefect's astonishment that so beautiful a girl should worship "a crucified God," she replies with the paradox of the cross — crucified in weakness, living in power. The painter, as always, entrusts the exchange to posture and hands: the prefect's gesture of inquiry, the saint's upright refusal, the attendants' watchfulness.

Set directly opposite the pastoral encounter, the scene completes the panel's first register as a diptych of seeing and seizing: on the left Olibrius desires from horseback, on the right he possesses and is defied. From this point the story descends, literally and narratively, into the machinery of coercion that fills the middle and lower registers — and the viewer following the boustrophedon path turns back across the panel to find its first instrument directly below.

#### The Torture of Margaret

The middle register opens, on the right, with the first tortures. Refusing sacrifice, Margaret is stripped and raised up between her tormentors, her red garment blazing at the centre of the compartment while an executioner on either side goes about his work; the framing architecture — a tower at the left, a loggia-like structure at the right — converts a city square into a theatre of punishment. The *Golden Legend* specifies the double torment that the painter appears to condense into a single field: beaten first with rods, she was then scraped with iron combs to the bone, so that the blood poured from her body as from a spring, and Olibrius himself veiled his face, unable to look.

The hagiographer's detail is pointed: the persecutor cannot bear the sight he has ordered, and the crowd weeps at the ruin of such beauty, while the martyr alone remains serene, answering their pity with the panel's theology — the torment of the body is the salvation of the soul. The painter keeps faith with this economy of gazes. The slight, pale figure of the saint is the still centre; the violence is carried entirely by the bent, labouring bodies of the executioners, rendered with the quick descriptive energy that animates all his small figures.

Within the structure of the dossal this compartment begins the long central ordeal that the boustrophedon reading binds into a single movement: torture at the right, prison and dragon at the left, fire and water below. It is the register of Margaret's passion in the strict sense, and the painter grants it no landscape and no sky — only stone, instruments and the gold ground against which the tortured body stands like an inscription.

#### Margaret in Prison and the Dragon

The fourth scene is the heart of the panel and of the cult it served. The painter builds the prison as a massive block of masonry whose entire front dissolves into an iron grate, through which the viewer sees the cell as through the bars themselves — a device of startling directness, without parallel elsewhere in his work. Above, behind the grille, Margaret stands in prayer; below, in the darkness of the cell floor, coils the dragon, the demon who appeared to her, as the *Golden Legend* recounts, in the form of a monstrous serpent and swallowed her whole.

What follows made Margaret's fortune. Armed with the sign of the cross, she caused the dragon's body to burst and came forth unharmed — *illaesa exivit* — the episode that the legend itself flags with scholarly unease, noting that this swallowing and bursting is "apocryphal and not to be taken seriously," and that popular devotion embraced without reservation. Delivered intact from the belly of the beast, Margaret became the patroness of women in labour, invoked across Europe for safe delivery; her *Passio* was read, and her girdle laid, on the bodies of birthing mothers. The second combat of the prison — the demon in human form whom she seized, threw to the ground and set her foot upon — belongs to the same night.

The painter has given it the panel's most audacious formal invention: the barred screen that at once imprisons the saint and exposes her, making every viewer a witness at the grate. Prayer above, monster below, the composition is a vertical diagram of the trial itself — and of its outcome, already legible in the serene, upright orant who dominates her darkness.

#### The Torture by Fire and Water

Returned to the tribunal and still unbroken, Margaret undergoes the final torments, which the painter again gathers into a single compartment at the lower left. At its centre stands the great vessel, part cauldron and part font, into which the saint, stripped, has been plunged; flames are fed beneath and about her, an executioner labouring at the left while another figure attends at the right, and the architecture of gateways and towers once more closes the scene into a civic space. The *Golden Legend* is precise about the sequence: burned with blazing torches along her sides, she was then bound and cast into a vessel of water, "that the change of torment might increase the pain" — and at her prayer the earth shook, and she came forth unhurt before the eyes of the crowd.

The doubled element is the point. Fire could not consume what the dragon could not digest; water, meant to drown, becomes in the legend's own language a baptism, the saint praying that it be to her the laver of regeneration. The painter's cauldron, rising on its moulded base like a chalice, carries the sacramental overtone almost literally, and the viewer who has come from the Uffizi dossal will recognise the family resemblance to Cecilia's boiling bath — the same iconographic type, the martyr upright in the vessel of her torment, here transposed from bare rock to the ordered cruelty of the city.

The legend adds that five thousand believed at the sight and were beheaded for it, the first harvest of the martyr's constancy. Whether the subsidiary figures at the compartment's edges allude to these converts, or simply to the officers of the tribunal, remains open. With this scene the ordeal is complete, and the reading turns for the last time across the panel, to the sword and the angels.

#### The Beheading of Margaret and the Ascent of her Soul

The last compartment leaves the city for a wilderness of bare, faceted rock — the same elemental register in which the master set Cecilia's martyrdom — and divides itself between earth and heaven. Below, the sentence is carried out: Margaret kneels, haloed, awaiting the stroke of the executioner, while the legend's attendant figures complete the scene. Above, the panel's single irruption of the supernatural answers the angel of the Uffizi crowning: two angels, swathed in pale drapery against the gold, bear aloft between them the small crowned soul of the martyr, carried to the bridegroom she preferred to Olibrius.

The *Golden Legend* surrounds the execution with mercies and a final irony. Granted a pause for prayer, Margaret interceded for her persecutors, for those who would keep her memory, and — the clause that sealed her patronage — for women in childbirth who invoked her, that they might bring forth healthy children; a voice from heaven confirmed that her petitions were granted. The executioner, Malchus, begged her forgiveness and refused the blow until she commanded it as the condition of her crown; he struck once, and fell dead at her side. The figure collapsed or seated in the rocks of the lower compartment may belong to this episode.

As a conclusion the compartment binds the whole panel together. The diagonal of rock recalls the opening hillside, now stripped of its green and its sheep; the kneeling saint below and the crowned soul above restate, in narrative form, the double truth of the great central figure — the body given to the sword, the person standing whole and crowned. The boustrophedon path, begun among flocks at the upper left, ends at the lower right in the arms of angels: the furrow is complete, and the harvest is the image at the centre.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels and Two Female Saints (the Montici Maestà)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-11.jpg" alt="Gabled panel on a stone church wall: the Virgin in a dark mantle enthroned frontally on a rose architectural throne, holding the Christ Child in red, with two half-length angels leaning over the throne back with long striped wings, and two small female saints standing at the lower corners">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned with Two Angels and Two Female Saints, c. 1304–1310, tempera and gold on panel, Arcetri (Florence), church of Santa Margherita a Montici.</figcaption>
</figure>

The gabled panel presents the Virgin enthroned in the full frontal majesty of the Maestà type, scaled down from the civic monumentality of the great gallery examples to the devotional measure of a hillside parish. The throne is a massive architectural body of rose, ochre and red, its high straight back — the feature that ties the panel to late thirteenth-century Florentine practice, before the Gothic gable and the cosmatesque fantasy of the master’s later thrones — rising behind the Virgin’s shoulders between stout piers, its base projecting toward the viewer in broad, confidently drawn steps. A dark cushion softens the seat; the marbled platform below carries the whole structure like the podium of a small temple. Where the Pasadena and Accademia thrones dissolve into inlaid pattern, this one remains tectonic: the painter is still building furniture, not yet weaving it.

Over the throne’s back lean the panel’s most engaging invention: two half-length angels, symmetrical and attentive, their dark robes closing around jewelled collars, each resting a small vessel on the throne’s cornice while their long wings — banded white, red and black like a hoopoe’s — sweep down the gold on either side in great decorative curves. The motif of angels appearing behind the throne belongs to the Maestà tradition from Cimabue onward, but the master domesticates it: his angels do not hover in ranked choirs but lean in like attendants at a windowsill, and the enormous striped wings, flattened against the gold, turn the upper corners of the panel into fields of pure pattern. The vessels they present — phials, vases or censers — add a liturgical note whose precise sense remains open.

The Virgin herself is a single dark pyramid against the gold and rose. Her blue-green mantle descends from the veiled head in long unbroken falls, parting over the red-cuffed robe and breaking at the base into the angular, pooling folds the master loved; beneath its hem the rose lining flashes at the feet. Her head inclines almost imperceptibly toward the Child, but the gaze — as criticism has noted of precisely this panel — comes straight out at the beholder, level and grave, the pale long oval with small close-set eyes and minute mouth declaring the master’s hand at first sight. The Child, in a brilliant red robe that burns at the panel’s centre, sits high on her left arm, one hand raised toward his mother’s breast, the other extended across his lap; his halo is set directly against the throne’s back, so that the two figures and their architecture compress into a single emblematic body.

At the foot of the throne, at a drastically reduced scale, stand two small female saints, one at each corner of the platform — the feature that gives the panel its full title. The left-hand figure, veiled and mantled in rose over green, raises a hand in witness; the right-hand figure is now so darkened and abraded that her attributes can no longer be read. The photographic archive tradition identifies the pair as Saint Margaret — the church’s titular, whose presence here would bind the Maestà to its companion dossal across the aisle — and a second virgin martyr; the identification is plausible but is the archive’s, not legible fact. Their diminutive scale, which modern eyes read as naive, is of course hierarchic, and criticism has not spared the observation that their proportions sit oddly beside the ample Virgin — one of the marks by which the panel is placed early in the master’s development rather than late.

Critically, the Maestà and its companion have travelled together since the early twentieth century, when English connoisseurship first recorded both Montici panels under the master’s name; Offner retained them at the heart of his corpus, and the Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale counts them among the most reliable attributions. Within that secure pairing, however, the two panels divide the career between them: where the Saint Margaret dossal shows the supple, landscape-breathing manner of the 1310s, the Maestà keeps the straight-backed throne, the hieratic frontality and the archaising wings of an earlier moment, and Chiodo accordingly places it not much later than 1304 — a dating that finds documentary atmosphere, if not proof, in the indulgences granted to the Marian confraternity at Montici in 1296 and 1304, witness to an intense cult of the Virgin at the church in exactly these years. The panel’s surface, finally, asks for mention rather than silence: the broad vertical disturbance running down the right half and the darkening of the lower right corner, plainly visible in photographs, speak of old varnishes, candle smoke or past damage.

## The wider corpus: secure, probable and disputed works

Beyond the Uffizi panel, the Assisi scenes and the Montici pair, the corpus falls into several tiers of confidence.

### Saint Peter Enthroned

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-04.jpg" alt="Gabled panel: Saint Peter enthroned frontally in a green mantle and cross-marked pallium, blessing with his right hand and holding a book and a large gold key in his left, flanked by two standing angels resting their hands on the throne's arms, against a gold ground">
  <figcaption>Master of Saint Cecilia (attribution debated; also given to Lippo di Benivieni), Saint Peter Enthroned with Two Angels, dated 1307 (inscription recorded by the sources, no longer extant), tempera and gold on panel, Florence, church of Santi Simone e Giuda, from San Pier Maggiore.</figcaption>
</figure>

The great gabled panel now in Santi Simone e Giuda presents the Prince of the Apostles in full pontifical majesty. Peter sits frontally on a broad architectural throne of ochre and rose, its arms carried on slender colonnettes, its back and sides filled with the minute pierced and chequered inlay — cosmatesque ornament translated into pattern — that runs through the whole group of panels gathered around the Saint Cecilia Master. Over a grey-blue tunic he wears a deep green mantle that falls across his knees in long, weighty cascades, and over it the pallium, the Y-shaped band studded with dark crosses that declares his episcopal and papal dignity; his feet, in the apostle’s sandals, rest on the throne’s marbled step. The right hand rises in solemn blessing; the left, emerging from the folds, holds the red-bound book of his teaching and the great gold key of his office, bound with a red cord — the key of binding and loosing, displayed to the viewer like an instrument of state.

The head is the panel’s monumental centre: a massive, square-bearded Peter of the ancient Roman type, grey-haired with the apostle’s short curled fringe, his gaze level and frontal. The face is broader and more heavily structured than the small-featured oval native to the Cecilia Master’s secure figures, and this very gravity has fuelled the attributional debate around the work. Behind the throne, two angels — their wings folded close, visible at the shoulders — stand symmetrically at Peter’s sides, resting their crossed hands on the throne’s arms in attitudes of courtly attendance, their rose mantles over green robes answering the throne’s own chromatic chord. The composition as a whole revives, in early Trecento language, the venerable scheme of the enthroned Peter between angels that Roman panel and fresco tradition had consecrated.

The panel’s documentary importance exceeds that of any other work in the catalogue, for it alone carried a date. Painted for the Benedictine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedictine-order" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> nunnery church of San Pier Maggiore — whose titular it represents — it bore, according to the sources that transcribed it, the year 1307, and on that year the entire chronology of the Saint Cecilia Master’s Florentine activity has traditionally been hung. The inscription, however, can no longer be verified: the lower frame element that carried it was removed during a restoration in the 1980s, and the date has not been legible since the mid-twentieth century. The 1307 of every modern caption thus rests on earlier transcription — a circumstance that tempers the confidence with which the panel is used as a fixed point.

The attribution is the second open wound. The panel entered the master’s corpus early, added in the first decade of the twentieth century to the group forming around the Uffizi dossal, and Offner retained it. But since the 1980s an authoritative strand of Florentine scholarship has removed it, assigning it instead to Lippo di Benivieni on comparison with the Saint John the Baptist Enthroned at Christ Church, Oxford, and the Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale accordingly calls the attribution to the Cecilia Master anything but certain, placing both panels only in his circle. Chiodo has recently argued the contrary case, holding that the dense, shadow-built modelling of the Peter differs from Lippo’s delicate glazes and restoring the panel to the master himself. The question remains genuinely open, and the honest formula is that of a probable work, central to one reconstruction of the career and marginal to another.

On its resolution hangs more than a single panel, for the Saint Peter is the hinge of the Gaddo Gaddi hypothesis. A document of January 1328 records a payment to “Gaddo dipintore” for making braccioli — probably lateral elements — at the altar of Saint Peter in San Pier Maggiore; the inference that a painter of standing would accept so modest a task only on an altarpiece of his own making, joined to the attribution of this panel to the Cecilia Master, is the entire bridge by which the anonymous master acquires his conjectural name. If the Peter is Lippo’s, the bridge falls. The panel that once anchored the chronology thus now anchors the identity question as well. It remained in San Pier Maggiore until the church’s demolition in the eighteenth century, passing to the nearby Santi Simone e Giuda, where it stands today over its altar: the one work of the group still serving, in a Florentine church, something like its original function.

### Madonna and Child

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-10.jpg" alt="Gabled panel: half-length Virgin in a dark blue-green mantle with a gold star at the brow, holding the Christ Child in a pale translucent tunic who twists upward reaching for her mantle, two small inscribed plaques in the gold ground beside her head">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, c. 1290–1295 (Getty Museum dating; recent criticism proposes c. 1300 or later), tempera and gold leaf on panel, 85 × 66 cm, Los Angeles, J. Paul Getty Museum, acc. no. 2000.35, formerly Contini Bonacossi collection.</figcaption>
</figure>

The gabled panel presents the Virgin and Child at half length, filling the field against an unbroken gold ground — a work that, like the Horne Triptych and the Madonna delle Grazie, dispenses with the painted throne altogether. The Virgin’s deep blue-green mantle, marked at the brow with a radiant gold star and edged with a fine gold fillet, envelops head and shoulders in a single dark silhouette; beneath it show the red undercap gathered in rippling folds about the face and the red, gold-fringed cuffs at the wrists. The composition thus belongs to the venerable half-length icon type of Byzantine descent, the Hodegetria family rephrased in Florentine terms, and the two small dark plaques suspended in the gold at either side of her head, bearing gold lettering, carry the Virgin’s identifying sigla — their exact reading, whether the Greek ΜΡ ΘΥ or a Latin formula, cannot be fixed from reproduction.

Within this ancient scheme the painter stages a moment of surprising liveliness. The Child, dressed in a pale lilac-white tunic whose translucency recalls the veiled Christ of the Accademia panel, does not sit in hieratic profile but twists upward in his mother’s arm, his left leg swinging free, his bare feet crossing, one hand reaching for her mantle at the breast — the clinging, climbing motion of a real infant seeking its mother’s face. The Virgin’s long right hand, with the attenuated fingers that run through all the master’s figures, closes gently around the Child’s reaching arm. Her gaze, however, does not answer his: it passes over him, outward and aside, with the grave abstraction of one who knows what the beholder knows. The affective contrast — the Child all present tenderness, the mother already elsewhere — is the panel’s quiet drama, and it is managed entirely with the master’s native vocabulary: the pale long oval of the face, the small close-set eyes, the minute mouth.

The faces indeed offer the purest statement of that physiognomy anywhere in the catalogue, uncomplicated by repainting or by mural technique. The Virgin’s head, with its high smooth brow under the star, its long straight nose and its small grave mouth, is the very type recovered by radiography beneath the repainted face of the Uffizi Cecilia; the Child’s russet curls and large-browed head rework the same unchildlike gravity that the Accademia infant displays. Criticism has also connected the panel’s archaising elements — the half-length format, the linear gold edging of the mantle — with the late Duecento masters among whom the painter must have formed, and Chiodo has pointed specifically toward the [Master of Varlungo](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Maestro-di-Varlungo.html) and [Grifo di Tancredi](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Grifo-di-Tancredi.html) as his nearest points of departure.

The panel’s date is the most consequential open question in the master’s chronology. The Getty, following the dating advanced in the Offner–Boskovits Corpus volume of 1984, catalogues the work at about 1290–1295, which would make it the earliest surviving product of the career and the principal evidence that the painter was already active, fully formed, before the Assisi campaign. Chiodo has argued the contrary: observing that the composition depends closely on Giotto’s Badia polyptych, she finds it difficult to place the panel before that work, which would pull it toward 1300 and dissolve the documentary value of the early date. The disagreement is not a detail, for on it hangs the shape of the master’s beginnings — whether he came to Assisi as an independent Duecento painter or emerged there within Giotto’s first workshop. Both positions are recorded and neither is adopted.

One material circumstance should accompany any reading of the image. Chiodo notes that the panel is a fragment of a larger dossal that originally carried two saints on each side of the Virgin — a horizontal ensemble of the same family as the Uffizi and Montici panels, here reduced by cutting to its central compartment. The gabled silhouette and framing mouldings would then belong to the panel’s later history rather than its first design, and the iconic isolation that makes the image so effective on the museum wall is, at least in part, an accident of survival. The panel reached the Getty in 2000 from the Contini Bonacossi collection, where the mid-century connoisseurship that first shaped the master’s catalogue had already recorded it; it remains his one secure work in an American public collection apart from the Pasadena Madonna, and the two panels together frame the career — if the Getty’s early date holds — from its Duecento threshold to its Trecento close.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Saint Francis

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-03.jpg" alt="Gabled panel: the Virgin in a dark gold-edged mantle seated on a throne of red, white and green geometric inlay, holding the Christ Child in red who grasps a small bird, while Saint Francis kneels in prayer at the lower right">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned, with Saint Francis, c. 1315, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 126.4 × 68.9 cm, Pasadena, Norton Simon Museum, inv. M.1994.2.P.</figcaption>
</figure>

The gabled panel in Pasadena shows the Cecilia Master — if the now-prevailing reconstruction of his late activity is accepted — in the most intimate register of his art. The Virgin sits on a high-backed throne whose architecture is rendered not in the pale rose masonry of the narrative panels but as a dazzling geometric abstraction: bands of red, white and grey-green chevrons and stripes fold around the seat and its stepped platform, flanked by pilasters bearing slender red vases at their summits. It is fictive cosmatesque inlay translated into pure pattern, and it wraps the figures in an optical energy that the gold ground above holds in check. Two red cushions flare at the Virgin’s sides like the wings of the throne itself.

Against this chromatic architecture the Virgin is a single dark monumental form. Her deep blue-black mantle, edged in gold, falls from her veiled head in long unbroken cascades to pool over the pink step beneath her feet, parting to reveal the rose-red robe at her breast and knee. The pale oval face, with the small close-set eyes, long nose and diminutive mouth that technical study has confirmed as the master’s native physiognomy, inclines gently toward the Child, but her gaze slips past him toward the viewer — the direct frontal address that criticism has noted equally in the Montici Maestà. The hands are the painter’s best instruments here as in his narratives: the right rises in a gesture at once of presentation and of delicate restraint before the Child’s breast.

The Child, a robust russet-haired infant in a red tunic, sits high on his mother’s arm and performs two actions at once. With his right hand he blesses — or reaches toward the Virgin’s face in the affectionate motion the Trecento increasingly favoured — while in his left he grasps a small dark-winged bird, toward which the Virgin’s open hand hovers. The bird, conventionally read as a goldfinch, carries its familiar double sense: a child’s pet, and the creature whose red-stained face associated it with the Passion, so that the infant’s plaything becomes a quiet prolepsis of the Crucifixion. The identification of the species is conventional, and the symbolic reading rests on it.

At the lower right, kneeling on the rosy pavement at a fraction of the sacred figures’ scale, Saint Francis raises his joined hands toward the Child. The grey-brown habit, knotted cord and tonsure identify him beyond doubt. His presence does the work of a donor portrait by proxy: whether the panel was commissioned for a Franciscan church or by a lay devotee bound to the order, the saint models the beholder’s own posture of petition, establishing the diagonal of intercession — viewer, Francis, Child, Virgin — that gives this hieratic image its devotional circuitry. The original destination is unknown.

The panel’s place in the catalogue is itself a chapter of the master’s critical history. It entered the modern literature under the name of the Master of the Horne Triptych, the anonymous painter around whom Offner had grouped a cluster of small-scale Florentine works of the 1310s and 1320s, and it was still sold under that name in London in 1975. The merger of that personality into the Cecilia Master’s late activity, argued decisively by Boskovits in 2003, brought the Pasadena Madonna into the corpus along with the Horne triptych and the Accademia and Pian di Mugnone Maestà panels, and the museum now catalogues it under the master’s name with a date of about 1315. The attribution therefore stands or falls with that reconstruction — probable, widely followed, but not independently documented — and the panel is best presented as a secure work of the Horne group and, through it, of the Cecilia Master’s final decade: the narrative inventor of the Uffizi dossal here distilled into a single, patterned, tenderly watchful icon.

In Florence the late corpus includes three further works. The first is the *Maestà with six angels* from the oratory of Santa Maria Maddalena at Pian di Mugnone, on deposit from the Museo di San Marco and shown in the Uffizi near the Cecilia panel (230 × 150 cm, framed in the fifteenth century by Bicci di Lorenzo). The second is the *Madonna Enthroned* in the Galleria dell'Accademia, of unknown provenance and long on deposit at Pescia. The third is the triptych of the *Virgin and Child with two female saints* in the Museo Horne. Offner gave both *Maestà* panels to his Horne Triptych Master. Chiodo places the Accademia *Madonna* close to 1315 and the Pian di Mugnone *Maestà* slightly later. These are probable attributions within the reconstruction that merges the Horne Triptych Master into the Saint Cecilia Master, not independently secured ones.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Six Angels (the Pian di Mugnone Maestà)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-12.jpg" alt="Large panel in a gilded Gothic tabernacle frame: the Virgin in a dark mantle with a gold sunburst at the shoulder enthroned with the Christ Child in red, surrounded by six angels — four leaning on the throne, two below swinging censers — with a fifteenth-century Annunciation painted in the frame's upper spandrels">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned with Six Angels, c. 1315–1320, tempera and gold on panel, 230 × 150 cm, from the oratory of Santa Maria Maddalena at Pian di Mugnone; Florence, Museo di San Marco (on deposit at the Gallerie degli Uffizi); fifteenth-century frame attributed to the workshop of Bicci di Lorenzo, with the Annunciation in the spandrels.</figcaption>
</figure>

The largest surviving devotional panel of the group presents the Virgin enthroned amid a full angelic court, six attendants ranked three to a side — the master’s most ambitious essay in the Maestà type and the closest his surviving panels come to the great gallery altarpieces of Cimabue, [Duccio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Duccio-di-Buoninsegna.html) and Giotto that the Uffizi now hangs within sight of it. The throne is of the mature family: a polygonal structure whose faceted back, inlaid with slender ornamental bands, opens like a shallow apse around the Virgin, its wings angled forward to carve a real pocket of space out of the gold.

The angels do not float in the Byzantine manner but stand and lean within that space — the upper pairs resting their hands on the throne’s back and arms with the proprietary ease of courtiers, exactly as in the Montici Maestà, while the foremost pair stand full-length on the pavement below, swinging censers on long chains, deacons of a celestial liturgy. The graduated scale and overlapping of the six figures, screened behind and before the throne’s architecture, show the painter working out in his own idiom the spatial lesson of Giotto’s Ognissanti Madonna, which criticism has long regarded as the gravitational centre of all Florentine Maestà design in these years.

The Virgin herself is the grandest of the master’s surviving figures. The deep blue-green mantle, fastened with a radiant gold sunburst at the shoulder — the star of the Stella maris grown into a blazing ornament — descends in long, weighty, beautifully logical cascades over both knees to break on the pavement at her feet; the red robe with its gold-embroidered collar and cuffs shows at throat and wrist. The head, inclined ever so slightly toward the Child, carries the native physiognomy at monumental scale: the long pale oval, the small close-set eyes with their level outward gaze, the small grave mouth, here modelled with a breadth and softness that mark the mature phase. The Child, in brilliant red worked with gold, performs the double action familiar from the late Madonnas: he twists on his mother’s knee, one bare foot escaping the hem, raising his right hand toward her face while she steadies him with the long-fingered, strangely elegant hands that are the master’s signature — maternal furniture and theological throne at once.

The two censing angels of the foreground deserve particular attention, for they carry the panel’s liturgical meaning. Dressed not in the dark courtly robes of their companions but in pale rose albs — the vestment of the altar server — they stand turned inward, gazing up at the Virgin, each lowering a gold thurible on its chains toward the pavement beside the throne’s base. The motif translates the incensation of the altar into the picture itself: the painted Madonna receives, perpetually, the honour the officiating clergy paid her image below. The two bowed, censer-bearing figures, among the most gracious he ever painted, give this largest of his Madonnas the character of a Mass eternally in progress.

The ensemble as the visitor now sees it is a palimpsest of two centuries. The gilded tabernacle frame, with its crocketed ogival arch, twisted colonnettes and projecting cornice, is a fifteenth-century work attributed to the ambit of Bicci di Lorenzo, and the two figures in the upper spandrels — the angel Gabriel alighting at the left, the Virgin Annunciate in blue against a punched crimson field at the right — belong to that later campaign, not to the Cecilia Master: a Quattrocento Annunciation set like a commentary above a Trecento Maestà. An inscription runs along the frame’s base. The reframing itself is a document of the panel’s continued cult a century after it was painted: frame and panel are two voices.

The panel’s history follows the now-familiar arc of the late group. It came from the oratory of Santa Maria Maddalena at Pian di Mugnone, in the hills above Fiesole; it entered the state collections through the Museo di San Marco, on whose deposit it travelled; and it was shown at the Mostra Giottesca of 1937 still labelled as an anonymous Florentine, before Offner assigned it to his Master of the Horne Triptych and the Boskovits reconstruction of 2003 carried it, with that whole personality, into the Saint Cecilia Master’s final phase. Chiodo places it slightly later than the Accademia Madonna, toward the end of the 1310s, which would make it — with the Santa Maria Novella murals — among the last large statements of the career. Its present display in the Uffizi, within sight of the Cecilia dossal, quietly stages the whole argument of the reconstructed catalogue: the nimble narrator of the eight Cecilia scenes and the solemn author of this six-angeled court as one painter, seen at the two ends of a working life.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-09.jpg" alt="Gabled panel: the Virgin in a dark star-marked mantle seated on a throne with rose-red inlaid wings, holding the Christ Child in a pale tunic and translucent veil, who displays an inscribed Latin scroll and bears a red cross in his halo">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned, c. 1315, tempera and gold on panel, Florence, Galleria dell’Accademia; provenance unknown, formerly on deposit at Pescia.</figcaption>
</figure>

The tall gabled panel presents the Virgin and Child in the same hieratic key as the Pasadena Madonna, but with a graver, more monumental inflection. The throne is once again the master’s fictive cosmatesque furniture: tall lateral wings in rose and red, their recessed panels filled with minute chequered and pierced inlay, converge on a polygonal back whose gable — echoing the panel’s own cusp — frames the Virgin’s head against a field of patterned ornament. The seat below opens into small trefoiled arcades over marbled infill, and a green-and-red lozenge-patterned cloth spills over its edge at the left: the painter’s architecture, as always, is less a description of a possible object than a chromatic engine, surrounding the dark figure of the Virgin with the glitter of an inlaid shrine.

The Virgin fills the panel as a single columnar mass. Her blue-black mantle, starred at brow and shoulder and lined in green with red borders, descends unbroken from the veiled head to pool over the stepped base, opening only at the breast on the rose-red robe and the white veil that slips from beneath the hood. The face is the master’s own — the pale long oval, small close-set eyes, long straight nose and small mouth now confirmed by radiography as his native type on the Uffizi panel — but handled with the breadth and the soft, continuous modelling of his mature phase. Her gaze turns markedly aside toward the Child, while the head remains nearly frontal: that slight torsion, together with the elongated hand spread across the Child’s knee in a gesture of presentation rather than grasp, gives the icon its quiet interior movement.

The Child is the panel’s theological voice. Seated high on his mother’s left arm, he wears a pale rose tunic under a translucent white veil drawn across his legs — a velum whose gauzy transparency is a small tour de force of the brush — and his halo is charged with a bold red cross. With both hands he displays an unfurled scroll inscribed in red-touched Gothic letters, which may be read In ulnis matris manet hic sap(ient)ia patris: “here in the arms of the mother abides the Wisdom of the Father.” The verse makes explicit what the image enacts. This is the Christ-Logos as Sapientia Patris, the Sedes Sapientiae theme turned from metaphor into caption: the Virgin’s lap as the throne of Wisdom, doubled by the painted throne around her.

The scroll also marks the panel’s distance from the Pasadena Madonna, its closest sibling in the late group. There the Child played with a bird and the mood was domestic; here he neither blesses nor plays but teaches, holding out his text to the beholder with the solemnity of the adult Logos, his large-browed, unchildlike head — a deliberate archaism of the Wisdom iconography — set off by the cruciform nimbus. The pairing of the two panels shows the master’s late workshop adjusting a single compositional scheme, the dark pyramidal Virgin on the inlaid throne, to two distinct devotional temperatures; and it is precisely this seriality of the late Madonnas that persuaded criticism to gather them, with the Horne triptych and the Pian di Mugnone Maestà, into one closing phase of the career.

The attributional history follows the now-familiar path. The panel belonged to the constellation that Offner assembled around the Master of the Horne Triptych, and it entered the Saint Cecilia Master’s catalogue when Boskovits’s reconstruction absorbed that personality into the older master’s final decade — a merger argued decisively in 2003 and now generally followed, though it remains a critical construction rather than a documented fact. Chiodo places the Accademia Madonna close to 1315, at the head of the late devotional group, and the provenance is recorded as unknown: a reminder that even the master’s most imposing surviving icon reached the modern collections stripped of its altar, its church and its patrons, carrying as its only document the sentence in the Child’s hands.

### Madonna and Child between Two Female Saints (the Horne Triptych)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-13.jpg" alt="Triptych of three gabled gold-ground panels: at the centre the Virgin in a dark mantle cradles the Christ Child, wrapped in translucent white, almost horizontally in both arms as he reaches up toward her; on the left a veiled female saint holds a red book, on the right a female saint holds a martyr's palm">
  <figcaption>Master of Saint Cecilia (the “Master of the Horne Triptych”), Madonna and Child between Two Female Saints, 1310s, tempera and gold on panel, 120 × 160 cm (overall), Florence, Museo Horne.</figcaption>
</figure>

The small triptych in the Museo Horne is the keystone of the late catalogue in the most literal sense: it is the work from which the “Master of the Horne Triptych” took his name, and therefore the object around which the entire question of the Saint Cecilia Master’s final phase has always turned. Three gabled panels of nearly equal height stand side by side, each bordered by its own simple raised moulding, the silhouette of the ensemble rising and falling like a miniature mountain range — a portable, chamber-scaled echo of the great cusped dossals, built not for a high altar but for close devotion, whether in a convent cell, a sacristy or a private house. Its original destination is unrecorded, and the triptych reached its present home with Herbert Percy Horne’s collection, housed since 1921 in the palazzo on Via de’ Benci that the English collector left to the Italian state.

The central panel gives the group its most individual image. The Virgin, in the dark green-blue mantle over a red robe that runs through every Madonna of the series, does not display the Child upright on her arm in the hieratic manner of the Accademia or Pasadena panels: she holds him low and almost horizontally across her body, cradled in both hands, his small form — wrapped in the pale, translucent white that the master reserved for the infant Christ — stretched out like a figure at once nursed and offered. The Child twists in the cradle of her arms, one bare leg extended, one arm thrown up toward his mother’s face in the reaching, clinging motion already seen in the Getty Madonna; a narrow red sash or band slips beneath him, a fluttering accent against the white. The pose carries its familiar double sense: a mother dandling a restless infant, and the Christ laid out as he will one day be laid upon the altar and in the tomb — the eucharistic undertone that the horizontal presentation of the Child always awakens, here left implicit, with the gentleness that marks the painter’s whole devotional register.

The Virgin’s head above this busy bundle is among the most refined statements of the late facial type: the long pale oval inclined toward the Child, the small close-set eyes, the long nose and minute mouth, the white veil slipping from beneath the mantle’s hood — the physiognomy of the Uffizi side-scenes and the recovered first face of Cecilia, softened by two decades of practice into something almost tender. The two lateral panels answer her with standing female saints, each filling her gable like a column, each wrapped in a rose-red mantle worked with minute gold ornament over which the long late folds fall unbroken. The left-hand saint, veiled, holds a red-bound, gold-studded book in both raised hands; the right-hand saint, her hair loose beneath a light veil, carries the martyr’s palm. Neither bears a more specific attribute legible in reproduction, and the museum’s own label commits itself to no names — “Madonna and Child between two female saints” is the honest title, and the saints are better left unnamed. Book and palm make them a learned virgin and a virgin martyr; which of the many candidates the first owners saw in them, only a lost context could say.

As a structure the triptych distils the master’s late manner to its essentials. There is no throne, no architecture, no narrative — the three figures stand or sit directly against the gold, and the whole burden of the design falls on silhouette, fold and interval. Precisely this reduction made the work a touchstone: when early twentieth-century criticism, sorting the panels gathered around the Cecilia dossal, found here a softer, more languid inflection of the familiar types, it cut the knot by inventing a second painter. Offner codified the division, assigning the new master the Pescia Madonna, the Pian di Mugnone Maestà and other panels; the state catalogue’s entry for a related work characterises the personality by exactly the traits visible here — the languid wide-irised eyes, the full-contoured lips, the pointed chin — and notes that his works fall mostly in the second decade of the Trecento, just as the Cecilia Master’s secure activity closes in the first. It was that chronological dovetailing, joined to the near-identity of types, that led Boskovits to his solution: not two painters but one, the Horne group being the final phase of the Saint Cecilia Master himself — the reconstruction argued definitively in his 2003 article and now generally followed.

Seen from the end of the series, then, the little triptych is less a minor devotional object than the hinge of a historiographical argument. If the merger holds, the painter of the eight brisk Cecilia scenes and of the Assisi miracles closed his career here, with everything that once defined him pared away. Whether one reads that as exhaustion or as distillation is a matter of temperament; what the triptych itself shows is a hand still unmistakably the same, the long fingers, the pale ovals, the logic of the falling folds, carried to a stillness that the young painter of the wedding-feast at Santa Cecilia might not have recognised as his own future. It hangs today in the first room of the museum’s second floor, a few steps from the [Simone Martini](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Simone-Martini.html) and [Lippo Memmi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Lippo-Memmi.html) diptych — Florence and Siena, the two roads of the early Trecento, facing each other across one small room.

### The Madonna delle Grazie (Oratorio di Santa Maria delle Grazie)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia/maestro-di-santa-cecilia-14.jpg" alt="Arched detached fresco in a gilded frame: the crowned Virgin with a great rayed halo and white veil, in rose-red robes, holds the Christ Child across her lap as he twists away raising his right hand in blessing, while two angels behind hold up a red and green lozenge-patterned cloth of honour against a dark blue ground">
  <figcaption>Master of Saint Cecilia (attributed), Madonna delle Grazie (Madonna and Child with Two Angels), c. 1313(?), detached fresco, Florence, Oratorio di Santa Maria delle Grazie, Lungarno Diaz; from the tabernacle on the first pier of the Ponte Rubaconte, later called Ponte alle Grazie after this image.</figcaption>
</figure>

The arched fresco in the oratory on the Lungarno Diaz is unlike anything else gathered under the Saint Cecilia Master’s name, in function if not in form: not an altarpiece but a street tabernacle image, painted for the first pier of the Ponte Rubaconte, where a shrine housing it is documented from 1313. The devotion the image attracted was such that it renamed its own bridge — the Rubaconte became, and remains, the Ponte alle Grazie — and when the old pier chapels were cleared away in the later nineteenth century, the fresco was detached and carried to the present oratory, built in 1874 by the architect Vittorio Bellini for the Mori Ubaldini in the garden of Palazzo Alberti-Malenchini, on ground the Alberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-alberti-family" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> had held since the Duecento. The tradition connecting the commission itself to the Alberti, whose houses faced the bridgehead, is reported by the literature on the oratory and belongs to the plausible rather than the proven.

The image is a Maestà reduced to its devotional essentials, cut by the arch at the Virgin’s knees. She sits frontally, crowned above her white veil — the crown, rare in the master’s panel Madonnas, befits a civic image of the Regina coeli watching over the river traffic — and around the crowned head spreads a great halo of modelled golden rays, a sunburst that reads from a distance as sheer radiance. A transparent veil falls over the rose-red robe and mantle; the long right hand, with the attenuated fingers familiar from the whole series, steadies the Child across her lap. Behind the group two half-length angels, their heads inclined, hold up a cloth of honour patterned with red and green lozenges against the deep blue ground — the humble painted counterpart of the inlaid thrones of the panels, and a motif that turns the pier of a bridge into a presence-chamber.

The Child gives the image its civic meaning. Seated across his mother’s knees in a gold-ochre wrap, he twists away from her, his head in profile and his right arm raised, extended toward the edge of the field — the gesture that the oratory’s own tradition reads as the Child blessing the city: from the tabernacle’s original position on the bridge, his raised hand faced the houses and the river of Florence. Whether the gesture was designed as a blessing over the city or acquired that reading from its placement is exactly the kind of question a street image generates; the fresco’s fame rested on the second, whatever the painter intended by the first.

The attribution asks for honest framing. The fresco entered the master’s literature with a proposal of 1972, recorded by the Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale among the attributed works, and it circulates today under his name and on the oratory’s own documentation, usually with the qualifier “attributed.” Unlike the Uffizi dossal, the Montici panels or even the Horne group, it has not been consolidated by the subsequent monographic literature, and Chiodo’s recent synthesis does not place it among the works she discusses; it therefore stands a tier below the core catalogue — a plausible attribution, resting on the facial types (the long pale oval, the small mouth, the heavy-lidded close-set eyes are all compatible) and on the c. 1313 anchor of the tabernacle, which falls squarely within the master’s documented span.

Against this must be set the image’s material history: a fresco exposed for centuries to weather, floods and candle smoke, detached, transferred and repeatedly restored — the oratory suffered grave damage in the war and again in the 1966 flood — so that the present surface, with its hardened contours and strikingly dark-rimmed eyes, is a palimpsest in which the fourteenth-century hand must be read through much later intervention. For the conservation history, the 2011 volume on the oratory, which includes a study of the fresco, is the first place to look.

Whatever its final attributional fate, the Madonna delle Grazie earns its place in the dossier as testimony of a kind the panels cannot give. The altarpieces show what the master (or his circle) painted for churches; this image shows the same visual language working in the open street, at the scale of civic devotion — a crowned Virgin and a blessing Child set where every Florentine crossing the Arno would pass beneath them. Its fortune, the one work in the catalogue whose public loved it enough to rename a piece of Florence, is one no signed panel of the period can match.

Illumination forms a further chapter. Gradual D, written for the Cistercians of San Salvatore at Settimo, carries a subscription recording its completion in 1315 by the monk Vincenzo Monocolo; it is now in the library of San Bernardo alle Terme in Rome. In 1984 Boskovits cautiously recognised the master's hand in twelve of its fifty-three historiated initials, and Chiodo treats the 1315 subscription as an ante quem.

In 2024 she added a Dominican antiphonary sold at auction in Cologne in 2015, with ten historiated initials assigned to the painter's earliest Florentine years. In the same article she reconstructed a polyptych from a privately owned *Virgin and Child* and two panels in the Museo diocesano of Livorno, a Saint Nicholas of Tolentino and a deacon saint, previously given to the Horne Triptych Master. She proposed a date before the 1325 canonisation process of Nicholas and a possible provenance from Santo Spirito in Florence. These proposals have not yet been tested by the wider scholarly community.

Mural painting rounds out the catalogue. Besides the Santa Maria Novella frescoes already mentioned, there are fragmentary remains in the Rucellai chapel, documented as executed between 1303 and 1325. There is also a large fresco of *Saint Thomas Aquinas Enthroned*, discovered behind the fourth altar of the left aisle of Santa Maria Novella and published in 2020 with an attribution to the master; it is dated at or shortly before Thomas's canonisation in 1323. Chiodo also gives him the Michael scenes in the heavily repainted Velluti chapel in Santa Croce, which Boskovits had given to the Master of the Corsi Crucifix, and moves their date to the mid-1310s on a rereading of Donato Velluti's chronicle. She further assigns him, under Giotto's supervision, a painted architectural system in the upper walls of Ognissanti. Each of these is a hypothesis of varying strength; none is documented.

Some attributions are superseded or isolated. A *Madonna* in the oratory of Santa Maria delle Grazie was proposed in 1972. The Roman frescoes depend on the hypothesis of a Roman formation: a share in Cavallini's work at Santa Cecilia in Trastevere, and the Saint Catherine cycle from Sant'Agnese fuori le mura now in the Vatican Pinacoteca. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale notes that their "Cavallinian derivation" must be understood "in a rather broad sense". Chiodo has removed a painted cross at San Donato in Val di Botte from the catalogue.

## Style, narrative method and spatial experiment

Descriptions of the master's style have been shaped from the beginning by comparison with Giotto, often producing a negative definition. The enduring characterisations are worth restating, with the caution that some were formed on the repainted central figure of the Uffizi panel.

The most consistent observation concerns architecture. In the Cecilia scenes the painter builds imaginary rooms and buildings with superimposed storeys, exedrae, vaults, niches and balconies, within which the figures are placed with a precise sense of where they stand. The technical study confirms that these architectures were laid out with incised lines before the figures were drawn, and that at least one was redesigned during execution.

Offner saw the eponymous panel as the more archaic phase, with space bounded by architectural wings, and the Saint Margaret panel as the mature phase. There, he wrote, the architecture "undergoes a reduction in scale and shape to almost abstract simplicity, in order that the eye may pass more freely into the space, and immerse itself in its lyrical qualities." Chiodo adds the throne of the Cecilia panel: its foreshortened seat, moulded arms, inlays and statuettes of kneeling angels reflect research in Giotto's workshop around 1300 in which, she argues, the master played a leading part.

The figures have been judged more critically. The persistent formula speaks of tall, slender figures with small heads, elongated and conventional, still tied to the Duecento. The original face of Cecilia recovered by radiography matches the Assisi types and the side scenes, and Chiodo notes the painter's tendency to soften and rebalance these forms over time. His faces, in her words, are "always lacking a true bone structure". In the Montici *Saint Margaret* they become "elastic silhouettes" that are "immune to the psychological depth" of Giotto's figures: feelings "are recited rather than lived". That judgement reflects a modern preference for Giotto's psychological drama, but it describes something real. The master's narratives are clear, legible and lively, built on readable gesture and compact groups rather than on the charged silences of Padua.

His narrative method also involves choosing content. The comparison between the Cecilia and Margaret panels suggests a painter who adapted format, reading order and selection of episodes to setting and patrons: oratory and conversion for a civic audience in one, the fabulous and the martyrdom for popular devotion in the other. The *Saint Thomas* fresco, if correctly attributed and dated around 1323, shows him late in his career setting figures one behind another on two levels of benches, as in a real school of the period. Chiodo judges this an original idea, though the space is implausible and the foreshortening excessive. In her view it shows a painter still engaged with Giottesque research into illusionistic space but no longer able to follow its evolution.

## Giotto, Rome and Florence around 1300

The question of the master's training has received three answers. The first, now largely abandoned, holds that he was formed in Rome, collaborating with Pietro Cavallini at Santa Cecilia in Trastevere; the classicising features of his architecture were cited in support. Its extreme form was a 1994 proposal that the master was Cavallini himself, which would have validated a Florentine activity of Cavallini reported only by Vasari in 1568. It has found little acceptance. The Treccani encyclopedia's brief entry still describes him as dependent "on the Roman school and on the Florentine school of the thirteenth century" and influenced by Giotto.

The second view, Offner's, made him an alternative to Giotto, the head of a "miniaturist tendency" running parallel to Giotto's monumentality. The third, now dominant, sees him as rising from within Giotto's earliest entourage. Chiodo states this directly against Offner: for Offner the tendency was an alternative to Giotto's painting, whereas "the personality of the Master of Saint Cecilia springs from the closest Giottesque entourage of the first hour". In this reading, the master was the trusted collaborator who completed Assisi and then ran the Florentine workshop while Giotto moved between Florence, Rome, Assisi and Padua. Chiodo's Paragone article calls him Giotto's "principal interlocutor". These claims are interpretive. They depend on accepting the Assisi attribution, the Ognissanti architecture and the early dating of the Cecilia panel.

Between these poles remains the evidence of his Duecento roots. Chiodo herself connects the Getty *Madonna* with the Master of Varlungo and Grifo di Tancredi, and notes that the master never adopted the three-quarter view of the Virgin Giotto had already used in the Ognissanti *Maestà*. On her account he ran "parallel" to Giotto "but at a different speed".

## Proposed identifications and the anonymity question

Four named painters have been proposed as the master's true identity. They are best assessed in order of evidential strength.

The weakest is Buffalmacco. The identification rested on attributing to the master works at Badia a Settimo that the sources give to Buffalmacco. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale records it in writings of 1908 and 1924, and Offner dismissed it in 1927. The proposal belongs to the historiography of the master, not to the live debate. Buffalmacco is now reconstructed chiefly through the Camposanto frescoes at Pisa, a body of work entirely distinct from the Saint Cecilia Master's.

The second is Stefano Fiorentino, praised by early sources as Giotto's most gifted pupil but without any securely identified surviving work. The state catalogue attributes this proposal to a scholar writing shortly after 1903. It has no documentary anchor.

The third is Pietro Cavallini, the 1994 proposal already discussed, which has found no general acceptance.

The fourth and strongest is Gaddo Gaddi. In 1983 a Florentine archival study published a document of January 1328 (1327 in the Florentine style) recording a payment to "Gaddo dipintore" for making "braccioli" at the altar of Saint Peter in San Pier Maggiore. These were probably lateral arms or wing elements, and Chiodo gives the payment as 10 soldi. The argument runs as follows. A painter of Gaddo's standing would have accepted so modest a task only on a work he had painted himself.

The 1307 *Saint Peter* came from San Pier Maggiore and was attributed to the Saint Cecilia Master. The master would therefore be Gaddo. Boskovits supported the proposal on several occasions, notably in 2003, and connected it with the cathedral mosaic of the *Coronation of the Virgin* that Vasari gave to Gaddo. In her Paragone article of 2024, Chiodo judged that the hypothesis "has not been weakened or contradicted". She concluded that the traditional name should now be replaced with "'Gaddo Gaddi'", in inverted commas to mark the remaining doubt. The Uffizi website now attributes the panel to the "Master of the St. Cecilia (Gaddo Gaddi?)" and says only that Gaddo "may be the hidden identity of this master".

The hypothesis has clear weaknesses. The Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani entry on Gaddo Gaddi (1998) is unequivocal that "no documented work of his exists". The only sure fact it reports is his matriculation in the guild of physicians and apothecaries<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guild-physicians-apothecaries" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a> before 1320; Vasari's statement that he died in 1312 aged seventy-three is unreliable. The 1328 payment concerns a minor addition, not a painting.

The inference depends on two premises. The first is that the 1307 *Saint Peter* is by the master, which those who give it to Lippo di Benivieni dispute; the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale entry on Gaddo calls the doubts "fully justified". The second is that a painter would only modify his own work, which is a reasonable assumption about workshop etiquette, not a documented rule. Chiodo herself calls the reasoning "inductive" and acknowledges that it "still awaits definitive confirmation". She describes Boskovits's further connections with the mosaics as "a picture dangerously built on conjectures", though one she admires as an example of method.

A separate proposal, advanced by an American scholar in studies of 1923 and 1932, identified the [Isaac Master](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Maestro-delle-Storie-di-Isacco.html) of Assisi with Gaddo; it is not accepted in the current literature.

The proper epistemic status is therefore clear. The Saint Cecilia Master is a securely constructed artistic personality. The identification with Gaddo Gaddi is a serious hypothesis, adopted by the Uffizi with a question mark and by its leading recent student with inverted commas, but it is not documented. The traditional name is kept here, and the Gaddo hypothesis is attributed to those who hold it. The documentary and historiographical arguments are examined in the paper [The Master of Saint Cecilia and Gaddo Gaddi](/Content/Papers/MSCandGaddoGaddi.html).

## Errors in circulation

Several errors recur often enough to need explicit correction, and most have been addressed above. The claim that Santa Cecilia was rebuilt immediately after the 1304 fire, and that the panel was painted for the rebuilt church, is contradicted by the reported start of reconstruction in 1341 and by the panel's lack of fire damage. The statement that the master painted the final Assisi scenes "to the design" of Giotto is an interpretation, not a documented arrangement. A travel-oriented website places some of his Franciscan scenes in the Lower Church; the attributed scenes are in the Upper Church.

The San Giorgio alla Costa *Maestà* is still sometimes listed among his works, following Offner; Previtali, Boskovits and Bellosi assign it to the young Giotto, though not without dissent. The baptism on the Cecilia panel is usually labelled the "Baptism of Saint Tiburtius" but is now identified as Pope Urban baptising Maximus. The Getty *Madonna*'s date of 1290–1295, often cited as proof of pre-Assisi activity, is the museum's and is contested by the most recent stylistic argument.

## Legacy and current state of research

The master's afterlife is visible in the anonymous Florentine personalities Offner gathered around him, such as the Master of the Corsi Crucifix and the painters later reunited under the Horne Triptych Master. Recent scholarship adds the Master of the Dominican Effigies, whose panel in the Metropolitan Museum quotes the *Saint Thomas* fresco. On Chiodo's account these figures "escape easy classification" among Giotto's direct followers, yet played no secondary role in giving visual form to the imagination of the prosperous Florentine bourgeoisie. The master's influence on [Bernardo Daddi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Bernardo-Daddi.html), is plausible. One concrete trace is that Daddi's predella panels of Cecilia's story, now in Pisa, partly follow the Uffizi panel's model, but the broader claim remains an inference.

Research on the master is now unusually active. The Uffizi's scientific catalogue of its medieval paintings, for which the 2021 diagnostic campaign was undertaken, will be the next point of reference. Chiodo's 2024 synthesis and additions have shifted the debate towards an early date for the Uffizi panel and a central role in Giotto's early workshop; taken with the Gaddo Gaddi hypothesis, they amount to a substantial reinterpretation. Its components rest on very different levels of evidence. The diagnostic findings are hard data. The Assisi attribution is a majority view with authoritative dissent. The Gaddo Gaddi identification is a well-argued conjecture. The reattributions of mural fragments and late panels need wider testing.

What emerges is a painter of genuine historical weight: an early partner of Giotto who absorbed the new spatial and narrative language and gave it a distinctive inflection for a Florentine clientele over some three decades. His work is lively and legible, often architecturally inventive and at times charmingly implausible. Whether he was Gaddo Gaddi, and how much of Assisi he painted, may never be settled by documents. The surest path to understanding him remains close study of the works themselves, now aided by technical evidence unavailable to the critics who first gave him a name.

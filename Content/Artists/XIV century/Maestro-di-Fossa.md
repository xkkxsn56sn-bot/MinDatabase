## A note on names and the shape of a life

To write the biography of the *Master of Fossa* is, in the strict sense, to write the biography of a body of works rather than of a man. No document names him. No inscription records his hand. Not a single archival trace — no matrícula, no contract, no receipt for pigment or gold — survives to give him a baptismal name, a family, a workshop address, or a date of death. He belongs to that large and haunting category of medieval Italian personalities whom modern connoisseurship has reconstructed entirely from style: an artistic identity assembled by the eye, held together by the recurrence of a manner across panels, frescoes, and painted sculpture, and christened, according to the standard convention of art history, after the place where the work that first defined him happened to be preserved. That place is Fossa, a small village in the Aterno valley a few kilometres south-east of L'Aquila, in the Abruzzo; the work is a carved and painted wooden tabernacle of the Virgin and Child that stood, until 1979, in the isolated Cistercian church of Santa Maria ad Cryptas.

The convention "Maestro di Fossa" — Master of Fossa — is therefore a critical label and not a name. It is worth insisting on this at the outset, because the anonymity is not incidental to the subject; it is the subject's defining condition and the source of nearly every interpretive difficulty that attends him. The region in which he worked, the old Duchy of Spoleto and its Apennine hinterland, is almost uniquely poor in written records for the fourteenth century. Fires, neglect, and above all a long series of catastrophic earthquakes have destroyed the archives that elsewhere in Italy allow us to attach names to hands. As [Alessandro Delpriori](/scholars.html#delpriori-alessandro) has observed, the roster of artistic personalities active in Spoleto and the Valnerina in the Trecento is largely a collection of "Masters" who do not yet have — and perhaps never will have — a documentary identity or historical substance beyond their pictures. The Master of Fossa is the greatest of them.

And yet the paradox of this figure is that, for all his anonymity, he is now recognised as the pre-eminent painter of the Spoletan Trecento — the *caposcuola*, the head and fountainhead of a local school whose influence radiated across four modern regions (Umbria, the Marche, Lazio, and Abruzzo) and endured, in the persistence of his types and formulae, well into the fifteenth century. This biography attempts to give an ordered account of what can responsibly be said about him: the eponymous object and its fate; the geographical and cultural world he inhabited; the long and instructive history of his rediscovery; the character and sources of his style; the vexed question of whether he was a painter alone or, in the manner peculiar to his region, a painter-sculptor; the corpus of works attributed to him, with careful distinction between what is secure and what is debated; his legacy; and the losses that have made the reconstruction of his career so fragmentary. Throughout, disputed claims are attributed to the scholars who advanced them, and uncertainties are flagged rather than resolved by false confidence.

## The eponymous work: the tabernacle of Fossa

The object from which the master takes his name is a *tabernacolo mariano* — a monumental Marian tabernacle-altarpiece — whose surviving central element is a carved wooden statue of the Virgin and Child, now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA) in L'Aquila (inv. 1148). It came from Santa Maria ad Cryptas at Fossa, and it is generally dated by scholars to the fourth or fifth decade of the fourteenth century, that is, roughly the 1330s to the 1340s. The Fondazione [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico), in its online catalogue, records it more broadly as a work of the first half of the century (c. 1300–c. 1350).

### Madonna di Fossa

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 1.jpg" alt="Madonna di Fossa">
  <figcaption>Madonna di Fossa, 1330-40, carved and painted wood, 153 × 60 × 30 cm, Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA), L'Aquila.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the celebrated *Madonna di Fossa*, the eponymous work from which the anonymous sculptor-painter conventionally designated the Maestro di Fossa takes his name, and it stands today among the most refined testimonies of fourteenth-century Abruzzese wood sculpture preserved in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA) at L'Aquila.

Conceived as a devotional group of the Virgin and Child enthroned, the sculpture is carved in wood and finished with polychromy applied over a continuous layer of silver leaf that sheathes the entire wooden surface, a technically sophisticated procedure in which pigment and precious metal foil are integrated to produce the shimmering, almost enamel-like luminosity that still governs the surface. The statue belongs to a group of wooden exemplars attributed to the Maestro di Fossa, an artist for whom [Lunghi](/scholars.html#lunghi-elvio) has hypothesised a Spoletan origin.

The Virgin is presented frontally and crowned as *Regina Caeli*, her long blonde hair falling loose beneath the veil in a manner that betrays the transalpine, Northern Gothic models from which the type ultimately descends, and she is dressed in the courtly fashion of her age, wearing a deep crimson robe cinched at the waist by a decorated belt beneath an ample golden mantle whose broad, cascading folds are handled with a suppleness that already announces the new Gothic sensibility. With her left arm she supports the Child, who stands upright upon her knee in a pose of gentle animation, clad in a green tunic and a golden mantle and raising his hand in benediction, while in her right hand the Virgin grasps a small sceptre, an attribute that reinforces her regal characterisation and lends the group its quietly ceremonial gravity. The faces of Mother and Child are among the most expressive achievements of the piece, for their sweetness of feature and refinement of modelling mark a decisive departure from the residual Byzantine hieraticism of earlier Abruzzese wooden Madonnas and disclose instead the tender humanity of the emerging Gothic idiom.

The figural group is set within a tabernacle of triangular, rhomboidal plan whose painted interior functions as a compressed sacred architecture: the upper register is animated by a delicate Gothic arcading silhouetted against a deep starry sky, the intermediate zone is closed by a sumptuous red-and-silver damask cloth of honour serving as a *dorsale* behind the Virgin, and the lower portion suggests a Cosmatesque bench upon which she appears to be seated, so that the whole reads as a portable shrine in miniature.

It is essential to recognise, however, that this case was originally furnished with two painted wings, folding shutters bearing six scenes from the life of Christ likewise assigned to the Maestro di Fossa (from the left we see the Annunciation, the Adoration of the Magi, and the Presentation in the Temple. On the right we have the Crucifixion, the Flagellation, and the Kiss of Judas); these panels were stolen in January 1979, and of them only the *Presentation in the Temple* has since been recovered and now belongs to the museum. A telling material consequence of the original tabernacle setting is that the back of the Virgin has been cut away flush, a truncation so severe that it distorts the disposition of the arm and the hand resting upon the Child's shoulder, a peculiarity that continues to document the work's function as an enshrined cult image displayed to the faithful on feast days when the shutters were opened.

  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 6.jpg" alt="Madonna di Fossa (before theft)">
  <figcaption>Madonna di Fossa (before theft), 1330-40, carved and painted wood, from the Catalogo Generale dei Beni Culturali.</figcaption>
</figure>

In terms of attribution and dating, the sculpture is generally placed in the fourth to fifth decade of the fourteenth century, consistent with the conventional dating of circa 1330–1340, and it is ascribed to the hand of the anonymous master active across southern Umbria and Abruzzo in the first half of the century. The critical reconstruction of this artistic personality owes much to [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni), whose studies traced the presence of a Trecento Umbrian sculptural current bearing upon the Abruzzese material, while the group's compositional model has been plausibly connected to the Madonna of the funerary monument of Philippe de Courtenay in the Lower Basilica of San Francesco at Assisi, a comparison that situates the *Madonna di Fossa* firmly along the cultural axis linking Abruzzo to Umbria and to the wider currents of transalpine Gothic taste.

### The theft of 1979 and the dispersal

The tabernacle's later history is a small tragedy of the kind that recurs throughout this region's cultural record. In January 1979 the two painted shutters were stolen from the church. They were evidently sawn apart into their individual scenes for sale on the antiquities market; of the six panels, only one — the Presentation in the Temple — has been recovered. It is now preserved, together with the central wooden group, in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo. The other five painted scenes, the Annunciation, the Adoration of the Magi, the Crucifixion, the Flagellation, and the Kiss of Judas, remain lost. Our knowledge of them depends entirely on old photographs, including the campaigns of the Istituto Centrale per il Catalogo e la Documentazione (Fototeca Nazionale) and the images preserved in the Zeri photo archive, which document the ensemble as it was before the dispersal. For prudential reasons the wooden statue itself was subsequently moved from Santa Maria ad Cryptas to the greater security of the museum in L'Aquila.

It is worth registering here a persistent confusion in the popular and touristic literature that a scholarly account must correct. The church of Santa Maria ad Cryptas is celebrated above all for its extensive cycle of thirteenth-century frescoes — Genesis scenes, a Passion cycle, a Last Judgment, a calendar of the Months, and Marian stories — executed by several hands under a unified direction and associated with painters such as the anonymous "First Master of Santa Maria ad Cryptas" and, on a signed and dated panel of 1283 (the *Madonna del latte*, now in the Castello Piccolomini at Celano), with [Gentile da Rocca](/Content/Artists/XIII%20century/Gentile-da-Rocca.html). Those frescoes are Duecento work and have nothing to do with the Master of Fossa. He is named for the fourteenth-century wooden tabernacle alone. Conflating the master with the church's fresco decoration — a mistake one meets repeatedly online — obscures both the true chronology of the building and the real character of the artist, who was not, so far as the securely attributed corpus shows, the author of that celebrated mural ensemble.

### Christ blessing

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 2.jpg" alt="Christ blessing">
  <figcaption>Christ blessing, 1330-40, carved and painted wood, Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA), L'Aquila.</figcaption>
</figure>

This gable is the painted cusp (*cuspide*) that crowns the wooden tabernacle of the so-called **Madonna di Fossa**, and the half-length figure enthroned within it is Christ in the guise of the Redeemer, presented frontally and in majesty.

**The object and its context.** The image does not show an autonomous panel but the triangular crowning element of a *tabernacolo a pianta triangolare* — a shrine-tabernacle of the type that dominated altarpiece production in the territory between southern Umbria, the Rieti valley, and Abruzzo throughout the Trecento, in which a carved and polychromed wooden statue occupies the central housing while painted wings enclose it. The Madonna di Fossa was executed by the Maestro di Fossa after the town where the work was kept, in the church of Santa Maria ad Cryptas (Santa Maria delle Grotte) at Fossa, near L'Aquila.

The case we see today, within which the sculpture is set, is in reality only the central portion of the original tabernacle, which also comprised the wings, opened on feast days when the statue was displayed to the faithful. Those wings carried a six-scene Christological cycle also attributed to the master; the wings were stolen in 1979, and the Presentation in the Temple is the only panel that survives, now preserved at the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo together with the central portion of the tabernacle containing the wooden group of the Madonna and Child. The cusp you have photographed therefore belongs to the surviving central structure and functions, iconographically, as the theological apex of the whole assemblage: the enthroned Virgin below is surmounted by the sovereign Christ above.

Christ emerges at half-length from behind a parapet, his torso set frontally while the head inclines with a faint asymmetry that softens the hieratic scheme. He raises his right hand in the Latin gesture of benediction, the index and middle fingers extended and the remaining digits folded, an unambiguous act of blessing directed outward toward the beholder. With his left hand he steadies an open book — rendered here almost as an inscribed tablet or *cartellino* — that carries a Latin text in a Gothic minuscule. Though abraded and fragmentary, the legible portions ("ego s[um] … [ver]itas et … via, ve[ritas]… vit[a]") reconstruct the Johannine formula *Ego sum via, veritas et vita*, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), the pronouncement that fixes the figure's identity as the self-declaring Christ of salvation rather than a generic Pantocrator borrowed from Byzantine formulae.

The face is broad and grave, framed by auburn hair parted at the centre and falling in soft, striated locks over the shoulders, and closed below by a short, forked beard composed of small grouped masses — a treatment of the beard as clustered volumes that recurs across works associated with this milieu. The eyes are large, level, and slightly downcast, and the whole expression carries that faintly severe, almost sullen cast that scholars have noted as characteristic of the master's heads. Christ wears an olive-green tunic articulated with fine gold striations at the chest and a crimson mantle drawn across the left shoulder and arm, the drapery handled with a suppleness that departs from the earlier stiffness of Abruzzese woodwork. Behind the head spreads a broad cruciform nimbus, its ground worked in gold and its cross-arms picked out in dark tone against the ochre field, the whole set within the warm, burnished expanse that fills the tympanum.

The triangular field is bounded by a lobed, scalloped border — an *archeggiatura*, or blind arcading — running along both raking sides and articulated with small trilobed forms and fleur-de-lis-like sprigs reserved against a dark blue-black ground; a matching band recurs along the lower edge, above the horizontal ledge on which the book rests. In the fuller tabernacle this same vocabulary appears as a starry-sky arcading in the upper zone, a red-and-silver damasked hanging serving as a dorsal in the middle zone, and a Cosmatesque-type seat below on which the Virgin appears to sit. The cusp thus repeats, in its crowning register, the courtly-Gothic decorative idiom deployed throughout the shrine.

The Maestro di Fossa manner registers a decisive stylistic renewal: the faces of Mother and Child mark evident novelties that lift Abruzzese wooden sculpture out of the lingering late-Romanesque patina still steeped in Byzantine hieratism, with expressive features and a more delicate, Angevin-inspired handling of drapery. The blessing Christ of the cusp participates in exactly this transitional sensibility — Gothic softness tempering an inherited iconic frontality — and situates the ensemble within the broader cultural region that recent scholarship, above all Delpriori's reconstruction of the Spoletan school, has traced along the "left bank of the Tiber" between Spoleto and L'Aquila.

### Presentation at the Temple

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 3.jpg" alt="Presentation at the Temple">
  <figcaption>Presentation at the Temple, 1385–90, carved and painted wood, Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA), L'Aquila.</figcaption>
</figure>

This panel is the single surviving fragment from the pair of painted wings that once closed over the wooden tabernacle of the Madonna di Fossa, and it depicts the Presentation of Christ in the Temple.

What survives is a vertical painted wing (tempera and gold on wood) that belonged to the movable shutters of the triangular tabernacle from the church of Santa Maria ad Cryptas at Fossa, near L'Aquila. The wings, disposed like the doors of a cupboard so that they could be closed over the central shrine on ordinary days and opened to reveal the statue on feast days, carried a six-episode Christological cycle attributed to the same Maestro di Fossa; they were stolen in January 1979, and of the whole cycle only this Presentation was recovered and is now exhibited at the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo, while the remainder of the recovered material was, according to the museum's own account, placed in the deposits of the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Bologna. The scene formed part of the left wing, whose programme is generally described as the Annunciation, the Adoration of the Magi (or Epiphany), and the Presentation, the right wing carrying the Passion sequence of the Crucifixion, the Flagellation, and the Kiss of Judas.

The episode conflates, as was customary, the two Lucan moments of the **Purification of the Virgin** and the **Presentation** of the firstborn in the Temple (Luke 2:22–38), the narrative celebrated liturgically at Candlemas on the second of February. The composition turns on the ritual transaction conducted across the altar, where the Christ Child passes from the hands of his mother into those of the aged Simeon, the encounter that occasions the canticle Nunc dimittis.

At the far left stands an elderly, grey-bearded male saint, haloed in gold and clad in a green mantle drawn over an orange-gold tunic, his hands raised and partly veiled as he cradles a small wrapped bundle against his breast; he is to be understood as Joseph, bearing the humble sacrificial offering of the two turtledoves or young pigeons prescribed by Mosaic law for those unable to afford a lamb. Beside him the Virgin, enveloped in a dark blue-black mantle bordered in gold and haloed, inclines her head with characteristic tenderness and extends both hands forward over the altar, her gesture registering the very instant in which she relinquishes or reaches again toward the Child.

At the centre, spanning the pictorial and the ritual axis alike, rises the marble altar or parapet, draped below with a reddish cloth, across which the exchange is enacted. The infant Christ, lightly swaddled in white and haloed, is held in the arms of Simeon and turns back with both bare arms outstretched toward his mother, a naturalistic touch of infant reluctance that animates the otherwise solemn ceremony. Simeon himself, the aged high priest, appears in a rose-pink robe overlaid with a richly gold-embroidered mantle or humeral veil, his long white beard and gilded nimbus marking his sanctity, his hands reverently covered as he receives the Child. Behind and to the right gathers a cluster of witnesses: a veiled, haloed woman who is to be recognised as the prophetess Anna, who likewise acknowledged the infant, together with a further bearded elder in an ochre-yellow robe and one attendant figure pressed toward the right margin, where the panel is partly abraded.

The whole is staged within an elaborate painted temple conceived as a Gothic-classicising tempietto: slender colonnettes with foliate capitals frame the scene like a proscenium, behind which ribbed cross-vaults and pointed arches recede into shadow, and a gilded, pinnacled Gothic tabernacle or ciborium rises in the middle distance to denote the sanctuary. The upper register is dominated by a crowning entablature with a dentilled cornice and a central triangular pediment, flanked by wall-panels pierced with oculi enclosing rose-tracery; within the arched tympanum beneath the pediment a small seated, blessing figure is set between two adoring angels, drawn in a fine, almost monochrome line so as to read as sculpted or painted ornament of the sacred edifice, a Maiestas embedded in the fabric of the Temple itself. Above the architecture the ground reverts to burnished, punched gold.

The artist's manner here shows a slender canon, with refined and expressive heads, an Angevin-inflected softness of drapery, and a marked appetite for intricate architectural framing of Sienese descent.

### Madonna di San Silvestro

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 5.jpg" alt="Madonna di San Silvestro">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned (Madonna di San Silvestro), c. 1345, carved and painted wood with gilding and applied metal leaf, 155 × 49 × 25 cm, from the church of San Silvestro, L'Aquila, Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA), L'Aquila.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the elegant, fully Gothic counterpart to the Concanelle group — Madonna di San Silvestro, and it's worth knowing that this is the work often called **the supreme masterpiece of Abruzzese wooden sculpture**.

The group marks the full arrival of the Gothic idiom in Abruzzese sculpture and stands at the opposite pole from the hieratic, quasi-Byzantine Duecento Madonnas of the same collection. The Virgin is seated in an easy, courtly posture, her weight subtly shifted, the frontal rigidity of the older *Sedes Sapientiae* type dissolved into a lyrical, swaying elegance. She wears an openwork fleuron crown over a fine white veil, beneath which her hair falls loose in long blonde waves across her shoulders—a motif drawn from transalpine, French Gothic models. The face is the essence of the new sensibility: an elongated oval of great refinement, with arched brows, a small composed mouth, and an expression of tender gravity. Over a crimson gown, cinched by a decorated belt at a high waist and closed at the throat by an ornamented neckline, falls an ample gilded mantle that gathers across the lap and cascades over the legs in deep, richly undercut folds, the metallic ground lending the whole figure a shimmering preciousness.

The decisive innovation is affective rather than formal. The Christ Child, standing upon the Virgin's right side and steadied by her arm drawn protectively across his body, no longer sits in remote frontal benediction but turns toward his mother in a moment of mutual regard—the Virgin and Child brought into intimate colloquy. The Child is clad in a long silvery robe worked with metal leaf, its surface integrated with the pigment in a sophisticated interplay of paint and precious lamina. This humanized dialogue, together with the sinuous line and the softly modelled drapery, reflects the diffusion of Angevin court taste in the Kingdom of Naples and its French Gothic sources, and it is this quality that earned the group its standing as the culminating achievement of the region's *scultura lignea*. The polychromy survives in notably good condition, its fine craquelure and localized losses notwithstanding; the figure now rests on a modern wooden plinth.

The traditional label "Maestro di Fossa" rests on Costanza Gradara's 1921 reading of the work as a derivation of the *Madonna di Fossa*, and the two are indeed the museum's paired exemplars of the Trecento Gothic. But this is only one strand. Serra (1929) pushed the date to the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century; Mariani (1930) connected it instead to the *Madonna di Scurcola Marsicana*; and Gabbrielli (1933; *Inventario* 1934) proposed a Neapolitan provenance and referred it to a **Tuscan sculptor in the circle of Tino di Camaino**—a line that ties the piece to the Sienese-Angevin ambit at the Neapolitan court rather than to a local Abruzzese hand. Presenting it flatly as by the Maestro di Fossa would therefore assert a consensus that does not exist.

### St Michael the Archangel with Two Donors

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-Fossa/image 7.jpg" alt="St Michael the Archangel with Two Donors">
  <figcaption>St Michael the Archangel with Two Donors, 1335-45, fresco with gold dust and powdered lapis lazuli, crypt of the church of San Ponziano, Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fresco fills the conch of the right apsidiole, its curved field closed above by a triple polychrome border of stars, lozenges, rosettes and dogtooth motifs that follows the arc of the vault. Against a deep green-to-blue ground the archangel stands frontal and hieratic, occupying almost the full height of the semidome, his great wings spread symmetrically and graded from pale gold at the shoulders through ochre to a burning red at the pinions — the effect produced by the gold dust and lapis lazuli that the sources single out as the fresco's costliest and most conspicuous material feature, and which lends the plumage its unusual chromatic brilliance. The face is broad, pale and impassive beneath a crown of blond curls and a wide incised nimbus worked with radiating strokes; the gaze is level and remote, in keeping with the courtly rather than martial conception of the figure.

That courtliness is carried by the vesture. Michael wears a long white alb falling in quiet vertical folds, over which lies a richly embroidered gold loros-like stole crossed on the breast and descending to a broad jewelled orphrey at the hem, with matching jewelled cuffs and a gemmed pectoral at the throat; a red cord ending in a tasselled ornament hangs at the centre. This is the dress of a celestial dignitary — the archangel as prince of the heavenly court, in the Byzantine-derived imperial idiom — and it accords with the "French" Gothic component that Longhi detected in the master's Spoletan works, transmitted through his Abruzzese connections. The attributes reinforce the same reading. In his lowered right hand he grasps a slender vertical staff or sceptre, and in his raised left hand a golden sphere or globe. It is worth noting that at least one catalogue describes his attributes instead as a sword and scales; what is actually legible on the wall is the thin rod and the orb, so the figure reads as a cosmocrator-prince holding the globe of dominion rather than as the militant dragon-slayer or the soul-weigher of the psychostasis — an iconographic distinction I'd preserve carefully in the entry, and one that separates this Michael from the warrior-Michael type common elsewhere in the Spoletano.

At the archangel's feet kneel the two small oranti, dwarfed by his stature in the conventional hierarchy of scale. At the lower left, in profile, a male figure in a long crimson robe kneels with hands joined and face lifted toward the saint — the figure read variously as pilgrim or cardinal. At the lower right a female religious in a brown habit and white wimple kneels in the same attitude of prayer; her identification as a **bizzocca** points to the specifically Umbrian world of unenclosed penitent women, of whom [Angela of Foligno](/Content/Saints/Angela-da-Foligno.html) is the most famous. The lower zone around both figures is dense with incised graffiti — names, dates and invocations left by later visitors across the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, a genuine visitors' album — and it is here that the bizzocca suffered a later indignity: her eyes were scratched out and a mocking rhymed insult against beguines scrawled before her face, apparently the spite of a rejected admirer. These accretions, together with the reported later insertion of the oranti themselves, make the lower half of the composition a palimpsest of devotion and vandalism laid over the trecentesque original.

## The world of the Master of Fossa: Spoleto, "the Umbria to the left of the Tiber," and the road to Abruzzo

The Master of Fossa is called for an Abruzzese town, but he is, by the consensus of modern scholarship, a Spoletan artist. To understand him one must understand the particular artistic geography of the old Duchy of Spoleto and its Apennine reach — a cultural region that does not coincide with any modern administrative map.

The decisive framework here is the one proposed by Giovanni Previtali in his pioneering studies of Umbrian wooden sculpture, and most fully in his lectures on Umbrian Trecento sculpture of the early 1980s: the division of Umbria into two culturally distinct zones separated by the course of the Tiber. To the right of the river lay the Umbria of Perugia and Orvieto, strongly oriented toward Tuscany, its sculpture dominated by Sienese models radiating from the cathedral workshop at Orvieto. To the *left* of the Tiber lay a different world — the Umbria of Foligno, Spoleto, and the Valnerina — which extended across the Apennines to embrace parts of the Marche, Lazio, and Abruzzo: in effect the dioceses of Spoleto, Foligno, Ascoli Piceno, Teramo, L'Aquila, Sulmona, and Rieti, linked by an internal "line of communication" that ran from Assisi to L'Aquila by way of Spoleto. This was the ancient transhumance and pilgrimage corridor known as the *via degli Abruzzi*, and it is the artery along which the works and, presumably, the person of the Master of Fossa travelled. That a Spoletan master's masterpiece should end up in a church near L'Aquila is not an anomaly but a symptom of the deep unity of this trans-Apennine cultural region — a unity underlined by [Corrado Fratini](/scholars.html#fratini-corrado) and Alessandro Marchi, who have shown that the same artists left works on both slopes of the mountains.

The "Umbria to the left of the Tiber" had, in Previtali's and [Bruno Toscano](/scholars.html#toscano-bruno)'s shared reconstruction, its own rules, its own repertory of forms, and its own peculiar identity. Its capital was Spoleto — seat of the ancient duchy and, more importantly, of the diocese — which had been an exceptionally active artistic centre throughout the Middle Ages, home in the thirteenth century to painters (and probably sculptors) of the calibre of Simeone and Machilone. What gave the region its final and highest flowering, between the last decades of the Duecento and the first decades of the Trecento, was proximity to the single greatest artistic event of the age: the decoration of the Basilica of San Francesco at Assisi. The Assisi worksite drew masters from Rome, Tuscany, and beyond; the Spoletan painters absorbed the Giottesque revolution not at second hand but directly, "on the scaffolding," as [Andrea De Marchi](/scholars.html#de-marchi-andrea) has evocatively put it — and then bent it, with proud independence, to the grain of a robust local tradition.

The result was an art of striking originality and, in De Marchi's characterisation, of a permanently high expressive temperature: a *fauve* of colour and matter, an exaggerated mimicry oscillating between cries of pain and aching tenderness. Where Rimini, the other great "school" of the Giottesque periphery, exploded almost overnight from Giotto's own brief presence, Spoleto's Giottism sedimented slowly onto a pre-existing and vigorous foundation, producing results that resisted assimilation to any Tuscan norm. One of the most conspicuous "irregularities" of the region was its refusal of the great gothic compartmented polyptych — the gold-ground, many-panelled altarpiece that was conquering "the right bank of the Tiber," at Orvieto and Perugia.

In its place the Spoletan world cultivated a quite different type of altarpiece: the monumental tabernacle with a carved sculpture (more rarely a painted image) at the centre and hinged, closable wings normally painted with the narrative of the personage — the Virgin, or a saint — to whom the altar or church was dedicated. The Fossa tabernacle is the supreme and most mature surviving example of precisely this endemic type. Its typological roots reach back, through the *vita-icon* tradition, to transalpine and specifically French and Spanish models, whose influence was felt keenly in this cosmopolitan corner of the Apennines during the long decades of the Avignon papacy, when the region was continually traversed by French papal functionaries.

## The rediscovery: a history of the master's critical fortune

Because the Master of Fossa is a construction of connoisseurship, the history of his reconstruction is inseparable from his biography. It is a long and instructive story, spanning more than a century and moving from the local antiquarianism of Abruzzo to the high connoisseurship of the Longhi school and, finally, to the systematic monographic scholarship of our own moment.

The earliest sustained attention to the Fossa tabernacle came from the Abruzzese side, in the writings of local scholars and antiquarians of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — among them [Pietro Piccirilli](/scholars.html#piccirilli-pietro), Vincenzo Bindi, and Chini. These writers studied the tabernacle as a local treasure and, importantly, hypothesised from early on that the painted shutters were by the same hand as the central sculpture, and further that this "Master of Fossa" had also worked in Umbria, in the Spoletan area. This is a striking intuition, and it anticipates the later, fuller reconstruction: the recognition that the artist named for an Abruzzese town belonged in fact to a broader Umbro-Abruzzese cultural sphere. At this stage, however, the master remained essentially a local curiosity, unintegrated into the general history of Italian Trecento painting.

In the mid-twentieth century the master began to enter the wider bibliography of Italian primitives. [Edward B. Garrison](/scholars.html#garrison-edward), whose *Italian Romanesque Panel Painting: An Illustrated Index* (1949) remains a fundamental instrument, engaged with works of this milieu; the Zeri catalogue records Garrison's readings of related material. The presence of the master's orbit in Anglo-American scholarship — and, as we shall see, the eventual migration of one of his most important works to a collection in the United States — reflects the growing international interest in the "Umbrian primitives" during these decades.

The decisive moment in the master's rediscovery came from Roberto Longhi. In the academic year 1953–1954 Longhi held a now-legendary monographic course at the University of Florence on Umbrian painting of the first half of the Trecento. The lecture notes (*dispense*) were published posthumously in *Paragone* in 1973 under the title *La pittura umbra della prima metà del Trecento*, and they became the foundation of all subsequent research on the subject. It was in this course that Longhi reconstructed the personality of the Master of Fossa as a coherent artistic identity within the panorama of Umbrian Trecento painting — assembling around the eponymous tabernacle a group of panels and frescoes and giving the master his place in the general narrative. The Zeri Foundation catalogue cites Longhi's 1973 text (at pages 38–40) as the primary critical reference for both the Fossa tabernacle's painted stories and the related Passion panels.

The roll of students who attended that Florentine course reads like a directory of the next generation of Italian art history: [Carlo Volpe](/scholars.html#volpe-carlo), [Ferdinando Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando), [Mina Gregori](/scholars.html#gregori-mina), Giovanni Previtali, and Bruno Toscano. Several of them would carry forward, each in his own direction, the study of this region. Around 1970 Carlo Volpe, together with his young Hungarian assistant [Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos), planned a large study of Umbrian Trecento painting; it was never completed, but Boskovits's own contributions (from 1971) opened important lines of inquiry into the milieu.

A small but telling episode in the master's fortune concerns Luciano Bellosi. In 1966, in his volume for the popular *Maestri del Colore* series devoted to central Italian painting in the gothic age (*Pittura dell'Italia centrale nell'età gotica*), Bellosi placed a work by the Master of Fossa on the cover — this at a moment when the master was, in De Marchi's memorable phrase, virtually a *carneade*, an unknown quantity in the scholarship. The choice was prescient: it registered, ahead of the general consensus, the exceptional quality of an artist whom the wider discipline had not yet learned to see.

Giovanni Previtali's contribution deserves separate emphasis, because it opened a dimension of the master's activity that painting-centred scholarship had ignored: sculpture. In an article in *Paragone* in 1965 ("Sulle tracce di una scultura umbra del Trecento"), and in a series of studies over the following two decades culminating in his lectures on Umbrian Trecento sculpture (collected and re-edited in *Studi sulla scultura gotica in Italia: storia e geografia*, 1991), Previtali did for the sculpture of this region what Longhi had done for its painting: he brought into existence, as an object of study, the very idea of an Umbrian Trecento wooden sculpture.

Two of his insights are fundamental for the Master of Fossa. First, that in this region polychromy was not a mere finishing decoration but a genuine "collaboration between painting and sculpture," in which the colour did not simply adorn the carved surface but substantially *defined* the forms — so that these were, in his provocative words, sculptures that a Florentine or a Perugian would have refused to call sculpture at all. Second, that the culture behind these works was fundamentally French in inspiration, but that this French flavour derived not, as had long been assumed, from Angevin Naples, but rather from the north — from the French masterpieces to be admired at Assisi and Orvieto and from the continual passage of French papal officials through Umbria during the Avignon years. Previtali reconstructed a sculptor he called the "Master of the Madonna of Spoleto" (also referred to as the Master of the Madonna of the Duomo of Spoleto), and it is to this sculptural personality that the carving of the Fossa Virgin has been referred — a point to which we shall return, because it raises the central question of whether the Master of Fossa was himself the sculptor.

Bruno Toscano, another of Longhi's Florentine pupils, developed over a lifetime a distinctive "geographical" or territorial method for the study of this region's art — a method attentive above all to what has been *lost*. Toscano's guiding assumption, reiterated across his work, is that we study only a minimal fraction of what once existed; his fundamental essays on the fortune of the primitives (from the 1960s) and his study *Conservato e perduto in territorio spoletino* (1995) are essential for understanding both the dispersal of the Spoletan patrimony and the original contexts of works like the Fossa tabernacle. Toscano also directed the intellectually refined *Manuali del territorio* (on the Valnerina, 1977, and on Spoleto, 1978), models of a capillary, interdisciplinary, and civically engaged survey of a threatened patrimony. It was Toscano who, over the years, drew several of the master's works into the discussion — among them the panel of Saint Peter Martyr rediscovered at San Domenico in Spoleto.

Filippo Todini's two-volume *La pittura umbra dal Duecento al primo Cinquecento* (Longanesi, 1989) is, for all the criticism it has attracted, an indispensable reference tool: it catalogues the Master of Fossa's works and reproduces them (I, p. 141; II, figs. 316, 322–323). Todini had already, in the mid-1980s, recognised the specificity of a Spoletan Trecento culture and described its painters as the most original and fascinating of Umbrian Trecento art. De Marchi has nonetheless criticised the 1989 repertory sharply, as an atomising, de-hierarchising, and de-contextualising enterprise — a system of Berensonian lists that extracts works from their settings and severs painting from the woodcarving with which, in this region, it was inextricably compounded. The criticism is important because it defines, by negation, the methodological program of the current scholarship: a return to context, to the reintegration of painting and sculpture, and to rigorous philology.

The reconstruction of the Master of Fossa entered a new and decisive phase with a younger generation of scholars. Alessandro Delpriori's doctoral thesis, defended at the University of Florence in 2010 (*Produzione figurativa a Spoleto e in Valnerina nel XIV secolo. Problemi, contesti, casi esemplari*), became the basis for his monograph *La scuola di Spoleto. Immagini dipinte e scolpite nel Trecento tra Valle Umbra e Valnerina* (Quattroemme, Perugia, 2015), with a substantial introduction by Andrea De Marchi. This book — the first systematic, richly illustrated synthesis of the subject — treats the Master of Fossa at length (chapter VIII, "Il Maestro di Fossa e il Maestro della Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto"; catalogue pp. 219–230, 237–240, 256–258) and constitutes the fundamental modern reference for the artist. Delpriori's overarching thesis, developed from earlier intuitions of Corrado Fratini (1997, 1998, 2000), is that in this region the workshop of the woodcarver and the workshop of the painter coincided — a claim with direct consequences for how we understand the Master of Fossa.

Alongside Delpriori, several scholars have refined the Abruzzese dimension of the corpus. Stefania Paone's essay "Tabernacoli dipinti e scultura lignea in Abruzzo: il Maestro di Fossa e il Maestro del Crocifisso d'argento" (*Studi medievali e moderni*, 15, 2011, pp. 45–68) situates the master within the Abruzzese production of painted tabernacles and polychrome sculpture. [Cristiana Pasqualetti](/scholars.html#pasqualetti-cristiana), editor of the volume *La via degli Abruzzi e le arti nel Medioevo (secc. XIII–XV)* (L'Aquila, 2014), has advanced the study of the trans-Apennine artistic corridor and the Abruzzese context; Delpriori's own essay in that volume ("Il San Nicola di Monticchio e i tabernacoli monumentali come pale d'altare") extends the discussion of the monumental tabernacle-as-altarpiece. Matteo Mazzalupi (2009) made known a painted crucifix at Ussita whose *tabelloni* he attributed to the Master of Fossa — an addition of real importance for the master's activity as a painter of sculpted crosses.

The culminating public event was the great exhibition *Capolavori del Trecento. Il cantiere di Giotto, Spoleto e l'Appennino*, curated by [Vittoria Garibaldi](/scholars.html#garibaldi-vittoria), Alessandro Delpriori, and Bernardino Sperandio, held from 24 June to 4 November 2018 across four sites — Trevi, Montefalco, Spoleto, and Scheggino. The exhibition gathered some seventy gold-ground panels, polychrome wooden sculptures, and illuminated manuscripts, and at Montefalco it reassembled, for the first time in modern history, the *corpus* of the Master of Fossa's works, including pieces normally kept from public view in the papal apartments of the Vatican and restored for the occasion by the Vatican Museums' laboratories. The exhibition catalogue (Garibaldi–Delpriori, 2018) remains, with Delpriori's 2015 monograph, the principal modern source. Most recently, the first monograph devoted entirely to the church of Santa Maria ad Cryptas — *Santa Maria ad Cryptas. Storia, arte, restauri*, edited by Michele Maccherini and Luca Pezzuto (Editori Paparo, 2022), with contributions by scholars including Laura Cavazzini and Aldo Galli — has renewed attention to the site and its history.

## Formation, style, and artistic sources

If the documents are silent, the pictures speak, and what they say about the Master of Fossa's formation is remarkably consistent across the modern literature. The consensus, articulated most forcefully by De Marchi and Delpriori, is that the deepest understanding of the master runs through Assisi — and specifically through three great presences in the Lower Basilica of San Francesco: the Giotto of the Lower Church, Simone Martini, and Pietro Lorenzetti — with a further, close parallel in Puccio Capanna. Assisi, in Delpriori's summary, is the true point of reference.

This triangulation defines the master's peculiar synthesis. From Giotto he took a sense of monumental structure and of figures that occupy space with weight and dignity; he is a painter, in De Marchi's formula, capable of moving "downstream of the culture of Giotto." But onto this Giottesque armature he grafted the *grazia* of Simone Martini — the linear elegance, the courtly sweetness, the refined chromatic delicacy that made Vittoria Garibaldi and others compare the Montefalco dossale to a work by Simone himself. The presence of Pietro Lorenzetti is felt above all in the crowded, emotionally charged Calvaries that are so characteristic of the Spoletan Trecento, modelled on Pietro's Crucifixion in the left transept of the Lower Basilica. And the parallel with Puccio Capanna — Giotto's most gifted follower in the region — is chronological as well as stylistic, placing the Master of Fossa securely at the middle of the fourteenth century.

To these Assisi-mediated Tuscan sources the master added a further ingredient that recurs in the descriptions of his work: Angevin, or more broadly transalpine French, culture. Garibaldi, discussing the 2018 exhibition, stressed precisely this — that the Master of Fossa gathers a whole set of influences, "not least the Angevin culture." Whether this French flavour reached him from Naples or, as Previtali argued for the sculpture, from the northern French works at Assisi and Orvieto and from the Avignonese papal presence, remains a matter of interpretation; the older literature tends to say "Angevin," the more recent to nuance the point toward a northern, Avignon-mediated source. What is not in doubt is the delicacy of the result: soft modelling, clear and luminous flesh tones, subtle transitions of colour, delicate oval faces, and drapery of a refined, courtly cast.

The stylistic profile that emerges is therefore that of a painter who is at once fully abreast of the most advanced Tuscan painting of his day and deeply rooted in a local tradition with its own expressive imperatives. His art speaks directly to the beholder; it aims at emotional involvement and the "theatricalisation" of devotion, in keeping with the visualised, recited religiosity of the Umbrian *laudi* and sacred representations. The clear incarnations, the soft painting built up from subtle passages of colour, and the tender oval faces — of the female saints, of the Virgin, of the Christ Child — are the recurring diagnostic features by which his hand, and the hands close to him, are recognised. On the strength of these features the *Catalogo generale dei beni culturali* and other authorities have drawn comparisons between the securely attributed works and a penumbra of frescoes and panels judged to be "close to" or "in the manner of" the Master of Fossa.

## Painter, or painter-sculptor? A central and open question

One of the most consequential and genuinely unresolved questions about the Master of Fossa is whether he was a painter only, or whether — in the manner peculiar to the Spoletan world — he was simultaneously the sculptor of the wooden images that his painting completed.

The general thesis, argued with force by Delpriori (following Fratini), is that in the "Umbria to the left of the Tiber" the two crafts habitually coincided in a single workshop, and often in a single person. This is not a mere assumption; it rests on evidence. The wooden statues of this region were conceived from the Duecento onward in a deliberately summary, "unfinished" way — with smoothed transitions and receding projections — precisely because they demanded gesso and vivid polychromy as their indispensable complement. Conversely, the painting on the wings was subordinated to the integration of the sculptural whole. The strongest single piece of documentary support is an inscription of 1262 at the foot of the wooden *Madonna delle Concanelle* from Bugnara (now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo), signed by the Spoletan painter *Machilone* with his son, using the verb *depinserunt* — "they painted" — for what is a sculpted image, which strongly suggests that the same men were responsible for both carving and colour. Delpriori observes, further, that the stylistic series run in constant parallel: the Master of Cesi always works with the sculptor of the Santa Cristina of Caso; the Master of the Cross of Trevi with the Master of the Cross of Visso; and the Master of Fossa with the Master of the Madonna of the Duomo of Spoleto.

The last pairing is the crux. If the painter (Master of Fossa) and the sculptor (Master of the Madonna of Spoleto / of the Duomo of Spoleto) are indeed two facets of one artist, then the Master of Fossa carved as well as painted the central Virgin of his own eponymous tabernacle — and his activity as an *intagliatore* (woodcarver) extends across a further group of statues. De Marchi judges this identification as "highly probable," and points to a remarkably well-preserved *Holy Bishop* (Santo vescovo) in the Museo del Bargello in Florence — with passages of polychromy on the gold of the cope — as one of the master's earliest works in this expanded, sculptural sense. He notes, too, that the definitive test will come when the heavy repainting is removed from the wooden Crucifix of Ussita, whose *tabelloni* were painted by the Master of Fossa (per Mazzalupi's 2009 attribution): the joint responsibility for carving and painting could then be verified directly.

It must be stressed, however, that this identification is a hypothesis, not an established fact, and that it has not gone unchallenged. Other scholars — the Zeri catalogue records the caution of specialists, and De Marchi himself notes that the problem "still awaits serious and resolving studies" — have warned against automatically merging the painter and the sculptor into a single personality, arguing that a signature on polychromy does not by itself prove identity of carver and painter, and that the question must be settled case by case. The honest position, and the one adopted here, is that the coincidence of painter and sculptor in the Master of Fossa is a strong and attractive hypothesis, well supported by the general practice of the region and by close stylistic correspondences, but not yet demonstrated beyond dispute. It is precisely the kind of claim that should be flagged as open rather than resolved.

## Chronology

In the absence of any dated or documented work, the chronology of the Master of Fossa rests entirely on stylistic argument and on the dating of comparanda. The broad consensus places his activity in the first half of the fourteenth century, with the core of his career around its middle decades. The eponymous Fossa tabernacle is dated to the 1330s–1340s (the fourth and fifth decades of the century). The Master of Cesi's key works fall a little earlier — the eponymous dossale of 1308, the Vallo di Nera ancona of 1315, the Santa Croce chapel at Montefalco of 1333 — and the Master of Fossa is understood to be the somewhat younger, culminating figure of the school, the artist in whom the Spoletan absorption of Assisi reaches its highest and most graceful expression. De Marchi's placement of him "in perfect parallel, chronological as well as stylistic, with Puccio Capanna" points to the 1330s–1350s as his mature period. Attempts to build an internal chronology of his work — an early phase closer to sculpture and to the local Duecento tradition (the Bargello *Holy Bishop*), a mature phase of the great tabernacles and dossali — remain necessarily provisional, and depend on the still-contested identification of the master with the sculptor of the Spoleto Madonna.

The stylistic arguments for the dating cluster around several diagnostic features that allow scholars to position the Master of Fossa's works within the evolution of Trecento style. Most crucial are his treatment of drapery — the soft, voluminous folds that betray his engagement with the northern Gothic idiom and distinguish his work from the crisper, more linearly-defined garments of earlier Spoletan masters — and the refinement of facial modelling, particularly in the oval faces of his Madonnas and female saints, which mark a decisive move away from the more rigid, frontal arrangements of the Duecento. The delicate flesh tones and the luminous palette that characterize his work suggest an artist responding to and absorbing the lessons of Simone Martini's presence in the Lower Basilica at Assisi, lessons that had to be received and transmitted before they could suffuse his own production. The Montefalco works, in particular, with their extraordinary chromatic subtlety and their sophisticated deployment of lapis lazuli and gold dust, point to an artist working at the height of his powers and at the command of considerable technical resources — a position ordinarily reached only after years of established reputation.

The question of the master's earliest phase remains one of the least settled aspects of his chronology. De Marchi's suggestion that the Bargello *Holy Bishop* represents an early work, close to the local Duecento tradition and to the sculptural foundation of his practice, is attractive but unproven. If this identification is correct, it would push the beginning of his documented activity back toward the 1310s or early 1320s, giving him a career span of some forty or fifty years — a not impossible duration for an artist of sustained productivity and evolving style. Conversely, if the *Holy Bishop* is by a different hand or a later copy, then the master's documented works cluster more tightly around the 1330s–1350s, a career of perhaps twenty or thirty years such as characterized many other medieval artists. The internal evolution of his style from such early works to the mature masterpieces would be instructive, but without secure attribution of the earliest pieces, this narrative of development remains speculative.

At the opposite end of his career, the question of whether the Master of Fossa continued to work into the 1350s or 1360s is equally problematic. The Sulmona frescoes, with their "poor state of preservation" and their monumental scale — an altarpiece painted directly on the wall rather than on a portable panel or tabernacle — represent a different register of his activity and are harder to date precisely. If De Marchi's acceptance of the attribution is correct, they may suggest a continuing practice into the later decades of the century, or they may represent works executed during his established maturity but not distinctly later in style. The fragmentary condition of much of his fresco work makes it difficult to trace a stylistic evolution through the 1350s and beyond. Bruno Toscano's principle — that we possess only a fraction of what once existed — is particularly acute for the fresco works, which, being immovable and site-specific, are far more vulnerable to loss and destruction than portable panels and sculptures.

The broader regional chronology provides a stabilizing framework for the Master of Fossa's timeline. The artistic production of the Spoletan school shows a recognizable sequence: the generation of the Master of Cesi dominates the first and second decades of the Trecento, with his works providing the stylistic foundation; the Master of Fossa represents the apogee of this tradition in the middle decades, roughly the 1330s–1350s; and the later followers — the Master of the Calvaries, the Master of Eggi, and others — extend the tradition, with increasing conservatism and reliance on older formulae, into the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. This sequence is supported by the presence of dated or datable comparanda at Assisi (Giotto's work in the Lower Basilica can be placed in the 1320s–1330s; Simone Martini's presence there is documented in the 1320s; Pietro Lorenzetti's work spans the 1320s–1340s), and by the dated works of other Spoletan and Umbrian masters. Within this stable framework, the Master of Fossa's position as a somewhat younger contemporary of Puccio Capanna, and as the heir to the Master of Cesi's innovations, is reasonably secure.

The persistence of the Master of Fossa's style and formulae across a period of more than a century — from his own presumed working lifetime in the 1330s–1350s into the fifteenth century and beyond — is itself a phenomenon that complicates the chronological picture. The tendency of later Spoletan painters to repeat his types, his tabernacle compositions, and his characteristic tender, oval-faced figures means that works executed in the 1400s or later can appear, at first glance, to belong to his own hand. The *Maestro di Eggi*, mentioned by Federico Zeri with admiration for his nostalgic attachment to Trecento models, is the clearest example of this conservative persistence. It follows that the chronology of individual works attributed to the Master of Fossa must be established not only by internal stylistic analysis but also by a careful assessment of their relationship to documented works, to their original contexts (when known), and to the broader history of the workshop tradition they represent. In this sense, the chronology of the Master of Fossa is inseparable from the chronology of the Spoletan school as a whole.

## Iconography, typology, and technique

The Master of Fossa's art is best understood not as a series of isolated pictures but as part of a coherent devotional culture with its own preferred object-types, iconographies, and techniques.

The master's characteristic and most original format is the *tabernacolo monumentale* — a large tabernacle housing, at its centre, a carved (or, more rarely, painted) sacred image, closed by hinged wings painted with the narrative of the central figure. This type, endemic to the "Umbria to the left of the Tiber," functioned as an altarpiece and stood, often, on major altars; it descends from the *vita-icon* tradition and dialogues with transalpine (French and Spanish) models. Its destination was above all the enclosed choirs of female monastic communities, where the closed wings concealed and protected the central image and the painted *storiette* of Christ and the Virgin could be contemplated at close range. The Fossa tabernacle is the supreme surviving example; the Montefalco dossale and the Passion panels belong to the same devotional world.

On the wings and dossali the master deployed cycles of the life of Christ and the Virgin — Annunciation, Epiphany, Presentation, Flagellation, Crucifixion, Kiss of Judas, and the Passion more broadly. These narratives, in the older descriptions, show a Giottesque derivation, especially in the very light tonality of the garments and grounds, even as the master fails (or declines) to break through into a convincing third dimension, rendering architectural settings — the Temple as a gothic church — as flat backdrops. The interest is emotional and devotional rather than spatial.

Like the Master of Cesi and the Master of the Cross of Trevi, the Master of Fossa cultivated the crowded, populous Calvary, modelled on Pietro Lorenzetti's Crucifixion in the Lower Basilica at Assisi and aimed at intense emotional participation. The fragmentary Calvary and *Madonna della Misericordia* associated with his manner at Santa Croce in Trevi belong to this tradition.

The master and his region are notable for the sophisticated integration of paint and precious metal — gold leaf and gold dust, and lapis lazuli, as in the San Ponziano fresco executed *con polvere d'oro e di lapislazzuli*. On the wooden sculpture, the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo emphasises the refined interplay of pigments and metal foils. This technical luxury, together with the northern-derived delicacy of the modelling, is a hallmark of his best work.

## Legacy and influence

The Master of Fossa is not merely one figure among the Spoletan Trecento masters; he is, in the modern consensus, its *caposcuola* — its founder and beacon. In De Marchi's and Delpriori's formulation, he is **the true and great artist of Spoleto at the middle of the century, a beacon for all the art that follows, right into the fifteenth century.** The whole subsequent development of local painting is said to rest on his teaching. His immediate and later followers include the painter tentatively identified as *Bartolo di Spoleto* — the so-called "Master of the Calvaries" (*Maestro dei Calvari*), a somewhat subdued protagonist of the late Trecento — and the line extends down through the *Maestro di Eggi* (a painter admired also by Federico Zeri, who into the mid-fifteenth century and beyond continued to paint with a nostalgic attachment to Trecento models and to the very formulae of the Master of Fossa) to the beginnings of the prolific Foligno painter Giovanni di Corraduccio in the early Quattrocento. The persistence of his types — the monumental tabernacle, the narrative wings, the tender oval faces — across more than a century is itself the strongest testimony to his stature.

His legacy is clearest not in direct imitation of single compositions but in the survival of a visual grammar. The Frontal Madonna, the softly modelled child, the courtly mantle, the starry or gilded field, and the compact narrative wing all became part of the regional repertory, ready to be reactivated by later hands even when the original wooden shrine had disappeared. In that sense the Master of Fossa did for the Spoletan world what a major workshop tradition often does in medieval Italy: he fixed a set of types that outlived the artist and became locally authoritative.

That authority is visible in the way later painters and sculptors recalibrate his formulas rather than simply copying them. The Calvary groups of the late Trecento retain his emotional density but stiffen it into a more repetitive devotional syntax; the later Eggi painter keeps the tender physiognomies but simplifies the drapery; Corraduccio, at the point where the tradition enters the Quattrocento, inherits the sense of intimacy and narrative concentration while translating it into a more expansive and urbane language. The line of descent is therefore not linear reproduction but successive adaptation.

It is equally important that his influence was not confined to painting on panel. If the hypothesis that the Master of Fossa also worked as a sculptor proves correct, then his afterlife must be understood as a transfer of form across media: carved and painted images, tabernacles, and wings all belong to a single workshop ecology. Later Umbrian and Abruzzese works that preserve the same combination of carved central image and painted shutters suggest that what was transmitted was as much a way of making devotional objects as a style in the narrow sense.

For this reason the Master's legacy has a methodological importance as well as an artistic one. He is one of the clearest cases in which the history of an anonymous medieval artist depends on the reconstruction of a school, a region, and a type of object rather than on a biographical file. The very fact that modern scholarship can trace his influence so widely, despite the disappearance of so many originals, shows how much the art of the old Duchy of Spoleto was organised by workshop memory, local repetition, and the slow authority of exemplary works. The Master of Fossa survives, finally, as both an artist and a system of recognition.

## Loss, dispersal, and the fragility of the record

No account of the Master of Fossa can omit the theme that runs through the whole scholarship on his region: loss. The Spoletan and Abruzzese patrimony has been savaged by earthquakes (the tremors of 1313 and later at Fossa itself; the Valnerina earthquake of 1979; the Umbria–Marche earthquake of 1997; the L'Aquila earthquake of 2009), by Napoleonic spoliation and the consequent dispersal of movable works, by theft (the sawing-apart of the Fossa shutters in 1979), and by simple neglect. The monumental tabernacles that were once the region's characteristic altarpieces have been almost universally dismembered, their wings sold off and their central sculptures orphaned, so that our very sense of the type has been diminished. The Fossa tabernacle, still miraculously intact in the 1960s, is the emblem of this history: it survives now only as a hollowed statue and a single recovered panel, its five other scenes known only from photographs.

This condition has a double consequence for the biography of the master. On the one hand, it means that the corpus we possess is a minute and possibly unrepresentative fraction of what he made — a caution that Bruno Toscano built into the very method of the field. On the other, it lends a particular urgency and value to the surviving works, and to the campaigns of restoration and study (the 2018 exhibition, the 2022 monograph on Santa Maria ad Cryptas, the ongoing restorations by the Soprintendenza and the Vatican laboratories) that have, paradoxically, been spurred by the same seismic catastrophes that threatened the patrimony. The restorations following the earthquakes have repeatedly returned new "texts" to scholarship, and it is on such recoveries — the freeing of the San Domenico panel from its overpaint, the eventual cleaning of the Ussita crucifix — that further progress on the Master of Fossa is likely to depend.

The problem is not merely that individual objects have been lost, but that their original settings have been broken apart. For the Master of Fossa, as for much of the Spoletan tradition, the work was designed to operate as an ensemble: shrine, wings, painted architecture, and carved image formed a single devotional mechanism. Once the shutters were removed, the sculpture displaced, or the shrine itself damaged, the visual and liturgical logic of the object became much harder to recover, and later scholarship has had to reconstruct that logic from fragments, photographs, and old descriptions.

Earthquake damage has been especially cruel to this material because it attacks both the building and the object at once. Churches in the Aterno valley, the Valnerina, and the wider Abruzzese hinterland were not neutral containers for movable works; they were the very conditions that gave those works meaning, placement, and access. When walls crack, roofs collapse, and altars are dismantled for safety, the surviving sculptures and panels are often detached from the coordinates that once anchored them in local ritual life.

The archival dimension of this loss is equally severe. In regions where municipal and ecclesiastical records were already thin, every fire, transfer, or suppression erased another layer of evidence that might have tied a work to a patron, a payment, a transport, or a consecration. The Master of Fossa therefore lives in an unusually narrow documentary corridor: the art survives more generously than the paper, but even the art survives in a fractured state, so that attribution, chronology, and original function must all be inferred from material remains that are themselves incomplete.

This is why the modern history of the master is inseparable from conservation history. Every cleaning campaign, every rediscovered fragment, and every new photograph has the potential to alter the argument, not by producing a name, but by sharpening the comparison on which anonymous artists depend. In that sense, the Master of Fossa is a particularly vivid case of how restoration can become a scholarly event: the work of saving what remains is also the work of making the past legible again.

## Conclusion: an anonymous master and the limits of biography

The Master of Fossa is a biography without a life — or rather, a biography whose subject is a manner, a workshop practice, and a set of surviving objects, held together by the connoisseur's conviction that a single sensibility produced them. What can be said with confidence is this: that in the second quarter of the fourteenth century a painter of the highest gifts was active in the old Duchy of Spoleto and along the trans-Apennine road toward L'Aquila and Sulmona; that he had absorbed, directly at Assisi, the lessons of Giotto, Simone Martini, and Pietro Lorenzetti, and reworked them, with an added grace of northern and possibly Angevin derivation, onto the vigorous foundation of a local tradition; that he practised — perhaps as painter and carver together — the region's characteristic art of the monumental tabernacle, joining sculpture and painting in a single devotional machine; and that his authority was so great that the whole later history of Spoletan painting can be written as a series of footnotes to his example.

What remains open is nearly everything a conventional biography would want: his name, his dates, the extent of his hand in sculpture, the precise sequence of his works, the boundary between his own production and that of his close followers. These questions are not idle; they are the live agenda of current scholarship, and the honest reader of this artist must hold them open. To close them prematurely — to promote a hypothesis into a fact, to merge the painter and the sculptor without proof, to accept every "close to" attribution as autograph — would be to betray the very philology by which the Master of Fossa was recovered in the first place. He is, in the end, one of the finest demonstrations of what the connoisseurship of anonymity can achieve, and of the limits it must respect.

That limit is not a failure of scholarship but one of its conditions. Medieval art in this part of Italy is preserved in such uneven fragments that the historian is often forced to work from proximity rather than from certainty, and from families of works rather than from signatures. The Master of Fossa therefore exemplifies a mode of knowledge that is cumulative, comparative, and deliberately cautious: not the declaration of a personality in the modern biographical sense, but the slow reconstruction of an artistic presence from repeated formal choices.

Seen in that light, his anonymity becomes historically productive. It obliges the historian to ask different questions: how did a workshop transmit a repertory of faces, gestures, and tabernacle forms; how did a region maintain a visual language across generations; how did devotion, patronage, and local memory stabilize a style even when the maker's name vanished? The Master of Fossa is valuable precisely because he cannot be made to fit the neat narrative of a documented career.

He also reminds us that medieval authorship was often collective in practice even when it appears singular in retrospect. A shrine such as the Fossa tabernacle presupposes carving, painting, gilding, assembly, transport, installation, and, probably, periodic repair. The modern label attached to the whole object therefore compresses a chain of operations that may have involved more than one hand, more than one generation, and more than one moment of intervention. The master's name is a tool of order, not a transcript of how such objects were actually made.

At the same time, the coherence of the corpus should not be underestimated. Anonymous does not mean amorphous. The persistence of the master's physiognomies, draperies, and compositional habits across panels, sculpture, and frescoes is substantial enough to justify speaking of a single artistic intelligence, even if that intelligence operated within a workshop and a regional habitus. The biography written here therefore rests on a double discipline: the recognition of coherence, and the refusal to overstate what coherence can prove.

This is why the Master's best scholarship remains regional rather than merely nominal. His story can only be told alongside the geography of Spoleto, the route toward Abruzzo, the Assisi worksite, the history of earthquake damage, and the afterlives of local devotional forms. To study him in isolation would be to miss the very conditions that made him possible; to study him only as a node in a broader school would be to flatten the specificity of his own achievement. The right scale is somewhere between the two.

There is also a moral dimension to this kind of art history. The Master of Fossa survives because scholars, conservators, and local custodians kept returning to damaged objects, old photographs, and neglected churches with enough patience to see that something important was there. The task is not glamorous, and it rarely yields certainty. But it does recover texture, sequence, and relation, which are the materials from which historical understanding is built.

The consequence is that future work on the Master of Fossa will likely remain incremental. A cleaning might clarify an attribution; a restoration might expose an inscription; a rediscovered fragment might tighten a chronology. None of these events would by itself solve the biography, but each could improve the map. That is the proper horizon for an anonymous master: not final resolution, but better proportion, finer distinctions, and fewer false certainties.

In that sense the Master of Fossa is not just a subject of study but an argument for the discipline itself. He shows that art history at its best can reconstruct a world of practice without pretending to possess a full life story. It can trace influence without reducing it to dependence, distinguish workshop from hand, and preserve uncertainty without surrendering to vagueness. The biography ends where the evidence ends, but the historical intelligence it has required does not.

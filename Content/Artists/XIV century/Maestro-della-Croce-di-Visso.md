## A prefatory note on method

The subject of this essay is not a man with a name, a birthdate, or a documented career. He is a *critical construct*: a coherent artistic personality reassembled, entirely on the evidence of style, from a small group of carved and painted wooden crucifixes scattered across the upper valley of the river Nera, on the border between southern Umbria and the Marche. No contract, no signature, no chronicle entry, no notarial deed has ever been connected to him. What we call the "Master of the Cross of Visso" (*Maestro della Croce di Visso*, sometimes *Maestro del Crocifisso di Visso*) is therefore a *Notname* — a conventional name of convenience, coined by connoisseurs and attached to an eponymous object rather than to a person. Any "biography" of such a figure is unavoidably a biography of the scholarship: of the works, the attributions, the disputes, and the still-open questions. This essay is written on that footing, and it flags at every point where certainty ends and hypothesis begins. It also observes one further caution from the outset: **none of the works discussed here is signed. Every attribution is a stylistic judgment, and several are actively contested.** The reader who wants firm ground will find only two hard facts — that the eponymous crucifix exists, and that it is anonymous — and, around them, a rich and unusually contentious body of connoisseurship.

A second caution, specific to the name. The town of Visso possesses more than one celebrated "cross," and they must not be confused. The object that gives our master his name is a **carved wooden crucifix** of the early fourteenth century. It is entirely distinct from the late-Gothic **silver processional cross** (*croce astile*) of Abruzzese goldsmith's work, stolen from the collegiate church of Santa Maria di Visso in June 1973 and famously recovered in 1975 by Rodolfo Siviero — a work of metalwork, not sculpture, and unrelated to the painter-carvers discussed below. The distinction matters, because a search for "the cross of Visso" will surface the Siviero episode long before it surfaces the wooden Christ that concerns us here.

## Introduction: an artist without a name

Among the anonymous masters of Italian medieval art, the Master of the Cross of Visso occupies a peculiarly marginal and peculiarly instructive position. He belongs to a school that, until the last quarter of the twentieth century, scarcely existed in the literature at all: the wooden sculpture of the Trecento produced in the territory of the old Duchy of Spoleto and its Apennine hinterland. He is anonymous even by the generous standards of that school, where names are rare and documents rarer still. And he is doubly elusive because the central scholarly question about him is not *what did he make?* but *did he exist as a sculptor at all, or only as one half of a single artist who also painted?*

The reconstruction of the figure rests on the connoisseurial tradition inaugurated by [Roberto Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto) and carried into the domain of sculpture by his pupil [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni). It was Previtali who, across a series of studies published between 1965 and 1986, first argued that the polychrome wooden statuary of southern Umbria and the adjacent Marche and Abruzzo constituted a distinct artistic province with its own grammar, and who isolated within it a number of individual hands — among them the anonymous carver of the Visso crucifix. The figure was subsequently expanded, debated, and in part dismantled by later scholars, above all [Corrado Fratini](/scholars.html#fratini-corrado), [Andrea De Marchi](/scholars.html#de-marchi-andrea), and [Alessandro Delpriori](/scholars.html#delpriori-alessandro), whose 2015 monograph *La Scuola di Spoleto* gave the whole problem its fullest treatment to date.

What makes the Master of the Cross of Visso worth an essay of this length is precisely that he sits at the intersection of several large art-historical questions. He tests the limits of attribution by style alone in a territory nearly devoid of documents. He is the clearest single case for one of the most striking hypotheses in the study of central-Italian medieval art: that in the Spoleto region the workshop of the wood-carver and the workshop of the panel-painter were not merely allied but frequently *one and the same* — that a single artist conceived a sacred image in the round and then, or first, painted its narrative wings and its mourning figures. And, more soberly, he is a case study in the fragility of the material record: the crucifixes on which his identity depends are concentrated in exactly the towns — Visso, Norcia, Campi, Preci, Ussita — that were devastated by the Central Italy earthquakes of 2016. To write his biography in the second half of the 2020s is unavoidably to write about rescue, deposit, and reconstruction as much as about carving and painting.

The reader should keep three interlocking limitations in view throughout. First, the *evidentiary* limitation: the primary sources for this artist are the objects themselves — a handful of wooden Christs — and nothing else; there is no textual archive. Second, the *attributive* limitation: the corpus has grown and shrunk as different scholars have added and removed works, and its boundaries remain unsettled. Third, the *ontological* limitation: the very independence of the "Master of the Cross of Visso" as a personality distinct from the painter known as the "Master of the Cross of Trevi" is disputed, defended by some scholars and firmly denied by others. These are not marginal quibbles to be tidied away in footnotes; they are the substance of what can honestly be said about him.

## The land: Visso, the Valnerina, and "Umbria to the left of the Tiber"

To understand the Master of the Cross of Visso one must first understand the country that produced him, because in this instance geography is not background but argument. Visso lies in the province of Macerata, in the Marche, but culturally it belongs to a world oriented westward and southward — toward Spoleto, Norcia, and the valley of the Nera (the Valnerina) — rather than eastward toward the Adriatic. It sits in a basin at the confluence of five valleys in the heart of the Sibillini mountains, at the administrative edge of the Marche but at the cultural core of a region that modern boundaries obscure. The medieval and early-modern history of the surrounding castles — Croce, Ussita, Castelsantangelo sul Nera, Preci, Campi di Norcia — is a history of shifting allegiances among Norcia, Spoleto, Camerino, and the papacy, and of a dense, capillary settlement in which, as Delpriori has put it, every hill has its village, its pieve, and its small castle.

The decisive interpretive framework for this territory was supplied by Giovanni Previtali in his lectures on Umbrian Trecento sculpture, published in the early 1980s. Previtali proposed that Umbria in the fourteenth century divided culturally into two zones separated by the course of the Tiber. To the *right* of the river lay the Umbria of Perugia and Orvieto, more strongly influenced by Tuscany, where sculpture was dominated by the Sienese-inflected models radiating from the Orvieto cathedral workshop. To the *left* of the Tiber — *l'Umbria alla sinistra del Tevere*, the phrase that has since become a term of art — lay the Umbria of Foligno, Spoleto, and the Valnerina, a zone that spilled across the Apennine to embrace parts of the Marche and the Abruzzo, corresponding roughly to the old dioceses of Spoleto, Foligno, Ascoli Piceno, Teramo, L'Aquila, Sulmona, and Rieti. This left-bank Umbria was, in Previtali's account, less "Tuscan," far more expressive, and above all characterized by an intimate, symbiotic relationship between carving and polychromy that was peculiar to it.

That symbiosis is the second key to the region and to our master. In the Spoleto sphere, Previtali observed, wooden sculptures were conceived in a manner that can look schematic — with softened transitions and receding projections — precisely because they *demanded* gesso and vivid polychromy as an indispensable complement. The colour did not merely decorate the surface; it defined the form. Previtali coined a deliberately provocative phrase for these objects: they were, he wrote, "sculptures so to speak" (*sculture per modo di dire*), works that a Florentine or a Perugian would have refused to call sculpture at all, because they were only completed by the painter. The full sacred image — a Madonna and Child, a saint, a crucified Christ — emerged from the collaboration of chisel and brush, and often, the argument runs, from a single mind directing both.

This has a further material consequence that shaped the whole visual culture of the zone. Where the rest of Italy, in the age of the compartmentalized Gothic polyptych, produced gold-ground altarpieces subdivided into painted panels, left-bank Umbria produced something different: *monumental tabernacles* with a carved image (more rarely a painted one) at the centre and hinged wings that could be closed over it, the wings usually painted with the narrative of the figure enshrined within. It also produced large painted Calvaries and, above all, carved and painted crucifixes placed *in medio ecclesiae* — in the middle of the church, on the rood-beam or screen dividing nave from presbytery. Andrea De Marchi has stressed how alien this typological world is to the Tuscan or Venetian norm, and how tenaciously it resisted the imported Gothic polyptych, not out of mere archaism but as the proud assertion of a local identity that dialogued vigorously with Assisi and Giotto without ever assimilating to them.

The engine of that dialogue was Assisi itself. The great painting campaigns in the basilica of San Francesco — Cimabue and his circle in the transept and apse, then the workshops that produced the Franciscan cycle of the Upper Church around 1290, then Giotto and his followers in the Lower Church in the following decades — acted as a permanent school for the whole surrounding region. De Marchi speaks of an "electrifying" conjuncture, at its most intense between roughly 1260 and 1320, in whose shadow the Spoleto masters were formed. The painters and carvers of the Duchy absorbed the new Giottesque ideals directly and enthusiastically, but bent them to their own ends: toward a chromatic and material intensity, an exasperated mimicry of grief and tenderness, a high and nostalgic expressive temperature that (in De Marchi's reading) links the Duecento and the Trecento in this zone into a single continuous tradition. It is within exactly this milieu — the last, highest flowering of the "Spoleto school" in the first decades of the fourteenth century — that the Master of the Cross of Visso must be placed.

## The making of a Notname: Previtali and the reconstruction of Umbrian Trecento sculpture

The Master of the Cross of Visso does not appear in the older literature at all, for the simple reason that the field to which he belongs did not exist as a coherent object of study before the second half of the twentieth century. His construction is inseparable from the wider recovery of Umbrian Trecento wooden sculpture, and that recovery is above all the work of one scholar.

The foundational figure is Giovanni Previtali (1932–1988), a pupil of Roberto Longhi. Longhi himself had rehabilitated the painting of the Umbrian Trecento — the *"Passione degli umbri,"* as he memorably called it — in a celebrated course delivered at the University of Florence in 1953–54, published posthumously in 1973. But it was Previtali who extended Longhi's connoisseurial method into the neglected domain of sculpture. The decisive sequence of his interventions runs as follows. In 1965, in the journal *Paragone*, he published "Sulle tracce di una scultura umbra del Trecento," announcing the discovery of an anonymous carver he baptized the *Master of the Santa Caterina Gualino* after a wooden Madonna he had encountered — his "hidden gem," as he called it — at an antiques fair in Rome; this was the first of a series of unknown wood-carvers sharing a common formal language, a diffused pictorial sense of form, and a common geographical origin.

He added to that master's catalogue in a further *Paragone* note of 1966. Then, in the two-part "Due lezioni sulla scultura 'umbra' del Trecento," he laid out the interpretive architecture of the whole field: part II.1, "L'Umbria alla sinistra del Tevere. 1. Maestri espressionisti tra Assisi, Foligno e Spoleto," appeared in *Prospettiva* 38 (1984); the segment that concerns us most directly, "3. Tra Spoleto e L'Aquila: il 'Maestro della Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto' e quello del 'Crocifisso di Visso,'" appeared in *Prospettiva* 44 (1986). All of these studies were re-edited together in the volume *Studi sulla scultura gotica in Italia. Storia e geografia* (Turin: Einaudi, 1991).

It is in the 1986 essay that the Master of the Cross of Visso is properly defined. Previtali there isolated, alongside the "Master of the Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto," a distinct anonymous carver responsible for the Visso crucifix and for works related to it in style. The two masters were, for Previtali, exemplars of the same left-bank Umbrian culture — the "expressionist masters between Assisi, Foligno and Spoleto" of his 1984 title — and their reconstruction proceeded by the classic Longhian technique of grouping works by stylistic affinity across the modern administrative frontiers that had kept them apart.

The method deserves emphasis, because it is the only method available in this territory and because its results are only as secure as the eye that applies them. Previtali worked, in the words of his admirers, with an almost disarming simplicity: a fine philological description of stylistic detail, applied to objects that were genuinely new to scholarship and therefore hard to read. He built series — groups of works united by the treatment of a face, the fall of a loincloth, the striation of hair, the profile of a brow — and from those series he inferred hands and workshops. What he did *not* have, and what no subsequent scholar has had, is documentary corroboration. As Delpriori has bluntly acknowledged, the roster of artistic personalities active at Spoleto and in the Valnerina in the Trecento is a large set of "Masters" who do not yet have — and perhaps never will have — a civil name and a historical existence, in a territory almost wholly lacking archives and documents earlier than the late fourteenth century, obliterated by time and, repeatedly, by earthquakes.

Two methodological corollaries follow, both of which the honest reader must hold onto. First, the *stability* of a figure like the Master of the Cross of Visso depends on the persistence of the stylistic series that defines him; if a later scholar reassigns a key work, the figure himself is altered. This is not a hypothetical risk — it has happened, as we shall see, with the Tolentino figures. Second, the *boundaries* between one anonymous carver and his neighbours are inherently porous in a culture defined by shared workshop practice and shared debt to Assisi; the distinction between "the Master of the Cross of Visso" and "the Master of the Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto," or between the carver and a putative painter-self, is a distinction the modern eye draws, and different eyes have drawn it differently.

The two scholars who most extended Previtali's edifice were Corrado Fratini and Alessandro Delpriori, with Andrea De Marchi providing the most influential recent synthesis. Fratini, in a series of studies from the mid-1990s onward — notably "Per un riesame della pittura trecentesca e quattrocentesca nell'Umbria meridionale" (1997) and "Scultura lignea policroma e pittura nell'Umbria del Trecento" (in *Commentari d'arte*, 1998) — developed the crucial thesis that the carving and the painting in this zone issued from single workshops, and, taking up a hint already present in Previtali (1986), proposed that individual carvers and individual painters be matched into single personalities. Delpriori's *La Scuola di Spoleto* (2015), the expansion of his 2010 Florence doctoral thesis "Produzione figurativa a Spoleto e in Valnerina nel XIV secolo," gave the whole problem its systematic form, devoting an entire chapter (VII) to "Il Maestro della Croce di Trevi e il Maestro della Croce di Visso" and pressing the identification of the two hardest of all. De Marchi's introductory essay to that book, "Spoleto 1300: contesti, tipi e linguaggi di un paesaggio artistico," is the most eloquent statement of the case for the carver-painter unity.

## The eponymous work: the Crucifix of Visso

### Crucifix

This monumental *Christus patiens* belongs to the small but coherent group of carved crucifixes produced in the Spoletan orbit during the second quarter of the fourteenth century, and it is from this work that its anonymous author takes his conventional name. Christ is shown dead upon the cross, the head fallen onto the right shoulder, the eyes closed, the body suspended in a shallow curve from the outstretched arms — an image conceived less as an anatomical study of suffering than as an object of contemplative devotion, calibrated for the liturgical space of a rural collegiate church and for the affective piety of the communities of the upper Nera valley.

The sculptural language is distinctive and has been described with some precision in the critical literature. Giovanni Previtali, who first isolated the group, drew attention to its deliberate refusal of monumental scale and to a modelling that is enveloping, soft and convex — a surface worked without deep hollows, sharp undercutting or violent contrasts of light and shade. The result is a body defined by continuous, rounded volumes rather than by incisive carving: the ribcage is indicated more than dissected, the abdomen swells gently, and the loincloth falls in broad, unagitated folds. Previtali explicitly compared this handling to the softly modelled, French-inflected Marian statues gathered under the name of the Master of the Madonna of Spoleto Cathedral, and it is within that stylistic constellation — Spoleto, Fossa, Scurcola Marsicana — that the Visso Crucifix must be situated.

Decisive for the character of the work is the fact that carving and painting are not successive and independent operations but complementary phases of a single creative process. As in the polychrome Madonnas of the same milieu, the gesso preparation smooths and softens the carved form, while the pictorial surface — flesh tones, blood, the chromatic and possibly metallic treatment of the perizoma — completes the modelling rather than merely covering it. The image therefore stands at the intersection of two disciplines that modern connoisseurship has habitually separated, and it is precisely this integration that has driven the attributional debate around it.

That debate turns on a question of identity. Corrado Fratini, noting the striking homogeneity between the painted surface of the Visso Crucifix and the pictorial idiom of the painted Cross of Trevi, proposed identifying the Master of the Visso Crucifix with the Master of the Cross of Trevi — a single artistic personality operating with what he termed an "operative bivalence" across sculpture and panel painting. Alessandro Delpriori has taken up and developed this proposal in *La scuola di Spoleto* (2015), where the Visso Crucifix is examined at length. [Cristiana Pasqualetti](/scholars.html#pasqualetti-cristiana), while accepting the evidence of homogeneity, has argued that the phenomenon is better explained by the concept of a *polyvalent workshop* than by the fusion of two conventional names into one artist — a distinction of real methodological consequence, since it displaces the question from individual authorship to the organisation of labour in Appenninic workshops of the period.

The Visso Crucifix does not stand alone. It heads a small series of related carved crucifixes from the same cultural area, including those of San Domenico in Spoleto, of Preci, and of San Salvatore at Campi (Spoleto, Museo Diocesano), all four of which were brought together and catalogued in *Capolavori del Trecento. Il cantiere di Giotto, Spoleto e l'Appennino* (Spoleto–Trevi–Montefalco, 2018), where the Visso piece was discussed by Mariaceleste Di Meo. Considered as a group, they document the diffusion, on both flanks of the central Apennines, of a devotional idiom formed on the Assisi models and translated into a register at once tender and grave — an idiom that circulated freely across the diocesan and political frontier between the Duchy of Spoleto, the Marche and the Aquilano.

## Works attributed to the Master of the Cross of Visso

The catalogue of the Master of the Cross of Visso is small, entirely unsigned, and — a point that cannot be repeated too often — assembled and periodically revised by connoisseurial judgment. Its core consists of a group of wooden crucifixes clustered in the upper Nera valley and its extensions toward Rieti and Ascoli Piceno. Around that core lie additions and subtractions proposed by individual scholars, several of them still debated. What follows is an inventory of the works that have been brought into, or excluded from, his orbit, with the attribution history flagged in each case.

### The Crucifix of San Salvatore a Campi

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-Croce-di-Visso/image 3.jpg" alt=The Crucifix of San Salvatore a Campi">
  <figcaption>The Crucifix of San Salvatore a Campi, 1330-40, carved and polychromed wood (corpus); tempera on panel (painted terminal tablets), from the church of San Salvatore, Campi di Norcia; Museo Diocesano, Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

This crucifix belongs to the coherent group of carved *Christus patiens* figures produced in the Spoletan orbit during the second quarter of the fourteenth century and gathered by criticism under the conventional name of the Master of the Visso Crucifix — a nucleus that comprises, alongside the eponymous work at Visso, the crucifixes of San Domenico in Spoleto, of Preci, and this one from San Salvatore at Campi. Displayed against a dark ground, the sculpture retains an unusual completeness: the three-dimensional carved body of the dead Christ is set upon a cross whose polylobed terminals preserve their painted decoration, so that the object stands materially at the intersection of the two techniques — carving and panel painting — whose interdependence is the central problem of this workshop.

The corpus itself exemplifies the sculptural language first characterised by Giovanni Previtali: a modelling that is soft, enveloping and convex, worked without deep hollows, sharp undercutting or violent chiaroscuro. The dead Christ hangs in a shallow, quiet curve, the head fallen onto the right shoulder, the eyes closed, the ribcage indicated rather than dissected, the abdomen gently swelling. The perizoma falls across the hips in broad, calligraphically ordered folds, its white surface catching the light against the darker flesh — a passage in which the painted finish completes the carved form rather than merely covering it. Thin runnels of blood at the side wound and along the arms are painted, not carved; here again the pictorial layer is doing descriptive work that the chisel has deliberately left understated. The overall effect is grave and tender rather than dramatic, calibrated for the affective, contemplative piety of a rural community in the upper Nera valley and for the modest scale of a Benedictine pieve.

The painted terminal tablets carry the customary programme of a monumental painted-cross type transposed onto a sculpted crucifix: the mourning figures at the arm-ends, with the Virgin at Christ's right (the viewer's left) and the youthful Saint John the Evangelist at his left, and a further scene in the upper cimasa. Their pictorial idiom — the pale, elongated faces, the sharply drawn brows, the shot fabrics passing from one hue to another — is closely comparable to the painted surfaces of the related crucifixes and to the painted Cross of Trevi, and it is precisely this homogeneity that has driven the attributional debate.

That debate turns on the division of labour between carver and painter in the Appenninic workshops of the period. Corrado Fratini, noting the affinity between the painted surfaces of these crucifixes and the idiom of the Trevi cross, proposed identifying the Master of the Visso Crucifix with the Master of the Cross of Trevi — a single artistic personality of "operative bivalence" working across sculpture and painting. Alessandro Delpriori (*La scuola di Spoleto*, 2015) has developed this line, while Cristiana Pasqualetti, accepting the evidence of homogeneity, has preferred the concept of a *polyvalent workshop* to the fusion of two conventional names — a distinction that shifts the question from individual authorship to the organisation of production. The Campi crucifix, preserving both the carved corpus and its painted apparatus in a single object, is among the most eloquent witnesses to that integration.

The work's later history is bound up with the fragility of its region. Removed from San Salvatore to the parish house and then to the Museo Diocesano di Spoleto to guard against the thefts that plagued the area, it thereby escaped the fate of the church itself, which was ruined in the 2016 central Italy earthquakes and largely collapsed in the following days.

This crucifix is one of the small, coherent series of carved *Christus patiens* figures produced in the Spoletan orbit in the second quarter of the fourteenth century and assembled by criticism under the conventional name of the Master of the Visso Crucifix — a nucleus comprising the eponymous crucifix at Visso, this example from San Domenico in Spoleto, the crucifix of Campi di Norcia, and that of Preci. Unlike the Visso and Campi pieces, which retain the polylobed cross with its painted terminal tablets, the San Domenico corpus survives as a sculpture alone: it is here shown mounted on a modern metal stand, the crossbar and base being twentieth-century display supports rather than the medieval cross, which is lost. The image should therefore be read as a museum installation of the figure, not as the original devotional object in its entirety.

The corpus embodies the sculptural language first isolated by Giovanni Previtali: a modelling that is soft, enveloping and convex, worked without deep hollows, aggressive undercutting or violent contrasts of light and shade. Christ hangs dead from outstretched arms, the head fallen forward and to the right, the eyes closed, the long hair descending in a heavy lock over the right shoulder — a formula of quiet, interiorised suffering rather than muscular agony. The torso is rendered in continuous rounded volumes, the ribs indicated with restraint beneath a smooth abdomen; here, if anything, the note of pathos is a little more insistent than in the companion pieces, the fallen head and the slack, elongated limbs pressing the affective register that suited the meditative piety these images were made to serve.

Characteristic of the group is the interdependence of carving and painted finish. The perizoma is the passage where this is most legible: knotted at the right hip and falling in an ordered cascade of folds, its surface carries a painted linear decoration — fine stripes and patterning — that supplements the carved drapery rather than merely tinting it. The blood at the wounds of hands, side and feet, and the thin runnels down the arms and torso, are likewise painted, doing descriptive work the chisel has deliberately understated. This integration of the two techniques — modelled form completed by pictorial surface — is precisely the feature that has made the group so important for the historiographical debate about Spoletan workshops, in which the boundary between sculptor and painter is difficult to draw.

That debate is well known. Corrado Fratini, struck by the affinity between the painted surfaces of these crucifixes and the pictorial idiom of the painted Cross of Trevi, proposed identifying the Master of the Visso Crucifix with the Master of the Cross of Trevi — a single personality of "operative bivalence" working across sculpture and panel. Alessandro Delpriori (*La scuola di Spoleto*, 2015) has extended this reading, treating the Master of the Cross of Trevi and the Master of the Visso Crucifix together, while Cristiana Pasqualetti, granting the homogeneity, has preferred the notion of a *polyvalent workshop* to the merging of two conventional names — a distinction that relocates the question from authorship to the organisation of production. Wherever one stands, the San Domenico crucifix is a primary document of that shared Spoletan enterprise, formed on the Assisi models and diffused across both flanks of the central Apennines.

## Style and sources: the carved body and the painted skin

If the Master of the Cross of Visso can be characterized as an artistic personality at all, it is through a cluster of formal and expressive traits that recur across his crucifixes and that scholars have read as the signature of a single sensibility.

The first and most fundamental is the *dependence of carved form on painted surface*. As set out above, this is not incidental to the master but constitutive of him and of the whole culture Previtali reconstructed. The wooden Christ is carved with a deliberate economy — an essential, almost abbreviated modelling — that presupposes completion in gesso and colour. The polychromy is not cosmetic; it supplies the anatomical reading, the bruising, the blood, the very legibility of the flesh. This is what Previtali meant by "sculptures so to speak," and it is why the study of these objects cannot separate the sculptor's contribution from the painter's without distorting both. The methodological consequence — that carver and painter may be one — is discussed in the next section; the stylistic consequence is that the master's "hand" is visible as much in a choice of painted welt or trickle of blood as in a cut of the chisel.

The second trait is *expressive extremity*. Previtali grouped these artists under the rubric of "expressionist masters," and the term has stuck because it fits. The Visso Christ, with its head wrenched round in spasm and its half-open dying eyes, pushes the *Christus patiens* type past the point of decorous suffering into something harsher and more visceral. This is a devotional art of maximum emotional involvement, calibrated to the theatricalized piety of the Umbrian mystics — the world of Jacopone da Todi's *laude*, of [Angela da Foligno](/Content/Saints/Angela-da-Foligno.html), of Clare of Montefalco — in which the image functions as a direct and almost violent medium between the worshipper and God.

The third trait is the *particular reception of Giotto*. The masters of the Spoleto school were formed in the shadow of Assisi, and the Visso master's crucifixes register, specifically, the impact of Giotto's Crucifixion in the right transept of the Lower Basilica. But — and this is De Marchi's crucial observation — the reception is not imitative. Where Giotto monumentalizes and composes, the Visso master inverts the sign, translating Giottesque gravity into raw laceration. The bruises of the flagellation over the pure, ivory-white body are De Marchi's chosen example of this "capovolgimento di segno," this reversal and radical reinvention. The result is an art that is unthinkable without Giotto and yet, in temperature and purpose, at the opposite pole from him.

A fourth trait, more contextual than formal, is the master's *typological embeddedness*. His crucifixes were not free-standing devotional curiosities but functional objects within a specific liturgical furniture: crosses set *in medio ecclesiae*, on rood-beams or screens, sometimes integrated (as at Campi) with painted mourning figures on their terminal tablets, sometimes (as the whole logic of the region suggests) coordinated with painted Calvaries, tabernacles, and dossals produced by the same or an allied workshop. To read the Visso master's style in isolation from this system of images is to misread it. The De Marchi–Delpriori reconstruction insists that these objects be understood as parts of coordinated ensembles produced under a single workshop responsibility — an insistence that returns us to the central problem of the master's identity.

It is worth registering, as a matter of intellectual honesty, that the "expressionist" reading of this material — inherited from Longhi's *Passione degli umbri* and elaborated by Previtali — is itself an interpretive lens with a history. It served, in the mid-twentieth century, to redeem a body of provincial work that older, Tuscan-centred criticism had ignored or disdained, by recasting its "irregularities" as expressive virtues. That framing has been enormously fruitful, but a careful scholar will remember that "expressionism" is a category the twentieth century brought *to* these objects, not one they announce of themselves. The Visso master's art is genuinely and unusually intense; whether "expressionist" is the last word on it, or a period label that later criticism may refine, is a question worth keeping open.

## Painter or sculptor? The identity thesis and the Master of the Cross of Trevi

The single most important — and most contested — question about the Master of the Cross of Visso is whether he existed as a sculptor distinct from a painter, or whether "he" is one half of a single artist who worked in both media. This is not a peripheral curiosity; it goes to the very ontology of the figure, and the scholarly community is genuinely divided on it.

The painter at the centre of the question is the **Master of the Cross of Trevi** (*Maestro della Croce di Trevi*), named after a painted cross from the church of San Francesco at Trevi. This painter is also known in the literature under two other conventional names attached to other works: the **Master of the Cini Diptych** (*Maestro del dittico Cini*), after a diptych in the Galleria di Palazzo Cini in Venice — a work whose L'Aquila provenance was already remarked by Longhi — and the **Master of the Poldi Pezzoli Diptych** (*Maestro del dittico Poldi Pezzoli*), after a diptych in the Museo Poldi Pezzoli in Milan; the Fondazione Federico Zeri catalogues him under the double name "Maestro della Croce di Trevi (Maestro del dittico Poldi Pezzoli)." His painted corpus, as reconstructed by Delpriori and others, includes the eponymous Trevi cross, the Cini and Poldi Pezzoli diptychs, a Crucifixion dossal in the Vatican Pinacoteca (from San Francesco a Montefalco), a Deposition of Christ in the sepulchre (formerly Alana Collection, Newark), a Crucifix of San Francesco datable c. 1320–25, the fragmentary painted Calvary in the counter-façade of Santa Maria a Preci, and frescoes recently recognized in the L'Aquila region. He is characterized, alongside the more "colourful and colloquial" Master of Cesi, as the most "spare and incandescent" of the Spoleto Giottesques, whose language grew steadily more autonomous from its Giottesque prototypes.

The identity thesis proceeds as follows. Its remote root is a hint dropped by Previtali in 1986. It was developed into a general principle by Corrado Fratini, who argued (1997, 1998) that in the Duchy of Spoleto the workshop of the carver and the workshop of the painter coincided, and who spoke of the "operative bivalence" (*bivalenza operativa*) of these masters. Fratini's most cited piece of evidence is not a crucifix but a signature: the wooden Madonna delle Concanelle at Bugnara (now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo, L'Aquila), signed in 1262 with the verb *DEPINSERUNT* ("they painted") by the Spoletan painter Machilone and his son — a painter who also signed panels jointly with Simeone, and who, Fratini argues, would hardly have inscribed a *painter's* signature on a wooden statue unless he claimed responsibility for the whole object. The general practice thus attested — a single workshop responsible for both intaglio and polychromy — is then applied to the Trecento pairs: the Master of Cesi with the sculptor of the Santa Cristina di Caso; the Master of Fossa with the Master of the Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto; and, our case, the Master of the Cross of Trevi with the Master of the Cross of Visso.

Alessandro Delpriori pressed the specific identification of the Visso carver with the Trevi painter repeatedly and in full in his 2015 monograph, and **Andrea De Marchi** endorsed and amplified it in his introduction to that book. Their decisive object is the Campi cross, where a Christ of the Visso group carries *dolenti* of the Trevi group on one and the same cross — a coincidence they read as demonstrating that the two "masters" are one artist working in two media. The counter-façade of Santa Maria a Preci, pairing a Visso-group crucifix with a Trevi-group Calvary in a single church, is adduced as corroboration.

But the thesis is far from universally accepted, and the honest statement of the case must give the dissent its full weight. [Elvio Lunghi](/scholars.html#lunghi-elvio), in his study of the pictorial cycles and medieval works of art in the cathedral of Spoleto (2002), *firmly denied* the proposed anagraphic identity between the painter (Master of the Cross of Trevi / Poldi Pezzoli Diptych) and the sculptor. Fratini himself, even while arguing for workshop unity, framed the relationship as "operative bivalence" rather than strict personal identity, and returned to the question of professional and administrative boundaries between carving and painting along the Apennine ridge in a 2001 study. Most recently, **Cristiana Pasqualetti** has offered a pointed terminological correction: to the "operative bivalence" attributed to the master she prefers the concept of a "polyvalent workshop" (*bottega polivalente*) — a formulation that preserves the observed coordination of carving and painting while stopping short of collapsing two conventional masters into one hand. Others, such as **Lorenzo Mor**, have urged general caution about equating painter and sculptor across the whole body of thirteenth- and fourteenth-century Umbrian and Abruzzese wooden sculpture, noting that a *DEPINSERUNT* signature need not by itself prove that painter and carver were the same person.

The state of the question, then, is this. It is agreed by nearly all recent scholars that carving and painting in the Spoleto region were unusually integrated, and that the crucifixes of the "Visso" type were routinely completed and coordinated with "Trevi"-type painting. It is *not* agreed whether this integration was the product of a single artist working in two media (Delpriori, De Marchi), a "polyvalent workshop" under unified direction (Pasqualetti), an "operative bivalence" short of personal identity (Fratini), or two allied but distinct personalities (Lunghi). A responsible account cannot adjudicate this; it can only lay out the positions and their evidence. What it can say is that the very name "Master of the Cross of Visso" now denotes, for some scholars, an independent sculptor, and for others merely the sculptural face of the painter of Trevi — and that the reader should be alert to which sense a given author intends.

There is also a live sub-question about the painter's, and therefore possibly the carver's, geographical origin. The recognition of frescoes by the Master of the Cross of Trevi in the L'Aquila region has, as Pasqualetti notes, reopened the question of where the painter came from and revived an earlier conjecture of [Ferdinando Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando) on the point. The details of that conjecture lie beyond what can be responsibly summarized here, but its revival is a reminder that even the regional labelling of these masters — "Spoletan," "Umbrian," "Abruzzese" — is provisional, and that the true field of activity of the Visso/Trevi personality may straddle the Apennine more evenly than the name "of Visso" suggests.

## Chronology

No document dates any work by the Master of the Cross of Visso, and so his chronology is entirely inferential, resting on style and on the datable milieu around him.

The consensus places his eponymous crucifix in the early years of the second quarter of the fourteenth century — the later 1320s or earliest 1330s — on the strength of [Zappasodi](/scholars.html#zappasodi-emanuele)'s association of it with the collegiate building campaign at Visso of 1324–32. If the identification with the Master of the Cross of Trevi is accepted, the personality's activity can be triangulated against the painter's better-anchored milieu. The Trevi painter runs parallel to the Master of Cesi, whose securely datable works include the Montefalco dossal of 1308 and the Vallo di Nera Marian altarpiece of 1315, and whose latest documented undertaking — the decoration of the chapel of the Cross in Santa Chiara at Montefalco — was commissioned in 1333 by the rector of the Duchy, the Frenchman Jean d'Amiel. The recovery, during restoration for the 2018 exhibition, of the date 1336 and of the same Jean d'Amiel's name in the inscription of the Vatican Montefalco polyptych further fixes the milieu in the 1330s. A crucifix of San Francesco assigned to the Trevi painter has been dated c. 1320–25; the Deposition formerly in the Alana Collection, c. 1330.

Taken together, these anchors suggest that the artistic personality behind the Visso crucifixes — whether a single carver-painter or a coordinated workshop — was active across roughly the first third of the fourteenth century and into its second quarter, say c. 1310/15–1340, with the eponymous Visso Christ falling near the middle of that span. This is a plausible and internally consistent chronology, but it must be labelled for what it is: a network of stylistic inferences hung on a handful of dated *neighbouring* works, not a documented career. Any future discovery — an inscription, a datable restoration layer beneath later repaint, a payment record — could shift it.

## The scholars: a historiographical map

Because the Master of the Cross of Visso is a creature of scholarship, an account of him is incomplete without an account of the scholars who made and remade him. The following map identifies the figures whose work bears directly on him, in rough order of their contribution.

Roberto Longhi (1890–1970) is the distant progenitor: his rehabilitation of Umbrian Trecento painting, above all in the 1953–54 Florence course published in 1973, established both the method (attribution by style) and the sympathy for provincial "expressionism" on which the whole field rests. Giovanni Previtali (1932–1988) is the proximate founder: his studies of 1965, 1966, 1984, and 1986, re-edited in 1991, created the category of Umbrian Trecento wooden sculpture, coined the concept of "l'Umbria alla sinistra del Tevere," and defined the Master of the Cross of Visso himself in 1986. [Bruno Toscano](/scholars.html#toscano-bruno) supplied the complementary territorial and "geographic" method — attentive to what has been lost as much as to what survives — and, with collaborators, the fundamental *Manuali del territorio* on the Valnerina (1977) and Spoleto (1978); his 1995 study "Conservato e perduto in territorio spoletino" is basic for the region's losses.

Corrado Fratini developed the carver-painter unity thesis (1997, 1998, 2001) that underlies the modern debate about the master's identity. Andrea De Marchi is the most influential recent interpreter of the whole Spoleto milieu and the strongest advocate of the carver-painter identity, above all in his 2015 introduction to Delpriori. Alessandro Delpriori is the master's monographer: his 2010 Florence doctorate and its 2015 book *La Scuola di Spoleto* remain the fullest treatment, and he co-curated with [Vittoria Garibaldi](/scholars.html#garibaldi-vittoria) the 2018 exhibition *Capolavori del Trecento* whose catalogue contains the current standard entries on the crucifixes (by M. Di Meo and G. Massari). Elvio Lunghi, a specialist in Umbrian wooden sculpture, is the principal published dissenter from the carver-painter identity (2002) and an important voice on thirteenth-century Umbrian *scultura lignea* more broadly (e.g., his 2011 study in *L'Umbria nel XIII secolo*). **Enrica Neri Lusanna** proposed the (subsequently revised) Tolentino attribution and has written on the related Camerino material. Emanuele Zappasodi added the Ascoli crucifix to the corpus, dated the eponymous work in relation to the Visso collegiate campaign, and has studied the enclosed spaces of female monasteries in which such images functioned (his doctoral work *Sorores reclusae*). Cristiana Pasqualetti has most recently refined the identity debate with the concept of the "polyvalent workshop" and has extended the study of the Trevi painter into the Abruzzo.

Behind these direct contributors stand others whose work frames the field: [Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos) and [Carlo Volpe](/scholars.html#volpe-carlo), who around 1970 planned an (unrealized) study of Umbrian Trecento painting; [Millard Meiss](/scholars.html#meiss-millard), an early student of the Master of Cesi (1962); [Filippo Todini](/scholars.html#todini-filippo), whose 1989 repertory *La pittura umbra dal Duecento al primo Cinquecento* catalogued these painters (and drew De Marchi's sharp methodological criticism for atomizing them out of context); [Luciano Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano) and Ferdinando Bologna, both of whom touched the milieu; [Matteo Mazzalupi](/scholars.html#mazzalupi-matteo), who published Master of Fossa material relevant to the parallel carver-painter case (2009); and [Gaetano Curzi](/scholars.html#curzi-gaetano), who reconstructed the sculptor-painters Giacometto and Giuliano di Paolo. The point of this roll-call is not completeness for its own sake but a warning: to cite "the Master of the Cross of Visso" is to enter an active, unfinished conversation, and any statement about him inherits the position of the scholar one is following.

## After 2016: earthquake, rescue, and the present state

No account of this master written today can omit the catastrophe that has overtaken the very towns on which his corpus depends. The Central Italy earthquake sequence of 2016–2017 — the shocks of 24 August 2016, and the still more destructive shocks of 26 and 30 October 2016, with aftershocks into early 2017 — had its epicentres precisely in the upper Nera valley and the Sibillini: Visso, Norcia, Preci, Campi, Ussita, Castelsantangelo sul Nera. The heritage consequences for the Visso master's world were severe.

At Visso, the collegiate church of Santa Maria was heavily damaged; among the works rescued from it by MiBACT technicians and firefighters in February 2017 was the detached fresco of the niche on the right wall, a work of c. 1515–25 by Giovanni di Pietro, called "lo Spagna" — a sixteenth-century object, not our master's, but an index of the church's importance and its peril. The Museo Civico-Diocesano, housed in the adjacent deconsecrated church of Sant'Agostino (a building itself of the 1330s), was rendered unusable; the great bulk of its holdings was evacuated to a deposit at San Severino Marche while the emergency continued. In the wider rescue operation, restorers, art historians, architects, and Civil Protection volunteers secured some three hundred works of art recovered from the rubble across the comuni of the zone — dangerous work carried out in the "red zone" amid continuing tremors. The complex of Sant'Agostino, and with it the future home of the museum, remained the object of public appeals for years afterward; as of the mid-2020s the historic centre of Visso was still largely a restricted zone, with reconstruction proceeding unevenly and the museum's future site not yet settled.

At Campi di Norcia, the church of San Salvatore — the original home of the composite cross whose Christ is attributed to the Visso master and whose *dolenti* are attributed to the Trevi painter — was destroyed. The cross itself, already dismembered and dispersed in the twentieth century and reunited before the earthquake, is reported in recent literature as being in the Museo Diocesano di Spoleto; its fate and the status of San Salvatore's other contents are part of the vast ongoing recovery and reconstruction effort, and current details should be verified with the competent offices.

For the eponymous crucifix specifically, the responsible statement is a careful one. The Visso museum's collections were evacuated and placed in deposit; the crucifix is to be understood as among the works removed from Sant'Agostino and safeguarded during the emergency, rather than as on display in Visso. Its precise present location and conservation condition are matters for the Soprintendenza Archeologia, Belle Arti e Paesaggio delle Marche, the Ufficio del Soprintendente Speciale per le aree colpite dal sisma del 2016, and the Archdiocese of Camerino–San Severino Marche, and any scholar or visitor should confirm the current situation directly with them. What is not in doubt is that the earthquake has made the study — and the physical survival — of this small, dispersed, fragile corpus a matter of some urgency, and that several of the churches that gave the master's works their context (San Salvatore a Campi above all) no longer stand as they did.

## Assessment and open questions

What, in the end, can be said with confidence about the Master of the Cross of Visso? Less than one would wish, and more than the bare anonymity might suggest.

With confidence: that there survives, in the museum of Visso, a carved and polychromed wooden Christ of exceptional expressive intensity, datable on stylistic and circumstantial grounds to the first half of the fourteenth century; that this Christ belongs to a small group of related crucifixes in the upper Nera valley and its extensions toward Rieti and Ascoli Piceno; that these works are products of the distinctive artistic culture of "left-bank" Umbria reconstructed by Previtali, in which carving and polychromy were fused into a single expressive system; and that this culture drew on, and radically transformed, the Giottesque achievement at Assisi.

As hypothesis, and no more: that these crucifixes are the work of a single anonymous carver; that this carver is identical with, or the sculptural persona of, the painter known as the Master of the Cross of Trevi (and thus also of the Cini and Poldi Pezzoli diptychs); and that his activity fell roughly between about 1315 and 1340. Each of these propositions is defended by serious scholars and contested by others; the identity thesis in particular is affirmed by Delpriori and De Marchi, nuanced by Fratini and Pasqualetti, and denied by Lunghi.

The open questions are, correspondingly, three. First, the *identity* question: was there a Master of the Cross of Visso at all, as a sculptor distinct from the Master of the Cross of Trevi, or is the name merely a label for the carving side of a single, or a workshop-based, enterprise? This is unlikely to be settled without new evidence — a signature, a document, or a technical study capable of distinguishing hands within the coordinated carving-and-painting process. Second, the *corpus* question: the boundaries of his catalogue remain fluid, as the migration of the Tolentino figures out of it and the Ascoli crucifix into it both show; further additions and subtractions are to be expected as the material is restudied after the earthquake. Third, the *geography* question: the recognition of related work as far as L'Aquila and Ascoli Piceno, and the revival of Bologna's conjecture about the painter's origin, suggest that "of Visso" may be a convenient rather than an accurate territorial label, and that the true theatre of this personality's activity straddled the Apennine watershed.

There is, finally, a larger significance to this obscure figure that justifies the attention paid to him here. The Master of the Cross of Visso is one of the clearest test cases for a proposition that, if it holds, changes how we think about the division of artistic labour in medieval central Italy: that in one well-defined region, and against the specialized practice normal in Siena or Venice, a single hand or a single workshop could both carve the sacred body and paint its skin, its narrative, and its grief. He is also a reminder — sharpened by the events of 2016 — of how much of the medieval record survives only precariously, in small towns and mountain churches, and of how thin the thread is on which even the identity of an "artist" can hang: in this case, a single wooden Christ, a handful of related crucifixes, and a scholarly conversation still very much in progress.

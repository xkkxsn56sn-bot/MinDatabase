*Giovanni di Ugo da Campione* was one of the most accomplished sculptor-architects of fourteenth-century Lombardy, a leading representative of the celebrated *Maestri Campionesi* tradition who left an indelible mark on the ecclesiastical landscape of the Po Valley and the Lombard lake districts. Though his precise birth date remains unrecorded, scholarly consensus places his birth at approximately 1320 in Campione, a small village on the eastern shore of Lake Lugano — today known as Campione d'Italia — within the territory broadly administered under the diocese of Como.

## Family Origins and Dynastic Context

Giovanni di Ugo da Campione was born into one of the most distinguished families of the *Magistri Campionesi*, a loosely organized guild of stonemasons, sculptors, and architect-builders who had migrated from the village of Campione and surrounding localities on Lake Lugano to serve the great ecclesiastical patrons of northern Italy from roughly the eleventh century onward. His father, Ugo da Campione, was himself an accomplished *magister* — a master builder and sculptor — whose name appears in two surviving epigraphs that bracket the chronology of his life and activity: the first, dated 1353, reads *Iohannes filius magistri Ugi*, confirming that Ugo was still alive in that year; the second, of 1360, gives *Iohannes filius quondam domini magistri Ugi*, establishing his father's death between those two dates. The family operated primarily out of Bergamo, where Ugo had established a workshop of considerable repute, and it was in this environment that Giovanni received his earliest and most formative professional training.

Giovanni documents himself in surviving inscriptions and archival records variously as *Iohannes de Campilione*, *Iohannes de Cumis*, and simply *Iohannes*, reflecting the fluid geographic and civic identities that characterized the itinerant Campionese builders of his era. The Campionesi were not a monolithic school in any formally constituted sense, but rather a kinship-based network of craftsmen who passed technical knowledge — including stonecutting, portal design, and figural relief — from generation to generation within the family unit. Giovanni thus inherited not merely his father's tools and workshop but an entire intellectual and aesthetic patrimony that had been accumulating for more than a century. 

His mother's identity remains entirely unknown, as does any information about siblings, though the collaborative nature of Campionese practice suggests that the workshop was almost certainly a familial enterprise involving multiple relatives beyond the immediate father-son dyad. The strong patrilineal character of artisan transmission in medieval Lombardy meant that Giovanni's professional identity was inseparable from that of his father, and he continued throughout his career to identify himself by patronymic reference to Ugo — a gesture that carried both sentimental and legal-professional weight in the workshop economy of the period.

The family lineage extended beyond Giovanni himself: his sons Nicolino and Cristoforo are both documented as active collaborators in his workshop during the early 1360s, with Nicolino appearing in the account books of the Misericordia Maggiore of Bergamo<a href="../../../endnotes.html#fn-misericordia-maggiore-bergamo" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> from April 1362 through at least February 1363. A document from 1364 records that Nicolino made a journey — described with the phrase *pro designando ostium ecclesiae* — on behalf of the workshop, suggesting that he had already assumed the role of site representative and design consultant on behalf of his father's enterprise. Cristoforo, the second attested son, is mentioned in connection with work on the Baptistery of Bergamo, though his individual contribution is more difficult to disentangle from the collective output of the workshop. 

The continuity of the family workshop across at least three generations — Ugo, Giovanni, and then Nicolino and Cristoforo — reflects the dynastic logic of Campionese artisan practice, in which mastery was both inherited and transmitted as a form of cultural capital. It is in this context that Giovanni's career must be understood: not as an isolated individual genius but as the most distinguished node in a multigenerational network of craftsmen who collectively shaped the built environment of Trecento Lombardy.

The broader *Maestri Campionesi* community to which Giovanni's family belonged was deeply embedded in the ecclesiastical economies of northern Italian cities, particularly Bergamo, Como, and Milan. The Campionesi were habitually employed by confraternities, cathedral chapters, and mendicant orders — patrons who required not just skilled labor but the kind of institutional reliability and accumulated expertise that a family workshop could uniquely provide. Giovanni's father Ugo had, around 1314, participated in work on the *Loggia degli Osii* in Piazza Mercanti in Milan, one of the great civic architectural projects of early Trecento Lombardy, a commission that speaks to the high standing the family had achieved within the regional building trade. 

Giovanni's own career built upon this foundation of civic and ecclesiastical connection, extending the family's geographic reach from Bergamo across the lake districts of Como and into the broader Lombard plain. The family was thus simultaneously a biological unit, a professional corporation, and a cultural institution — a fact that gives Giovanni's biography a depth and complexity that purely individual biography cannot adequately capture.

Though the precise circumstances of Giovanni's domestic life remain opaque — no marriage contract, no testament, and no record of private property has survived in the available documentation — the workshop records of the MIA in Bergamo give indirect testimony to a stable and prosperous household capable of sustaining multiple simultaneous contracts. 

The regularity of weekly salary payments to Giovanni and his assistants between 1361 and 1367, the presence of named sons in the accounts, and the complexity of the decorative programs he designed all suggest a substantial familial enterprise with deep roots in the local community. Giovanni's death is conventionally placed around 1375, a date extrapolated by modern scholars from the cessation of documentary references after 1367 and from analogy with the documented deaths of other Campionese masters of the same generation. The exact cause of his death is not recorded in any surviving source, though the prevalence of epidemic disease in northern Italy during the third quarter of the fourteenth century — including the catastrophic recurrences of plague in 1361–1363 and 1373–1374 — makes natural death from infectious illness a plausible hypothesis.

## Patronage Networks

The principal institutional patron of Giovanni di Ugo da Campione's career was the *Consorzio della Misericordia Maggiore* (MIA) of Bergamo, a powerful confraternity that had assumed administrative and financial responsibility for the fabric of the Basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore, one of the most prestigious Romanesque churches in Lombardy. The MIA's extensive account books — preserved in the Biblioteca Civica of Bergamo under the shelf-marks MSS 845, 846, and 847 — constitute the single most important documentary source for Giovanni's biography, recording weekly salary payments to him and his assistants across multiple decades of engagement with the basilica's building and sculptural programs. The confraternity's interest in the continuous embellishment of Santa Maria Maggiore reflected the deep civic pride of Bergamo's merchant and professional classes, who saw the basilica as the pre-eminent symbol of the city's cultural ambitions and religious identity. Giovanni's relationship with the MIA was not a single commission but rather a long-term professional partnership that made him effectively the principal artistic officer of the basilica's construction program for nearly three decades.

The Baptistery of Bergamo, begun under Giovanni's direction around 1340, also reflects a form of institutional patronage closely tied to the cathedral chapter and the bishop of Bergamo, who would have exercised ultimate spiritual and juridical authority over a building used for the administration of the sacrament of baptism. The octagonal plan of the baptistery, its pyramidal covering, and the elaborate sculptural program of Theological and Cardinal Virtues at its corners all speak to a patron willing to invest considerable resources in a monument of theological as well as architectural ambition. The original placement of the baptistery at the center of the nave of Santa Maria Maggiore, near the presbyterial area, suggests that it was conceived as a monumental liturgical furnishing of the first order, occupying a position of exceptional prominence within one of Lombardy's most visited pilgrimage churches.

The north portal (*protiro*) of Santa Maria Maggiore, completed in 1351 and bearing Giovanni's signature in two separate inscriptions, was a commission that directly reflected the aspirations of the MIA to compete with the great portal programs of Tuscan and Emilian cathedral architecture. The *protiro* was designed not merely as a functional entrance but as a programmatic theological statement, its sculptural program organized around the equestrian statue of Sant'Alessandro — the patron saint of Bergamo — flanked by saints Barnaba and Proiettizio, and surmounted by a group of the Virgin enthroned with Saints Grata and Esteria. The choice of Sant'Alessandro as the central figure of the portal is deeply political as well as devotional, aligning the MIA's patronage with the civic mythology of Bergamo and its claims to apostolic foundation through the martyrdom of the Roman soldier Alexander. This conflation of civic identity, saintly patronage, and architectural display is entirely characteristic of the confraternal patronage model that Giovanni served throughout his career.

The south portal of Santa Maria Maggiore, completed in 1360 and equally bearing Giovanni's name, was likewise funded by the MIA and continued the decorative program of the north *protiro* in a stylistically distinct but iconographically complementary key. The southern entrance is distinguished by its white-marble *leoni stilofori* — the emblematic column-bearing lions of Campionese tradition — and by a frieze in the crowning band populated with figures of Christ, the Apostles, saints, and allegorical representations of the trades and professions, a subject unusual in Lombard portal iconography and suggestive of the confraternity's desire to celebrate the dignity of artisan labor and civic virtue alongside sacred history. This iconographic program reflects the hybrid character of confraternal patronage, which blended conventional ecclesiastical imagery with the social values and professional self-representation of the merchant and artisan classes who financed the work. The minor portal on the north side of the choir, executed between 1366 and 1367, was a further MIA commission, this time involving Giovanni's son Nicolino as a site representative, confirming the family workshop's continuing monopoly on the basilica's major sculptural contracts.

The commission for the restoration of the parish church of Santi Giorgio, Nazario e Celso at Bellano — on the western shore of Lake Como — came from the local parish community following the catastrophic flooding of the River Pioverna in 1341, which had partially destroyed the fabric of the building. A document dated 18 July 1348 records the final payment to Giovanni and two collaborators — Antonio di Giacomo di Castellazzo di Peglio and Comolo di Goffredo di Osteno — for work carried out according to a contract agreed the previous year, indicating that the commission was organized through a formal written agreement between the parish and the workshop. This type of rural ecclesiastical patronage, though less prestigious than the great confraternal commissions of Bergamo, was equally important to the economic sustainability of the Campionese workshop model, providing steady employment between the larger and more sporadic urban contracts. The geographic reach of the Bellano commission also demonstrates that Giovanni's reputation extended well beyond Bergamo into the broader lake district of Como, where the memory of Campionese excellence in ecclesiastical construction was deeply rooted.

The monument to Cardinal Guglielmo Longhi degli Alessandrini, positioned within Santa Maria Maggiore in Bergamo, has long been associated with the Campionese tradition and specifically with the workshop environment in which Giovanni was trained, though current scholarship prefers a cautiously generic attribution to the broader Campionese milieu rather than to Ugo or Giovanni individually. Cardinal Longhi, who had served as Bishop of Bergamo and was a major benefactor of Santa Maria Maggiore, was a patron of immense prestige, and the funerary monument erected in his honor represents the highest level of aristocratic and ecclesiastical patronage available in Trecento Bergamo. The association of Giovanni's workshop with the Longhi monument — even if indirect and mediated through his father's earlier activity — positioned the family at the very apex of Bergamasque artistic patronage and ensured their continued employment by the institutions that Cardinal Longhi had served and endowed.

## Sculptural and Architectural Style

Giovanni di Ugo da Campione belongs to what [Pietro Toesca](/scholars.html#toesca-pietro) identified as a late survival of Romanesque decorative culture within a broadly Gothic architectural framework — a stylistic condition that accounts for much of the formal complexity and apparent inconsistency of his surviving oeuvre. His two principal architectural commissions in Bergamo, the Baptistery and the north and south portals of Santa Maria Maggiore, are characterized by the use of polychrome marble surfaces — white, black, and rose — arranged in alternating horizontal bands and carefully calibrated color contrasts that recall the traditions of Lombard Romanesque church decoration far more than the monochromatic stone facades of contemporary French or even central Italian Gothic. 

This chromatic sophistication is one of the most distinctive features of the Campionese aesthetic, reflecting a deep familiarity with the properties of regional marbles and an acute sensitivity to the visual impact of material contrast. In Giovanni's hands, polychromy becomes a structural as well as decorative principle, articulating architectural divisions and hierarchies that might elsewhere be expressed through molding profiles or figural programs.

The Baptistery of Bergamo, begun around 1340 and attributed to Giovanni on the basis of an inscribed date and name found at its base, represents his most ambitious exercise in centralized architectural design. Originally housed within the nave of Santa Maria Maggiore and subsequently dismantled in 1660, it was reassembled — with significant modifications by the architect Raffaele Dalpino, working from a 1676 drawing by Simone Durello — first in the cloister of the cathedral canons in 1856 and eventually installed in its current location on Piazza Vecchia in 1898. 

The building was conceived as an octagonal structure with a pyramidal roof culminating in a lantern, its walls articulated by small columns and niches housing full-round sculptures of the Theological and Cardinal Virtues positioned at the corners, as well as personifications of the Sacraments and an angel crowning the lantern itself. The interior originally contained eight bas-relief panels depicting scenes from the Infancy and Passion of Christ, together with a standing statue of Saint John the Baptist — a sophisticated iconographic program that paired the sacrament of baptism with the full narrative arc of salvation history.

The north *protiro* of Santa Maria Maggiore, dated 1351 and bearing Giovanni's signature on the left bracket and on the base of the equestrian statue, is a work of considerable formal ambition. Structurally, it consists of two columns borne by red Veronese marble *leoni stilofori* at the front, with polygonal pilasters carrying foliate corbels mortared into the wall at the rear, creating a covered porch or *protiro* of the type long established in Lombard Romanesque portal design. The great strombate portal within this frame is decorated in its jambs with alternating twisted and thorned colonettes and carved pilasters populated with figures of warriors, ecclesiastics, and hunters — a program of secular and sacred imagery that reflects the complex social world of the confraternal patron. 

The upper register introduces a tripartite loggia with trilobate arches and a cross-vaulted ceiling, within which the equestrian Sant'Alessandro dominates the central axis, flanked by subsidiary saints in what appears to have been a carefully orchestrated program of civic hagiography. Above this loggia rises a further aedicule terminating in a pinnacle, containing the group of the enthroned Virgin with Saints Grata and Esteria — a composition that echoes the hierarchical Marian culminations of French Gothic portal programs while retaining distinctly Lombard formal characteristics.

The south portal, completed in 1360, differs significantly from its northern counterpart in both material and formal type, employing white marble *leoni stilofori* rather than the red Veronese stone of the north portal and adopting a single-register structure surmounted by a crowning frieze of columns. The frieze itself is one of the most iconographically unusual elements of Giovanni's production, populated in its intercolumnar spaces by figures of Christ, the Apostles, saints, and personifications of the trades — a vernacular iconographic program that draws on the tradition of occupational imagery found in calendar cycles and genre sculpture but transposes it into a monumental architectural context. 

The minor portal on the north side of the choir, executed between 1366 and 1367 in sandstone rather than marble, belongs to a formally distinct typological family: it is a Gothic *flamboyant* work, its cuspidal superstructure carried on *leoni stilofori* bearing statues of the Virgin and Saint John, decorated with flaming vegetation and terminating in a figure of the Crucified Christ. A relief of the Nativity occupies the lunette, demonstrating Giovanni's command of narrative relief composition alongside his more celebrated architectural and structural work.

The coexistence within Giovanni's oeuvre of Romanesque chromatic and compositional conventions on the one hand, and elements drawn from contemporary Gothic and specifically northern Italian Gothic traditions on the other, was first systematically analyzed by [Costantino Baroni](/scholars.html#baroni-costantino-nicola) in his foundational 1944 study *Scultura gotica lombarda*. Baroni identified two distinct stylistic currents running through Giovanni's figural work: a northern current, connected to the Veronese sculptural tradition, which is responsible for the heavier, more archaic figural types of the Baptistery Virtues and the portal warriors; and a Tuscan current, mediated through the recent Lombard activity of *Giovanni di Balduccio da Pisa*, which is responsible for the more refined, sinuous figural style visible in the portal saints and in the equestrian Sant'Alessandro. This Tuscan influence was by no means an abstract cultural phenomenon: Giovanni di Balduccio had worked in Milan on the famous Arca di San Pietro Martire in Sant'Eustorgio from 1336 to 1339, introducing Pisan Gothic refinement into Lombard sculptural culture at precisely the moment when Giovanni di Ugo was completing his training in his father's workshop.

The equestrian statue of Sant'Alessandro, the only work in Giovanni's surviving oeuvre that bears a direct autograph signature, is of particular stylistic and historiographical importance. The saint is depicted as a Roman soldier on horseback, a type derived from the ancient tradition of the *miles Christianus* but inflected through the contemporary influence of the Veronese *arche scaligere* — the grand equestrian funerary monuments of the della Scala lords, which had been erected in Verona from 1277 onward and which offered the most spectacular models of monumental equestrian sculpture available to any northern Italian artist of the period. Early scholarship, most notably [Alfred G. Meyer](/scholars.html#meyer-alfred-gotthold) in his 1893 study of Lombard Trecento monuments, proposed that Giovanni might himself have participated in the design of the Scaligeri tombs, but this attribution was definitively abandoned by Baroni and subsequent critics who recognized that the style of the Sant'Alessandro is derivative rather than originary with respect to the Veronese monuments.

The figural work of the portals, when examined in the light of the most recent restoration campaigns — particularly the 1998 restoration of the north *protiro* — reveals a workshop of considerable technical versatility, capable of operating simultaneously in the idioms of high-relief narrative, full-round freestanding statuary, decorative architectural carving, and architectural design. The studies of [Recanati](/scholars.html#recanati-maria-grazia), published in the 1998 volume *Il protiro di S. Maria Maggiore in Bergamo*, confirm on stylistic grounds that both the sculptural program of the loggia and the figurative apparatus of the upper aedicule are attributable to Giovanni and his workshop, while allowing for significant collaborative participation by assistants in the execution of subsidiary elements. This layered authorship — in which the master provided design intelligence and executed the most prominent figures while delegating subordinate passages to trained assistants — is entirely consistent with the workshop practice of the major Lombard building yards of the period.

## Artistic Influences

The most immediate and foundational influence on Giovanni di Ugo da Campione's artistic formation was the workshop tradition of his father Ugo, within which he served his apprenticeship from an early age. The Campionese *magistri* whom Ugo represented had by the early fourteenth century developed a highly sophisticated and distinctive formal language, rooted in the Romanesque traditions of Lombardy but continuously evolving through contact with the Gothic innovations arriving from France via the great cathedral building projects of Milan and Monza. 

This foundational training gave Giovanni a deep mastery of technical stonecutting, an acute understanding of the structural logic of portals and projecting canopies, and a firm command of the polychrome marble aesthetic that distinguished Campionese work from virtually all other regional schools of Lombard sculpture. The workshop was not a conservative or backward-looking environment: Ugo's documented participation in the *Loggia degli Osii* in Milan around 1314 brought the family into direct contact with the most progressive civic architectural thinking in Lombardy, and this exposure to urban Milanese culture almost certainly shaped Giovanni's subsequent approach to the design of monumental civic and ecclesiastical facades.

The impact of Giovanni di Balduccio da Pisa on Giovanni's figural style cannot be overstated. Giovanni di Balduccio had been summoned to Milan by the Visconti around 1334 and had executed the *Arca di San Pietro Martire* in Sant'Eustorgio between 1336 and 1339, a work that introduced into Lombardy the refined sinuous figure style of Pisano Gothic sculpture — the tradition descending from Nicola and Giovanni Pisano through Tino di Camaino and other Pisan and Sienese masters. For a sculptor of Giovanni di Ugo's generation, working in Bergamo at the very moment when these Pisan forms were being absorbed into the broader Lombard visual culture, the encounter with Giovanni di Balduccio's work would have been nothing short of revelatory. The elongated, elegantly draped figures, the refined facial types with their characteristic swelling brows and delicately modeled lips, and the compositional sophistication of the narrative reliefs all find clear echoes in the portal saints and the upper figural programs of Giovanni di Ugo's major works.

The Veronese sculptural tradition represented by the *arche scaligere* — the funerary monuments of Can Grande I (†1329), Mastino II (†1351), and Cansignorio (†1375) — constituted a further decisive influence on Giovanni's treatment of equestrian imagery. The *arche scaligere*, executed by Veronese masters working in a style that combined late Romanesque volumetric solidity with Gothic decorative refinement and heraldic display, offered the most imposing examples of monumental equestrian statuary available to any northern Italian sculptor of the mid-Trecento. Giovanni's Sant'Alessandro is clearly indebted to this tradition in its basic compositional formula — the armed knight on a caparisoned horse, set within an architectural aedicule — though the Bergamese figure lacks the sheer scale and dynastic swagger of the Veronese prototypes, reflecting the more modest civic and confraternal context of its commission. The relationship between Giovanni's work and the *arche scaligere* was so striking to early scholarship that Meyer proposed direct workshop participation, though this claim has since been conclusively rejected.[

The influence of French Gothic architectural thinking is visible above all in Giovanni's handling of the *protiro* superstructure at Santa Maria Maggiore, particularly in the tripartite loggia of the north portal with its trilobate arches and cross-vaulted ceiling. The organization of a portal composition around a sequence of vertically stacked registers — portal jambs, an intermediate loggia with statuary, and a culminating aedicule with Marian imagery — reflects the hierarchical portal programs of French Gothic cathedral architecture, filtered through the mediation of Italian adaptations such as those at Parma, Modena, and the Lombard mendicant churches. Giovanni never adopts the full vocabulary of French Rayonnant or Flamboyant Gothic in his architectural work, but the influence of northern Gothic conventions is clearly detectable in his decorative detailing, particularly in the foliate ornament, the pinnacles, and the cuspidal forms of the minor portal.

The broader tradition of the *Maestri Campionesi* itself — including the twelfth- and thirteenth-century masters who had worked at the cathedrals of Modena, Cremona, Ferrara, and Verona — formed an important historical horizon of reference for Giovanni's design thinking. The tradition of the *leoni stilofori* as portal bearers, the use of polychrome stone banding, the deployment of narrative relief within clearly demarcated architectural frames, and the systematic coordination of architectural and sculptural programs in a unified decorative ensemble are all features that Giovanni inherited from this century-long Campionese tradition. His achievement lay not in revolutionary formal innovation but in the intelligent synthesis and elevation of inherited conventions — bringing the Campionese portal tradition to its highest level of formal complexity and decorative richness while simultaneously absorbing the most progressive influences arriving from Pisa and Verona.

## Travels and Geographic Range

Giovanni di Ugo da Campione's career was geographically concentrated within a relatively compact zone of northern Lombardy, stretching from the lake district of Como and Lugano in the west and north to the Po plain city of Bergamo in the east — a region that had been the traditional heartland of Campionese building activity for more than a century. His earliest documented commission outside Bergamo proper was the restoration work at the parish church of Santi Giorgio, Nazario e Celso at Bellano on Lake Como, where he was paid in 1348 for construction work undertaken following the flood damage of 1341. The journey from Bergamo to Bellano, though modest by modern standards, would have required a substantial logistical effort in the fourteenth century, involving the transport of tools, materials, and assistants across the Alpine foothills and along the western shore of Lake Como. The commission at Bellano thus represents not merely a geographic displacement but a demonstration of the workshop's capacity to project its expertise across a meaningful geographic distance, servicing rural as well as urban ecclesiastical patrons throughout the broader region.

The procurement of marble for the Bergamo portals also necessitated regular travel to the quarries from which Giovanni's preferred stones were obtained. A document of 1366 records the transport of marble to Bergamo from a quarry at Brivio, a town on the Adda River south of Lecco, confirming that Giovanni's workshop was actively engaged in the logistics of stone extraction and transport as well as in the execution of finished sculptural work. The journey from Brivio to Bergamo along the Adda and over the foothills of the Bergamasque pre-Alps would have been a regular feature of the workshop's operational calendar, requiring Giovanni or his representatives to oversee the quarrying, rough cutting, and loading of marble onto river barges and then onto carts for the final overland stage to the building site. This logistical expertise — the ability to coordinate quarrying, transport, rough preparation, and finishing across an extended geographic network — was one of the defining competencies of the Campionese workshop tradition and a major factor in its commercial success.

The connection between Giovanni's workshop and Verona — implicit in the use of red Veronese marble for the *leoni stilofori* of the north portal of Santa Maria Maggiore — suggests a further geographic axis of engagement, though no documentary evidence survives to confirm that Giovanni himself ever visited Verona. The choice of Veronese marble was more than a practical decision: it was also a symbolic statement connecting Giovanni's work to the prestigious tradition of Veronese ecclesiastical and funerary sculpture, which was recognized throughout northern Italy as one of the leading schools of the period. Whether this material was obtained through established commercial channels or through direct workshop-to-workshop contact with Veronese stone merchants remains unclear, but the consistent use of Veronese stone in Giovanni's major commissions suggests a well-established supply relationship that would have required periodic personal attention and negotiation. The geographic horizon of Giovanni's professional world was thus considerably broader than his surviving building sites might suggest.

The network of Campionese contacts across northern Italy — from Milan to Modena, from Parma to Como — would have made Giovanni a participant, even if only episodically and through correspondence and workshop emissaries rather than personal travel, in the broader cultural geography of Trecento northern Italian art. The presence of Giovanni di Balduccio's Pisan-influenced work in Milan, the continuing activity of Campionese masters at the great Lombard cathedrals, and the emergence of the new Milanese Cathedral project from 1386 onward (a project that would eventually draw Campionese builders in considerable numbers) all formed part of the professional and cultural environment within which Giovanni operated. His death, conventionally dated around 1375 and most plausibly at Campione — the village that had given his family its name and identity — brought to an end a career that, despite its apparent geographic modesty, had placed him at the very center of the most important ecclesiastical building projects in Trecento Lombardy.

## Principal Works

### The Baptistery of Bergamo

<figure>
  <img src="/images/Giovanni di Ugo da Campione/image 1.jpg" alt="The Baptistery of Bergamo">
  <figcaption>The Baptistery of Bergamo, Piazza Vecchia, Città Alta, Bergamo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The original building was shorter and less slender than the current one; the windows were wider, and the structure as a whole had a more open, “perforated” appearance. The baptistery was dismantled in 1660, then reassembled in different forms, and finally relocated to its current position at the end of the 19th century. This history of the structure is important because it explains why today we see a partially reconstructed complex, yet one based on authentic 14th-century sculptures.

On the exterior, at the corners of the octagon, there are eight female figures in niches: the three theological virtues—Faith, Hope, and Charity—and the four cardinal virtues—Fortitude, Temperance, Justice, and Prudence—to which Patience is added. Beneath each virtue is depicted the corresponding vice, according to a moral scheme that contrasts virtue and sin. These statues are among the most characteristic elements of the baptistery, as they transform the architecture into a visual lesson on Christian life.

Inside, the symbolic centerpiece is the statue of St. John the Baptist, which originally stood at the center of the baptismal font under a canopy and is now placed in a dominant position on the wall opposite the entrance. Above it is also the dove, a sign of the Holy Spirit, while the two-tone marble floor evokes the waters of the Jordan. The ensemble links the rite of baptism to the Trinity and Christian salvation.

The reliefs that once adorned the baptismal font are now affixed to the interior walls and depict eight scenes from the life of Christ. Sources describe this cycle as comprising: the Annunciation, the Nativity, the Presentation in the Temple, the Baptism, the Arrest and Condemnation, the Crucifixion, the Deposition, the Resurrection, and the Ascension. In some summaries, the cycle is described as “Stories of the Infancy and Passion of Christ,” but the figurative content is as listed above.

The figurative program is highly coherent: on the exterior, virtues and vices define the moral conduct of the faithful; on the interior, scenes of Christ illustrate the history of salvation; at the center, Saint John the Baptist personifies the rite of baptism itself. The octagon is not merely a geometric choice, but a reference to the symbol of the “eighth day,” that is, the Resurrection. For this reason, the Baptistery should not be viewed solely as a sculptural work, but as a true catechism in stone.

In summary, the figures depicted are as follows: Faith, Hope, Charity, Fortitude, Temperance, Justice, Prudence, and Patience; their respective vices; St. John the Baptist; the dove of the Holy Spirit; and, in the interior reliefs, scenes of the Annunciation, Nativity, Presentation in the Temple, Baptism of Christ, Arrest and Condemnation, Crucifixion, Deposition, Resurrection, and Ascension. It is thus a unified iconographic complex, among the most important examples of Campionese sculpture in Bergamo.

### The North Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore

<figure>
  <img src="/images/Giovanni di Ugo da Campione/image 2.jpg" alt="The North Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore">
  <figcaption>The North Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore, Città Alta, Bergamo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The north portal (the “Red Lions” porch) of Santa Maria Maggiore in Bergamo is a three-tiered porch made of polychrome marble, created by Giovanni da Campione around 1351–53 and considered one of the greatest masterpieces of Campione-style sculpture in Bergamo. It is located on the northern side of the transept, facing Piazza Duomo, and essentially served as the “façade” of the basilica, given the absence of a central entrance. 

The portico consists of three superimposed levels: a wide-arched portico supported by columns resting on lion-shaped capitals, a central loggia, and an upper loggia. It is constructed of polychrome marbles: Musso white, Bardiglio, Verona red, Candoglia white, and Val Seriana black, which create a spectacular and richly decorated effect. The staircase in front, with ten alternating black and white steps, has a strong symbolic meaning: the first seven represent the days of the week, the next three the Trinity, so that the faithful ascended “from earthly life to heavenly life.”

- Figures on the lower register and the portal

At the level of the portal, Giovanni da Campione presents a highly complex iconographic program, combining soldiers, apostles, Trinitarian symbols, and moral figures.

*Four armed soldiers*: they appear as “guardians” against evil, within the jambs or in the spaces between the columns, evoking the struggle against the evil one lurking within man’s animal instincts.

*Twelve Apostles*: The twelve apostles are depicted in the panels of the lintel and in the side lunettes in a standing position, holding open or closed books, symbolizing preaching and the Word of Christ.

*Eagles, animals, and vegetation*: eagles, fruits, and flowers (Trinity lilies, bluebells) appear on the shutters and the edges of the portal, referring both to Temperance and to the number eight (symbol of the afterlife and baptism) and to the tree of life in Christ.

*Hunting scenes on the arch*: on the upper arch’s ring, a long hunt unfolds featuring dogs, lions, boars, rabbits, and hares, symbolizing the soul’s pursuit of salvation, the death of sin, and Christian rebirth. In particular, the struggle between the dog and the bear, the lion and the lioness, and the hunt for the deer—interpreted as a catechumen—are all allegories of the animal instinct that must die to make way for spiritual life.

- Christ Pantocrator and the Annunciation

In the center of the arch lies the theological core of the narthex:

*Christ Pantocrator*: within a “vescica piscis” mandorla, Christ is depicted beardless, with three fingers raised on his right hand to indicate the Trinity and the Book of Revelation in his left; the mandorla represents the meeting point between the earthly world and the divine order.

*Four-lobed figures*: next to Christ, in the side niches, two four-lobed figures with birds: on the right, a falcon looking at Christ; on the left, a bird of prey holding a small bird in its beak; they symbolize the poles of life/death and good/evil, while Christ acts as the judge holding up the Book of Revelation.

*Annunciation at the apex of the vault*: at the top, as if supporting the portico, is the scene of the Annunciation: the angel on the right holding a lily and the Virgin Mary on the left, representing the Incarnation of the Word and the transition from the spiritual to the material.

- Statues of the middle and upper loggias

In the middle loggia (second order) stand the figures of three patron saints of Bergamo, in white Musso marble: 

In the upper loggia (third order) appear:

*Saint Alexander on horseback*: central equestrian figure, patron saint of Bergamo; it is the statue most often attributed directly to Giovanni da Campione.

*Saint Barnabas*: to the left of Saint Alexander, one of the city’s patron saints.

*Saint Vincent*: to the right, co-patron of Bergamo and titular saint of the ancient church dedicated to him.

*Madonna and Child Holding the World*: central figure, with the Child holding a sphere or globe as a symbol of Christ’s universal dominion.

*Saint Esteria*: to the left of the Madonna, one of the local co-patronesses.

*Saint Grata*: on the right, co-patroness of Bergamo, depicted holding the head of Saint Alexander, evoking the cult of the city’s martyrs.

### The South Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore

<figure>
  <img src="/images/Giovanni di Ugo da Campione/image 3.jpg" alt="The South Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore">
  <figcaption>The South Portal (Protiro) of Santa Maria Maggiore, Città Alta, Bergamo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The south portal (the “White Lions” porch) of Santa Maria Maggiore in Bergamo is a single-order porch made of polychrome marble, created by Giovanni da Campione and his workshop around 1360, and overlooks Piazza delle Rosate in the Upper Town. It is called “dei leoni bianchi” precisely because of the pair of column-bearing lions in white Candoglia marble that support the arched portico, in contrast to the northern portico of the “Leoni rossi” in pink Verona marble. The style is Gothic-Lombard, with strong references to examples from Ferrara and Verona, but slightly more sober than the northern portico.Structure and Symbolism of the Portico

The portico is set on a single architectural level: a wide-arched portico supported at the front by two polygonal anhydrite columns resting on lion-columns, and at the rear by large corbels in red Verona marble. The arch’s ring is composed of alternating bands of white and black marble, while the upper attic is crowned by a rich frieze with hanging arches and carved niches. The structure is slightly simpler than the north portico, but remains rich in symbolism: the lower part is dedicated to the sinful condition of man, the upper part to the saints and the figure of Christ, creating a catechetical journey from sin to salvation.

- At the level of the portal and columns, a rich iconography of sins and humanity unfolds:

*Two telamons*: a man and a woman, at the base of the columns, bearing the architectural and symbolic weight of humanity’s sins; both carry a clearly visible pouch of money at their waist, signifying greed and avarice.

*Dragon and human heads*: the weight they bear is depicted as a dragon with the heads of Adam and Eve, a symbol of original sin, and of Lucifer, defeated only by the Agnus Dei (Christ) through his death and resurrection.

*Hunting scenes and animals*: the relief decoration features dogs, lions, boars, and dragons with human heads, bears eating the fruit of the tree, and snakes tempting man; the boar symbolizes the sins of the flesh, the hare fertility, the tree the forbidden fruit, and the entire scene represents the inner struggle against lust, gluttony, and carnal sins.

*Masks of the Earth and the Tree of Life*: flowering branches emerge from the masks of the earth, signifying the lignum vitae, the tree of life in Christ, which is the spiritual lifeblood of humanity; perched on these same branches are birds eating the good fruit, a symbol of souls drawing near to salvation through the Church.

*Three-branched sapling*: in one corner, a small sapling divided into three branches recalls the oaks of Mamre, where God appeared to Abraham in the form of three men, that is, the Trinity.

- In the upper band of the attic, featuring a relief frieze, a succession of statues of saints in niches represents the Christian response to sin:

Five saints in the upper outer section:

*Saint Rusticus*, with the palm of martyrdom and a sword in his right hand, whose name is inscribed on the base.

*Saint Defendent* (or Delfino), with a mace, symbol of his martyrdom, accompanied by a frieze of acanthus leaves and red flowers alluding to the martyr’s suffering.

*Saint Lawrence*, with the books of salvation, the griddle of martyrdom, and the deacon’s stole, indicating the figure of the deacon-martyr.

*Saint Anthony the Abbot*, with the “T”-shaped staff (tau), the bell, and the pig at his feet, according to his traditional iconography.

*A saintly bishop difficult to identify*, depicted with a miter and crosier, lacking other clear emblems.

The Four Crowned Saints: on the right side of the upper attic, four figures represent the Four Crowned Saints, protectors of stonemasons, with their tools of the trade (chisels, hammers, etc.). 

They are:
Severus, Severianus, Carpophorus, Vittorinus, martyrs by order of Diocletian.

Behind them, in a niche, a pine cone on a master’s chair, a symbol of eternity, because in tradition it accompanied the worthy to Paradise.

In 1403, the upper spire of the porch was completed by the master Anex de Alemania (Hans von Fernach), in a German-style Gothic design. The spire rests on a solid base with three statues in niches, which conclude the theological program of the portal:

*God the Father* giving his blessing in the center, with a gesture of blessing.

*Archangel Gabriel* on one side, holding a lily or the Annunciation scroll, symbol of the angelic visitation.

*The Virgin of the Annunciation* on the other, receiving the announcement of the Incarnation.

At the top, the spire culminates with the presence of five angels, who visually crown the portal as the entrance to the house of God.

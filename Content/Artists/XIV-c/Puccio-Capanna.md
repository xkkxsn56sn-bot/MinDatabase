## Introduction

Few figures in the history of Italian Trecento painting illustrate as sharply as *Puccio Capanna* the gap between documentary fact and art-historical construction. For roughly four centuries after Giorgio Vasari, "Puccio Capanna" existed principally as a name in the *Lives* — a Florentine pupil of Giotto credited with a scatter of works from Rimini to Bologna, most of them now lost or reassigned. Meanwhile, the paintings that we now regard as his finest achievements circulated under other names: "Giottino," Maso di Banco, or "Stefano Fiorentino." Only in the second half of the twentieth century did documentary discoveries and stylistic analysis converge to restore to a single historical personality — a painter of Assisi, not Florence — a coherent and remarkably high body of work.

This biography treats Puccio Capanna according to the strict evidentiary hierarchy the subject demands: what is *documented*, what is *attributed* (and by whom, and when), and what is *speculation*. It is a story in which the sources actively mislead, in which the most authoritative early witness (Vasari) is also the principal source of error, and in which the recovery of the artist is itself a case study in twentieth-century connoisseurship and archival research. It is also a cautionary tale about the durability of a printed source: Vasari's *Lives*, endlessly reprinted, held the field against the "stentata prosa" of a provincial friar's manuscript, and against the archive itself, well into the twentieth century.

The written sources for Puccio Capanna fall into three groups: the medieval and early-modern Florentine artistic literature ([Ghiberti](/scholars.html#ghiberti-lorenzo), the *Libro di Antonio Billi*, the Anonimo Gaddiano, [Gelli](/scholars.html#gelli-giovan-battista), and [Vasari](/scholars.html#vasari-giorgio)); the local Assisan tradition, above all the manuscript *Descrizione* of Fra Ludovico da Pietralunga<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ludovico-da-pietralunga" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> (c. 1570) and the lost *Libro* of the painter Dono Doni; and the archival record proper, which is astonishingly thin.

It is essential to state at the outset a correction that recurs in popular accounts. Lorenzo Ghiberti's *Commentari* do not mention Puccio Capanna at all. In the Second Book, when Ghiberti lists Giotto's pupils, he writes "E discepoli furono questi. Stefano fu egregissimo dottore," and describes a "gloria" begun by Stefano "nella chiesa d'Ascesi." It is Stefano Fiorentino, not Puccio, who is Ghiberti's Assisan Giottesque. Any biography claiming Ghiberti as a witness to Puccio's fame is repeating an error. This matters, because the reconstruction of Puccio's corpus in the twentieth century ran precisely *through* the figure of Stefano: the works were first assembled under Stefano's name and only later transferred to Puccio.

The single most valuable source is therefore not Florentine at all but Assisan: the *Descrizione della Basilica di San Francesco e di altri santuari di Assisi* by the Conventual friar Ludovico da Pietralunga (documented at Assisi from 1568, the manuscript elaborated around 1570 and after). Ludovico — nicknamed "il Filosofo" by his confrères, and an antesignano tour-guide who led illustrious visitors through the town's churches — recorded the local tradition that Puccio was an Assisan and gave a list of his works that has proved "sostanzialmente attendibile." Crucially, Ludovico drew on the opinions of his friend the painter Dono Doni, whose own lost *Libro* on the Basilica is partly recoverable. Ludovico's *Descrizione* was published only in 1926 (by Giuseppe G. Cristofani, in the *Bollettino della Deputazione di storia patria per l'Umbria*), and its late appearance in an obscure periodical is one reason the recovery of Puccio was so long delayed. A modern critical edition was produced by [Pietro Scarpellini](/scholars.html#scarpellini-pietro) (Treviso, 1982).

Ghiberti's silence is the first fact of the case. The pre-Vasarian Florentine sources — Filippo Villani's *De origine civitatis Florentiae* (c. 1400–1405), the *Libro di Antonio Billi* (c. 1481–1530), the Anonimo Gaddiano, Cristoforo Landino, Francesco Albertini, and Giovan Battista Gelli's *Venti vite* — are concerned with celebrating the illustrious painters of Florence. They name Stefano, Taddeo Gaddi, Maso di Banco, Buffalmacco, and others, and they say nothing of a "Puccio Capanna." The name simply is not part of the Florentine canon of Giotto's heirs before Vasari.

This absence is significant. It confirms that Puccio was not a Florentine figure of major local fame, and it makes the appearance of his name in Vasari all the more interesting: Vasari must have picked it up from a source outside the standard Florentine literature — in all probability from a signature or local report at Rimini, and from the Florentine painters' confraternity roll (the *Libro della Compagnia di San Luca*), which Vasari's collaborator Vincenzio Borghini could examine as first camerlengo of the newly founded Accademia del Disegno (1563).

Vasari treats Puccio in two places and across two editions, and the differences are revealing.

In the first (Torrentiniana) edition of 1550, within the Life of Giotto, Vasari mentions "Puccio Capanna" only briefly: as a collaborator with Giotto on the painted Crucifix of Santa Maria Novella ("sul quale Puccio Capanna suo creato in compagnia di lui lavorò"), and as the author of a lost shipwreck ex-voto in the church of San Cataldo at Rimini — "un voto d'una nave che par che affoghi nel mare, con gente che gettano le robe nel mare. Et evvi Puccio di naturale, fra un buon numero di marinari." Notably, the 1550 text gives no indication of Puccio's birthplace.

In the second (Giuntina) edition of 1568, the notice expands dramatically. Vasari now calls him "Puccio Capanna fiorentino" — expressly Florentine — and assigns him a large and heterogeneous corpus: the Rimini shipwreck; the Strozzi Chapel in Santa Trinita in Florence (a Coronation of the Virgin with a choir of angels "che tirano assai alla maniera di Giotto," and Stories of St Lucy at the sides); the Covoni Chapel of St John the Evangelist in the Badia Fiorentina; in Pistoia, the principal chapel and the Chapel of St Louis in San Francesco; in San Domenico at Pistoia a Crucifix with a Madonna and St John "wrought with much sweetness," and at their feet a complete human skeleton "wherein Puccio showed that he had sought to find the foundations of art"; at Assisi, scenes of the Passion in the Lower Church of San Francesco, a Christ in Glory in a chapel of Santa Maria degli Angeli "tutta affumicata dalle lampane," the Chapel of St Martin "painted in fresco for Cardinal Gentile," and a Christ at the Column with a Madonna between Sts Catherine and Clare in the street called Portica; and a panel with the Passion and Stories of St Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> in the tramezzo of a church in Bologna.

Vasari also reports the Assisan claim to Puccio as a native son, while insisting on the Florentine origin: "in Ascesi, where most of his works are, and where it seems to me that he helped Giotto to paint, I have found that they hold him for their fellow-citizen, and that still today there are in that city some of the family of the Capanni. Whence it may easily be believed that he was born in Florence, having so written himself, and that he was a disciple of Giotto, but that afterwards he took a wife in Ascesi, that there he had children, and that now he has descendants there." He adds the tradition that Puccio, "as some have it, did not live long, having fallen sick and died by reason of labouring too much in fresco."

The reported signature deserves precise statement. In the 1568 text, describing the lost San Domenico (Pistoia) Crucifixion, Vasari writes: **"In quest'opera si legge il suo nome, fatto da lui stesso, in questo modo: *Puccio di Fiorenza me fece*"** ("In this work one reads his name, made by himself, in this manner: Puccio of Florence made me"). Two points are critical. First, this inscription reads "Puccio di Fiorenza," not "Capanna." Second, **it was reported for Pistoia, not for Assisi**: there is no reliable early report of an inscription "Puccio di Fiorenza mi fece" in San Francesco at Assisi. The work Vasari described is lost and the inscription cannot be verified; it survives only through his report.

Vasari's reliability must be assessed as low for this Life. As [Veruska Picchiarelli](/scholars.html#picchiarelli-veruska) has shown in her study "Vasari e Assisi. Dalle fonti fiorentine al confronto con la tradizione storiografica locale nei casi esemplari di Cimabue, Giotto e Puccio Capanna" (in *Giorgio Vasari tra capitale medicea e città del dominio*, Firenze, Edifir, 2012), Vasari had no direct knowledge of Umbria when he compiled the 1550 edition; his Assisan information in 1568 derives from two brief visits (May 1563 and April 1566, reconstructed from his correspondence by [Karl Frey](/scholars.html#frey-karl)) and from local informants — very probably the painter Dono Doni and the friar Ludovico da Pietralunga. Vasari filtered this local material through the ideological programme of the *Lives*: the celebration of Tuscan, and specifically Florentine, primacy in the arts, that "salda impalcatura ideologica" whose end was "la celebrazione del primato toscano." Confronted with a body of superb painting at Assisi that local tradition attributed to a painter the town claimed as its own, Vasari — persuaded by the "Puccio di Fiorenza" signature at Pistoia and by the Florentine confraternity record — insisted on a Florentine Puccio, and transferred the finest Assisan works (the Coronation, the Stanislaus scenes, the Santa Chiara panel) to "Tommaso di Stefano detto Giottino," leaving in Puccio's file only the exterior, damaged frescoes of the San Gregorio hospital. The archivist Adamo Rossi memorably complained that this Vasarian verdict had been gathered "dal servo gregge degli eruditi... come responsi di oracolo."

## The documentary record — or its thinness

The archival record for Puccio Capanna consists, as the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* states plainly, of two documents only ("sul pittore sono noti finora due soli documenti"), to which a third, Florentine, trace may be added.

### The commission of 24 November 1341

Discovered and published by the Conventual Franciscan P. Giuseppe Abate in "Per la storia e l'arte della Basilica di San Francesco in Assisi" (*Miscellanea Francescana* 56, 1956, pp. 3–36, at 25–30), this is a contract of the Comune di Assisi. On 16 November 1341 the *capitano del popolo*, Bandino di Nerio (of the *comitato* of Marsciano), convened the council to commission images of the Virgin and saints on two city gates; after debate in the *Consiglio generale* (one councillor proposing to award to the lowest bidder, "cuicumque pro minori faciet," others deferring to the Priori), the matter was referred to the Priori, and on 24 November the contract was concluded with "Puccius Capanne et Cecce Saraceni pictores de Assisio" — Puccio Capanna and Cecce (Cecco) di Saraceno, painters of Assisi. They undertook to fresco, on the inner and outer faces of the "Porta externa platee nove" (the Porta "Bonaematris," gate of the Piazza Nuova) and the "Porta Sancti Ruphini," the enthroned Madonna and Child among the city's patrons — Sts Francis, Clare, Rufinus, Victorinus, and John — using "bono acçurro et cinabrio et bonis coloribus" (good azure, cinnabar, and good colours), with tin-gilt friezes in the archivolts and gilt crowns on Virgin and Child, at the painters' own expense, to be finished by the coming feast of Christmas. The most widely reproduced English-language transcription renders the naming clause "Puccius Cappanej et Cecce Saraceni, pictores de Assisio." (The surname's orthography varies among transcribers — Capanne / Cappanne / Cappanej — reflecting manuscript reading, and should be checked against Abate directly.)

### The sale of 1347

Published by Cesare Cenci in *Documentazione di vita assisana 1300–1530* (Spicilegium Bonaventurianum 109, vol. I, Grottaferrata 1974, pp. 85, 100): in 1347 Puccio sold, for five florins, an ox of red coat ("un bue di pelo rosso") to the friars of the Sacro Convento of San Francesco. This is a purely economic document, telling us only that Puccio was alive, resident, and doing business at Assisi in 1347 — and, incidentally, that he was among the few painters still active around the Lower Church after the Ghibelline upheaval of 1319–1322.

### The Florentine matriculation

In the *Libro della Compagnia di San Luca* (Florence, Archivio di Stato, Compagnia dei Pittori poi Accademia del Disegno, ms. 1), at carta 32, under the year 1350, appears the name *"Puccio di ser Firençe Capanna."* Two lines further on the same folio, under 1357, appears "Puccio Simoni" — i.e. the Florentine painter Puccio di Simone. As Picchiarelli notes (following Frey), the dates in the codex are approximate — the statutes of 1339 were transcribed only later, with fair copies made in 1386, 1395, and 1404 — and the surname "Capanna" is written in a different, fifteenth-century hand, an interpolation. The entry nonetheless indicates that Puccio established activity in Florence around mid-century, and that his father was a "ser" Fiorenzo Capanna, presumably a notable or jurist. This datum, though known since Luigi Manzoni transcribed it in 1904 (misreading the surname as "Campana") and Dominic Colnaghi recorded it correctly in his *Dictionary of Florentine Painters* (1928), was largely ignored until the modern reconstruction of the artist. It plausibly identifies Puccio with the "maestro Puccio che sta in via Larga" listed among the best Florentine masters c. 1348–1349 by the operai of San Giovanni Fuorcivitas in Pistoia.

It must be added that no autograph signature by Puccio Capanna survives on any extant work. The only securely attributable surviving painting — the 1341 gate fragment — is attributed through the archival contract, not through a signature. Vasari's "Puccio di Fiorenza me fece" was reported for a now-lost Pistoia Crucifixion and, as discussed below, most probably belonged to a different painter.

The archival silence beyond these traces must be stated frankly. Cenci found the Assisi communal archive "scarso e frammentario" until the mid-fifteenth century, and the notarial archive is entirely lost before 1374. We therefore have no birth date, no death date, no securely documented commission for any of the surviving frescoes, and no notarial trace of his father. The biographical framework — Assisan by birth, active c. 1330–1350, perhaps ending his career in Florence or the North — is an inference from the three surviving traces plus stylistic analysis. Pietro Maria Della Porta's dedicated documentary essay, "Puccius Capannae pictor de Assisio. I documenti" (in the 1989 Assisi catalogue, pp. 41–44), remains the point of departure for the archival question.

## The historiography from the nineteenth century to the present

The critical history of Puccio Capanna is inseparable from the "Assisi Problem" — the long controversy over the authorship of the frescoes in the two churches of San Francesco — and from the tangled knot of names (Stefano, Maso, Giottino) that Vasari created.

### The antiquarian and nineteenth-century tradition

The Umbrian antiquarian literature preserved the local memory of Puccio as an Assisan: Ludovico Iacobilli in the seventeenth century; the Conventual historian Francesco Maria Angeli, whose *Collis Paradisi Amoenitas, seu Sacri Conventus Assisiensis historiae libri II* (Montefiascone, 1704) was the first book devoted to the Basilica and the first printed at Montefiascone; and, in the nineteenth century, Antonio Cristofani. Johann David Passavant, Franz Kugler (*Handbuch der Geschichte der Malerei*, 1837), and Jacob Burckhardt (*Il Cicerone*, 1855) transmitted the Vasarian picture. The decisive nineteenth-century intervention was that of [Giovanni Battista Cavalcaselle](/scholars.html#cavalcaselle-giovanni-battista), in *A New History of Painting in Italy* written with [Joseph Archer Crowe](/scholars.html#crowe-joseph-archer) (1864), who correctly removed the Passion frescoes of the left transept of the Lower Church from Puccio and restored them to Pietro Lorenzetti. Carlo Fea and Sebastiano Ranghiasci had already (1820) reassigned the St Martin Chapel to Simone Martini. [Henry Thode](/scholars.html#thode-heinrich), in *Franz von Assisi und die Anfänge der Kunst der Renaissance in Italien* (Berlin, 1885; 2nd ed. 1904), engaged extensively with the Assisan Giottesques and named the "Master of Santa Chiara," but his attempts (following Vasari's indications) to reconstruct Puccio from the Vasarian texts failed, as did those of [Osvald Sirén](/scholars.html#siren-osvald) and Alessandro Chiappelli.

### Early twentieth century

[Adolfo Venturi](/scholars.html#venturi-adolfo), in his *Storia dell'arte italiana* V (1907), cautiously proposed that the group of Assisan frescoes around the Coronation might be the work of Stefano Fiorentino — reviving Ghiberti's Assisan Giottesque as a candidate — and connected to it the chapter-house Crucifixion and the Santa Chiara polyptych. Osvaldo Sirén (1908, 1917) at first assigned the group to "Giottino," then recognised that these paintings formed an independent artistic personality distinct from Maso and Giottino. [Raimond van Marle](/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond) (1924) suggested a pupil of the Maestro delle Vele. [Pietro Toesca](/scholars.html#toesca-pietro) (*La pittura fiorentina del Trecento*, 1929; *Il Trecento*, 1951), Wilhelm Suida, and Ersilia Zocca (in the *Catalogo delle cose d'arte* for Assisi, 1936) variously proposed Maso di Banco. [Bernard Berenson](/scholars.html#berenson-bernard) listed the works among the anonymous or Giottesque (*Italian Pictures of the Renaissance*, 1932).

[Evelyn Sandberg Vavalà](/scholars.html#sandberg-vavala-evelyn) treated the problem under "Giotto's Workshop" (*Burlington Magazine*, 1938). [Luigi Coletti](/scholars.html#coletti-luigi), in a series of studies (1942–1950), preferred the conventional name "Maestro colorista di Assisi," calling him the greatest colourist, the "pittore più pittore," of the entire Italian Trecento. The key intervention was [Roberto Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto)'s "Stefano Fiorentino" (*Paragone*, 1951), which — building on Venturi — assembled the Assisan group and several panels under the name of Stefano, on the strength of Vasari's description of Stefano's "maniera dolcissima e tanto unita." Longhi's brilliant reconstruction won wide acceptance (Bettini, [Volpe](/scholars.html#volpe-carlo), Gabbrielli, Lazarev, [Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni), [Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano), [Gnudi](/scholars.html#gnudi-cesare), [Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando)).

Longhi's "Stefano" thesis collapsed under the weight of Abate's 1956 discovery. In 1963 [Luisa Marcucci](/scholars.html#marcucci-luisa) ("Dal 'Maestro di Figline' a Giottino," *Jahrbuch der Berliner Museen* V, at p. 26 n. 12) intuited that the 1341 commission corresponded to the surviving frescoed fragment in the Pinacoteca Comunale di Assisi (the Madonna and Child with St Francis from the Porta San Rufino), thereby anchoring the entire stylistic group to the documented Puccio, not to the undocumented Stefano. Pietro Scarpellini, in "Di alcuni pittori giotteschi nella città e nel territorio di Assisi" (in *Giotto e i giotteschi in Assisi*, ed. G. Palumbo, Roma, 1969), carried out the exemplary reconstruction of the catalogue, definitively transferring the corpus to Puccio Capanna.

[Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos) authored the fundamental *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry (vol. XVIII, 1975, pp. 384–387), which remains the standard critical account and traces in detail the migration of works among Giotto, the Master of the Fabriano Altar (= Puccio di Simone), Nardo di Cione, Giovanni di Bartolomeo Cristiani, Pietro Lorenzetti, and Simone Martini. [Bruno Zanardi](/scholars.html#zanardi-bruno) — who, as a young restorer trained at the Istituto Centrale del Restauro, personally restored the semi-illegible 1341 fragment between 1974 and 1977 — published "Da Stefano Fiorentino a Puccio Capanna" (*Storia dell'Arte*, 1978; cited as vol. 33, pp. 115–127, though also referenced as vol. 22 in some bibliographies), narrating and confirming the critical passage from Longhi's "Stefano" to the documented Puccio.

[Filippo Todini](/scholars.html#todini-filippo) (in *La pittura in Italia. Il Duecento e il Trecento*, 1986; *La pittura umbra*, 1989; and the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry, 1993) developed the catalogue and set Puccio within the Umbrian Giottesque tradition. [Elvio Lunghi](/scholars.html#lunghi-elvio) (*Il Museo della cattedrale di San Rufino ad Assisi*, 1987; the catalogue in *Puccio Capanna*, 1989; "Puccio Capanna nella confraternita di San Gregorio ad Assisi," *Arte Cristiana* 81, 1993, pp. 3–14) refined the corpus and its dating. Carlo Volpe placed Puccio at the head of "il lungo percorso del dipingere dolcissimo e tanto unito" (in *Storia dell'arte italiana*, 1983). The 1989 Assisi exhibition and catalogue (*Puccio Capanna*, eds. F. Cerri, P. M. Della Porta, E. Lunghi, P. Scarpellini) gathered the studies — including Scarpellini's "Puccio Capanna venti anni dopo," Lunghi's "itinerario di una vicenda critica," and Della Porta's documentary essay.

Most recently, the exhibition *Giotto e san Francesco. Una rivoluzione nell'Umbria del Trecento* (Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria, Perugia, *14 March–14 June 2026*); over sixty works in eight sections, curated by Veruska Picchiarelli and [Emanuele Zappasodi](/scholars.html#zappasodi-emanuele), confirming Puccio's canonical status. [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico), whose photographic archive (Fondazione Zeri, Bologna) catalogues the works, filed the group under "Puccio Capanna" while recording the older attributions to "Stefano," Giottino, and Maso.

## The corpus of works, work by work

What follows organises every work ever attributed to Puccio Capanna, with the status of each attribution named. The governing distinction is between the single documented work, the accepted stylistic corpus (frescoes and one panel diptych in and around Assisi), the debated additions, and the Vasarian attributions since removed.

### St Stanislaus Raises a Dead Man (attr.)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 1.jpg" alt="St Stanislaus Raises a Dead Man">
  <figcaption>St Stanislaus Raises a Dead Man, 1330-40, fresco, Lower Basilica of San Francesco, Assisi.</figcaption>
</figure>

The scene belongs to the small fresco complex painted in the blind arch of the third bay of the left aisle of the Lower Church, the recess occupied since around 1300 by the *cantoria* (singing gallery) built after the demolition of the choir screen and faced with reused Cosmatesque panels. The niche above the tribune carries a *Coronation of the Virgin* at the centre, flanked by two narrative scenes from the life of St Stanislaus of Szczepanów — the miracle reproduced here and, on the opposite side, the *Martyrdom*, with a *Crucifixion* below it. The dedication is not casual: an altar to St Stanislaus in this position is already recorded in the bull of indulgences issued by Alexander IV in 1256, three years after Innocent IV<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-innocent-iv" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> canonised the Cracovian bishop at Assisi itself in 1253. The site is thus the principal Italian monument of the saint's cult, and the cycle is one of the three earliest surviving narrative cycles devoted to him.

The composition is articulated in two superimposed registers, unified by a single oblique viewpoint. Above rises a church seen from the north-west: an aisled basilica in white ashlar with a steeply pitched tiled roof, a clerestory pierced by four tall single-light windows with cusped, traceried heads, and a projecting eastern block terminating in a gabled front with an oculus. A small brick lantern or bell-cote, pierced by arcaded openings, sits astride the ridge. The building is set against a dense blue-black ground that functions as sky.

Below, an open portico with a low-pitched tiled roof, carried on timber posts and beams, forms a shadowed recess in which the action takes place. This is the classic Giottesque *scatola prospettica*: architecture is not backdrop but container, a stage-box whose ceiling and lateral supports establish depth and whose darkness isolates the illuminated figures. The whole is enclosed by painted borders imitating Cosmatesque inlay — pinwheel and star motifs in white, oxblood, brown and green, with square terminal panels and a central lozenge — an illusionistic response to the real Cosmatesque revetment of the *cantoria* immediately below.

The episode is the best-known miracle in the *vita* of St Stanislaus: the resurrection of the knight Piotr Strzemieńczyk of Piotrawin<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-stanislaus-piotrawin" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, raised from the grave so that he might testify before King Bolesław II that the bishop had lawfully purchased the disputed land. The identification is standard for this scene type, and the setting before a church — Piotrawin being a pilgrimage site precisely on account of the miracle — supports it. It should be noted, however, that the Assisi fresco suppresses the juridical apparatus (throne, king, court) usual in Polish and Hungarian recensions, and presents the miracle as a purely thaumaturgic event; the reading rests on iconographic type rather than on any inscription, since no *titulus* survives on the field.

- The resurrected man (far left)

Nude, emaciated, the ribcage and clavicles legible beneath ashen ivory flesh, he rises on one knee from a low slab or step at the threshold of the portico. The head is thrown back, mouth slightly open, hair short and disordered; the gaze fixes the bishop. His right forearm is seized by the saint, his left hand raised in a gesture at once of supplication and of astonishment. The nudity is not classicising but semantic: it denotes the condition of the corpse, stripped and shrouded, and its livid tonality is deliberately set against the warm chromatics of the living.

- St Stanislaus (centre)

Frontally placed but turned in three-quarter view, he wears a white alb, a golden-ochre chasuble lined in pale blue-green and closed by a broad orphrey embroidered with gold, and a white mitre with jewelled bands; the nimbus is plain and incised. He stands on a low plinth, which raises him fractionally above both groups and marks him as the compositional fulcrum. His right hand grasps the wrist of the dead man — the gesture of physical *presa*, the transmission of grace by contact — while the left is raised open in the rhetorical gesture of command or speech. The cascade of drapery falling from his left arm to the ground is the single most Giottesque passage in the fresco: broad, plastically modelled, and structurally weighty.

- The cleric behind the saint (left)

An elderly, clean-shaven man in a dark blue mantle with a white collar, hands joined in prayer, his lined face directed toward the miracle. The habit and the absence of insignia suggest a canon or an assisting cleric rather than a named protagonist.

- The group of witnesses (right, background)

Five male heads compressed in overlapping planes, arranged in a rising diagonal that closes the composition: an elderly bearded man immediately behind the mitre; two further heads pressed together, one balding with a grey beard, the other with heavy brow and beard; a hooded figure in brown, his hand laid on the shoulder of a companion in dull red-purple; and, at the far right, a young beardless man in a dark blue mantle with tawny lining and a close-fitting coif, seen almost in lost profile. The mutual touching, the leaning forward, and the differentiation of age and physiognomy constitute the Giottesque chorus of witnesses, whose function is to register the miracle emotionally and to establish the depth of the group.

- The kneeling figure (right foreground)

A youthful, beardless man in profile, wearing a rose-pink mantle of ample fall with a grey-white hood over the shoulders, hands joined, knees on the pavement. His scale, isolation and devotional posture distinguish him from the witnesses. He may be read either as a participant in the narrative or as a donor portrait; given that the heraldry frescoed under the arch of the chapel is that of the Soldani family, a commemorative reading is plausible but cannot be asserted from the image alone.

The fresco displays a mature Giottesque idiom inflected toward the softer, more chromatic manner associated with Assisi in the second quarter of the Trecento: solid volumetric modelling, restrained gestural rhetoric, an architecture used to measure space, but also a delicate, luminous palette — ochre, rose, dull violet, blue-green — closer to Sienese sensibility than to Florentine severity. Condition is uneven: the dark ground of the portico has suffered extensive abrasion and loss of the *a secco* layers, with pronounced vertical staining and bituminous blackening along the lower step; the flesh passages and the drapery of the bishop are comparatively well preserved.

The attribution to Puccio Capanna derives from Fra Ludovico da Pietralunga (16th century; ed. Scarpellini, 1982) and is accepted by most modern scholarship. Vasari instead named Tommaso di Stefano (Giottino), and also confused St Stanislaus with St Nicholas. Roberto Longhi (*Stefano Fiorentino*, 1943) and Pietro Scarpellini (*Di alcuni pittori giotteschi nella città e nel territorio di Assisi*, in *Giotto e i giotteschi in Assisi*, 1969) are the two references cited in the Fondazione Zeri catalogue entry for the cycle; both should be consulted directly, since the Giottino / Stefano Fiorentino alternative has never been definitively closed.

### Martyrdom of St Stanislas (attr.)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 3.jpg" alt="Martyrdom of St Stanislas">
  <figcaption>Martyrdom of St Stanislas, 1337-47, fresco, Lower Basilica of San Francesco, Assisi.</figcaption>
</figure>

The scene belongs to a small cycle painted in the chapel that the Lower Church devoted to the bishop-martyr of Kraków, and its presence in the Franciscan mother church is not incidental. Innocent IV had canonised Stanislaus at San Francesco itself in 1253, and Alexander IV granted indulgences in 1256 to those visiting the basilica on the saint's feast; the bull refers to an altar dedicated to him that had been commissioned by the chapter of Kraków Cathedral, and this became the Cappella di San Stanislao around 1300. The fresco is therefore the visual terminus of a Polish–Umbrian devotional relationship nearly a century old at the time of painting.

The composition is organised around an ambitious feat of fictive architecture. The narrative is framed by a painted pointed arch supported on clustered piers, with two half-length figures set in roundels in the spandrels; through this proscenium the viewer is admitted to a rib-vaulted church interior, its bays receding in sharply converging orthogonals toward a sanctuary in which a gilded cross rises above the altar within a Gothic tabernacle. The painter has rendered the vault webs in a deep blue-black that turns the interior into a dark cavity, against which the pale figures are silhouetted, and he has taken the trouble to include the horizontal iron tie-rod stretched across the nave — the kind of unrhetorical structural observation that marks the descent of this idiom from Giotto's interiors in the Arena Chapel and, more immediately, from the Assisan workshop practice of the preceding generation. The intelligence of the device lies in its narrative function: the crime is placed inside a consecrated space, and the altar with its cross presides over the sacrilege as silent witness.

The event depicted conflates two moments of the legend. Stanislaus, bishop of Kraków, had excommunicated King Bolesław II, who responded by killing him with a sword while he celebrated Mass; the fresco shows the king bending over the dead body while bystanders brandish the severed limbs; however. the reading of the stooping figure as Bolesław II is conventional but should be checked against the iconographic literature; some accounts treat all three assailants as anonymous executioners.

The saint lies prostrate in the foreground in a golden-ochre chasuble whose broad, softly modelled folds occupy the entire lower register, his head fallen to the left near the ground and his mitre displaced beside him. Over him stoops a heavy-set bearded figure in a lavender-blue mantle worn over a sage-green tunic, his back turned and his weight thrown forward — the compositional fulcrum of the scene. At the right, a bearded man in green raises a severed leg above his head, the foot rigidly extended; at the left, a figure in a rose-coloured mantle, seen almost as a *repoussoir* against the pier, lifts a severed hand into the upper field. The three standing figures thus describe an arc of upraised arms around the horizontal mass of the corpse, and the dismemberment — a detail of the *vita* that later Polish tradition read allegorically, as the divided kingdom that would be made whole again — is given its full brutal literalism.

Stylistically the fresco confirms the reasons why this personality has long been read as the most substantial Giottesque presence in Assisi after the departure of the Florentine workshop. The figures are conceived as weighted volumes, their drapery falling in broad planes that define the body beneath rather than describing surface pattern; the palette is deliberately subdued — earth reds, sage, ochre, a single passage of blue — and the emotional register is restrained to the point of gravity, with none of the shrillness found in contemporary Umbrian narrative painting. What distinguishes it from Florentine work proper is a certain slackness in the figures' articulation and a preference for silhouette over foreshortened mass in the secondary characters.

The state of preservation is uneven. Extensive lacunae disfigure the right half, where the intonaco has been lost down to the *arriccio*, and there is severe abrasion in the *a secco* passages, notably in the vaults and in the drapery of the figure at the right. The gilding of the altar cross survives only in part. Losses along the lower edge and in the pier at left further compromise the reading of the spatial box.

### Crucifixion (attr.)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 4.jpg" alt="Crucifixion">
  <figcaption>Crucifixion, c. 1340, fresco, Sala Capitolare (former Chapter Room, now Cappella delle Reliquie), Sacro Convento, Assisi.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fresco occupies the full lunette of an end wall in the chapter room of the papal palace annexed to the basilica — a square hall with cross vaults springing from a central pier, built between 1228 and 1239 under Gregory IX and reached through a door in the right transept of the Lower Church. The function of the room conditions the image entirely. A chapter house was the space of communal assembly, of profession and correction, and the painter has accordingly abandoned narrative for a devotional and institutional formula: not the historical Calvary with its executioners and crowd, but a Crucifixion presided over by a symmetrical assembly of intercessors, in which the friars gathered beneath saw their own Order ranged at the foot of the Cross.

The composition is governed by the shape of the field. A broad painted border of Cosmatesque derivation — bands of lozenges, quatrefoils and interlace, punctuated at intervals by small square medallions containing bust-length figures — follows the curve of the archivolt and returns along the base, giving the whole the character of an inlaid architectural panel rather than an opened window. Within it, the cross rises on the central axis to the apex of the lunette, its titulus set just below the crown of the arch and flanked by two small angelic figures.

The ground is now a uniform dark reddish-brown. This should not be read as an intended colour: it is the preparatory *morellone* layer, and the loss of the blue applied over it *a secco* has stripped the fresco of its original tonal register. The consequence is that the pale figures now stand out with a starkness the painter did not plan, and the surviving azurite passages in one or two garments are the only index of what the wall once looked like. Some accounts additionally describe the fresco as left unfinished — a claim worth separating from the question of the lost secco layer, since the two explanations are often conflated.

Christ hangs from a plain, unornamented cross, the body rendered in a chalky ivory, the torso long and only lightly modelled, the head inclined to the right, the perizoma falling in soft folds without theatrical agitation. Around the cross-arms move angels, some receiving the blood of the wounds in small vessels, others in attitudes of grief with foreshortened wings; the two upper figures are set frontally in white and rose, the two lower ones in dark blue, seen in flight against the ground.

Below, the standing saints are disposed along a shallow foreground ledge in a strictly balanced frieze. The company comprises Louis of Toulouse, Paul, the Virgin, Francis, Clare, John the Evangelist, Peter and Anthony of Padua. At the far left a mitred bishop-saint in a white cope holds a crozier; beside him an apostle in a heavy rose mantle; then the Virgin, veiled in white, her hands raised toward the cross. Balancing them at the right stand a figure in a dark mantle, an elderly bearded apostle in pale gold, and a Franciscan friar holding a book. The two kneeling figures immediately at the foot of the cross — a friar to the left, a veiled Clarissan to the right, both in profile with hands raised — are the ideological hinge of the picture: the founders of the two Franciscan branches placed closer to the crucified body than either the Virgin or the apostles, an arrangement that could hardly be more explicit about where the Order located itself in the economy of salvation.

Stylistically the fresco confirms the Giottesque affiliation of this personality while showing the characteristic softening of that idiom in Assisan hands. The figures are conceived as columnar masses under broad, quiet drapery; gestures are minimal; expression is carried by the inclination of heads rather than by facial distortion. What one does not find is the taut spatial construction of Giotto's own narratives — the saints occupy a strip of ground with almost no depth behind them, and the picture's coherence is rhythmic rather than perspectival.

Condition is poor. A large lacuna interrupts the lower centre, further losses affect the border and the base, and abrasion is general across the surface.

### The Crucifixion (attr.)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 2.jpg" alt="The Crucifixion">
  <figcaption>The Crucifixion, c. 1330, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 17.8 x 14.0 cm, North Carolina Museum of Art, Raleigh.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel belongs to the category of small-format devotional painting produced in central Italy in the second quarter of the Trecento, objects intended for private meditation at close range rather than for liturgical display. It has been reconstructed as the central compartment of one wing of a diptych, its companion being a Madonna and Child enthroned with angels in the Vatican Pinacoteca, framed by eight subsidiary compartments with female saints and the Annunciation. If that pairing holds, the Crucifixion functioned as the Passion counterweight to an Incarnational image — the standard bipartite devotional logic of the period.

The composition is governed by compression. Christ's cross occupies the full height of the field, but the picture plane is only nominally deep: a narrow shelf of grey rock at the base and a crowded frieze of mourners establish the ground, while the gold behind the figures denies any recession. The painter has articulated the sky as a rectangular field of lapis confined to the upper register, an unusual solution that reads simultaneously as darkened heavens and as an inlaid heraldic panel, and which throws the pale, attenuated body of Christ into sharp tonal relief.

The corpus follows the *Christus patiens* type in its mature Giottesque redaction: the head has fallen onto the right shoulder, the eyes are closed, the ribcage is drawn taut over a hollowed abdomen, and the body describes a shallow S-curve that displaces the hips to the left. The modelling proceeds from a green-earth underpaint through thin white lead highlights, a technique still legible in the raking light of the torso. Against this restrained naturalism the blood is handled in an emphatically graphic manner: vermilion is applied in linear rivulets that spring from the nails in taut, almost calligraphic arcs, streams from the lateral wound across the perizoma, and descends the shaft of the cross in a rhythmic cascade toward the rock below. This tension — plastic bodies articulated in Giotto's idiom, blood transcribed as ornamental arabesque — is characteristic of Assisan panel painting of these decades and marks the artist's distance from the Florentine mainstream.

The two half-length angels flanking the cross are foreshortened in flight, their wings splayed and abbreviated at the edges of the field; the angel at the left inclines forward with a vessel to receive the blood of the wound, a Eucharistic motif of long standing, while its counterpart at the right registers grief through a contracted brow and raised hand. Their haloes, like all others in the panel, are tooled with dense radial punching, the ray-halo convention that produces the shimmering, textile-like surface visible across the gold ground.

The lower register divides into two emotionally contrasted groups. At the left, the Virgin collapses in the *spasimo* — the swoon that thirteenth- and fourteenth-century devotional literature, above all the Franciscan *Meditationes vitae Christi*, had made central to affective Passion piety. She is caught by a holy woman in red and blue whose face is drawn into a mask of open grief, while a third figure recedes into shadow at the panel's edge. The hands throughout this group are rendered with splayed, elongated fingers, a mannerism the painter exploits as an index of emotion. At the right, John the Evangelist stands upright in a pale rose mantle over a blue tunic, head bowed and hand raised to his chin in the conventional posture of contained sorrow, his broad, plastically modelled drapery providing the compositional ballast against the collapsing left-hand group. Behind him press two soldiers, one in a vermilion tunic with gold-hemmed decoration and a soft cap, whose impassivity closes the narrative.

The panel's condition shows a fine overall craquelure, localised abrasion in the flesh tones and in the mordant-gilt hems, and the tooled engaged frame appears substantially intact.

### Madonna and Child enthroned among angels with Annunciation and Saints Catherine of Alexandria, Agnes, Lucia, Mary Magdalene, Elizabeth of Hungary, Clare, Agate

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 5.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child enthroned among angels with Annunciation and Saints Catherine of Alexandria, Agnes, Lucia, Mary Magdalene, Elizabeth of Hungary, Clare, Agate">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child with the Crucifixion, Angels and Saints (votive tablet - attr.), 1335–45, tempera on parchment, 33.5 × 48.5 x 2 cm, Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria, Perugia.</figcaption>
</figure>

The tablet is a small, horizontally disposed devotional field of remarkable iconographic density: within an area barely larger than a sheet of writing paper, more than thirty figures are distributed across a compartmental scheme of the kind normally reserved for painted altarpieces many times its size. The technical evidence is the most interesting thing about it. Parchment prepared and gessoed as though it were wood, then mounted on a wooden support, places the object at the intersection of two workshop traditions — panel painting and illumination — and accounts for the frequent suggestion that it served as the back or covering of a tabernacle rather than as an independent picture.

The armature is a painted grid rather than a carved one. Two principal rectangular fields — the Virgin enthroned at the left, the Crucifixion at the right — are separated by a narrow vertical strip of stacked compartments, and the whole is enclosed by outer vertical strips at each edge and by horizontal borders above and below, in which small figures alternate between rectangular fields and cusped, star- or quatrefoil-shaped frames. The effect is that of a fictive polyptych: the outer strips function as the *pilastrini* of a carpentered altarpiece, the borders as its cornice and predella.

The Virgin is enthroned frontally on a stepped seat with a semicircular base, dressed in a deep blue mantle over a red gown, her head veiled and inclined toward the Child, who sits on her left knee in a reddish tunic and reaches toward her breast. Behind the throne hangs a cloth of honour, its upper edge articulated by a band of raised white pearling and by rows of red and white dots that imitate an inscribed textile in the manner of pseudo-Kufic ornament — a device of Sienese and Umbrian derivation. Two haloed attendants flank the throne, and two smaller haloed figures in dark religious habits stand at the base of the steps, at the left and at the right; the identification of these last, and whether one of them records a donor, is not resolved by the catalogue.

Christ hangs from a cross whose *titulus* projects above the upper frame, the arms drawn taut and blood issuing from the hands, side and feet. The body is elongated and rendered in a chalky pallor against the dark ground. Two small haloed heads appear at either side of the cross in the upper field — mourning angels seen as busts, a compressed version of the flying angels of larger compositions. At the foot of the cross the ground is broken to reveal the skull of Adam, over which the blood descends: the Golgotha–Adam motif that binds the Passion to the Fall. To the left stands the Virgin, her head bowed and hands raised, and to the right an older bearded figure in a heavy blue-grey mantle who turns his face upward — traditionally John the Evangelist, though the physiognomy here is unusually aged.

The catalogue lists the following personages across the whole tablet: the Madonna, the Christ Child, Christ, John the Evangelist, Peter, Agnes, John the Baptist, Paul, George, Louis of Toulouse, Martin, Francis, Michael the Archangel, Anthony of Padua, Christopher, Catherine of Alexandria and Mary Magdalene, together with angels, saints, an apostle and prophets. These are distributed as follows, according to a published reading of the compartments (which my own reading of the image supports at every point where the attributes are legible):

- Left *pilastrino*, top to bottom:

St Peter (bust, upper corner) — St George, mounted, dispatching the dragon — St Louis of Toulouse, mitred over a Franciscan habit, the standard formula for the Angevin friar-bishop — St Martin, mounted, dividing his cloak with the beggar — St Catherine of Alexandria (lower corner).

- Central *pilastrino*, top to bottom

St John the Baptist, bearded and in his garment of hair — an unidentified apostle — St Francis, half-length within a cusped frame, displaying the stigmata — a female martyr with a sword — St Mary Magdalene, with her ointment jar, in the lower border.

- Right *pilastrino*, top to bottom:

St Paul (bust with sword, upper corner) — St Michael the Archangel — St Anthony of Padua, in the Franciscan habit — St Christopher, bearing the Christ Child on his shoulders — St Agnes (lower corner).

- Upper and lower borders:

sequences of cusped medallions containing half-length figures, described in the catalogue as angels and prophets. Their small scale and the abrasion of the surface make individual identification largely impossible without direct examination.

The programme is emphatically Franciscan without being exclusively so. Francis occupies the central axis between the two main scenes; Anthony of Padua and Louis of Toulouse complete the Order's canonical triad; and around them is assembled a repertory of universal intercessors — the two princes of the apostles, the two soldier-saints on horseback, the Archangel, Christopher, and the three great female saints — of exactly the kind one would expect in an object of private or confraternal devotion in Umbria in the 1330s and 1340s.

The handling is that of a painter trained on Giottesque models but working at miniature scale and adapting accordingly: the Virgin's drapery falls in broad, weighted masses and the throne is set out with a rudimentary sense of volume, while the small figures of the *pilastrini* are conceived as silhouettes with abbreviated attributes. The palette, so far as it can now be judged, was built on deep blue, vermilion, and gold, with extensive mordant gilding in the haloes and the cloth of honour.

The state of preservation is poor. The surface is heavily abraded and darkened by oxidised varnish, which has flattened the tonal range into a uniform brown; there are losses along all four edges and in the upper centre, a vertical split running down from the top edge, and considerable loss of the gilding. Much of the original chromatic distinction between the compartments has been lost.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels, the Annunciation and Saints

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 6.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels, the Annunciation and Saints">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels, the Annunciation and Saints, c. 1330, tempera and gold on board, golden frame, 36.5 x 24 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the surviving wing of a small two-panel devotional work, and its counterpart is the Raleigh panel which has been reconstructed as the central compartment of the opposite wing of the same diptych, this Vatican panel being the one that survives intact, with its enthroned Madonna and Child with attending angels at the centre and eight smaller compartments around it containing female saints and the Annunciation. The two halves thus articulated the standard bipartite structure of Trecento private devotion — Incarnation confronting Passion — with the Marian wing preserved in full and the Christological wing reduced to its central field.

What the intact panel makes visible, and what the Raleigh fragment cannot, is the sheer architectural ambition of these tiny objects. The picture surface is organised as a fictive polyptych in three registers, its compartments separated by slender colonnettes and closed by pointed arches, the whole rendered in raised and gilded gesso so that the painted architecture reads as physically projecting from the surface. At 36.5 × 24 cm the panel condenses the apparatus of a monumental altarpiece into something that could be closed and carried.

The Virgin is enthroned frontally beneath a cusped and gabled canopy crowned by crocketed pinnacles, executed in *pastiglia* and gilded. The throne back is hung with a red textile patterned in gold, set against a tooled gold ground. She wears a deep blue mantle over a red gown, her head veiled and inclined slightly to her right; the Child, lightly draped in rose, is carried on her left arm and turns upward to touch her chin — an affective gesture characteristic of the years around 1330, when the hieratic Maestà formula was being softened by the tenderness of Sienese models.

Two standing angels flank the throne within the same compartment, dressed in blue tunics with gold-embroidered borders, each holding a tall vertical attribute (a staff or candlestick). Two further angels appear as half-length figures in red, with spread wings, in the small quadrangular fields at the upper corners of the middle register — occupying, in effect, the spandrels of the fictive architecture.

The subsidiary compartments. Reading the registers in sequence:

- Upper register (four arched compartments)

The two central fields carry the Annunciation, disposed across the panel's vertical axis: at the left the angel Gabriel, in pale rose-white, caught in a forward stride with the right arm raised in salutation; at the right the Virgin Annunciate, wrapped in a dark blue mantle, head bowed and hands drawn across the breast in the gesture of humble acceptance. Flanking them stand two female saints — at the far left a figure in pale blue-grey holding a small vessel in her right hand, at the far right a figure in cream and gold, her right arm extended and holding an elongated attribute (a palm or a staff; the surface is too abraded to determine which).

- Middle register (two lateral compartments)

At the left, a female saint in pale rose wearing a circlet or crown, holding a small object at her breast; at the right, a female saint in a red mantle with a white veil, holding what appears to be a spray of flowers or a small phial.

- Lower register (four arched compartments)

Four further female saints, from left: a figure in orange-red with hands joined in prayer; a veiled figure in very dark blue, her hands at her breast, whose habit suggests a religious rather than a virgin martyr; a third in dark blue; and at the far right a figure in dark blue with white, holding an object at chest height.

The eight lateral figures thus constitute a litany of female intercessors surrounding the Virgin — a programme that suggests either a female patron or a female religious community as the original destination, though nothing in the panel itself documents this. It is worth noting frankly that in the present state of the surface most of these saints have lost their identifying attributes; any list of names would be conjecture, and I would not publish one without direct examination or a museum catalogue entry.

The technical repertory is the standard one of the Assisan and central Italian workshops of the period: water gilding over red bole, haloes tooled with dense radial punching, *pastiglia* for the architectural mouldings and pinnacles, mordant gilding for the drapery hems and the throne textile, and flesh built over a green underpaint. The blue passages are the panel's chief conservation problem — the mantles of the Annunciate Virgin and of the lower-register saints have darkened almost to black, the characteristic degradation of azurite, so that the original chromatic balance between blue, vermilion and gold is now badly distorted. The gold ground is abraded throughout, most severely in the upper register, and there are losses along the engaged frame.

### Annunciation

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Puccio-Capanna/image 7.jpg" alt="Annunciation">
  <figcaption>Annunciation, 1330-40, fresco, Monastero di San Giuseppe (ex chiesa di Sant'Apollinare), Assisi.</figcaption>
</figure>

The scene occupies the left-hand portion of the wall raised behind the *tramezzo* that divides the public nave from the monastic choir, immediately above the grille through which the enclosed community followed the divine office. It forms part of a sequence in which the Annunciation stands at the left and a Crucifixion at the centre; both were severely compromised by the later insertion of the wooden ceiling beams, which caused extensive loss of the *intonachino* and the destruction of the upper half of each narrative. A beam-head is still visible at the top edge of the present photograph, and the surviving painting reads as an irregular island of original plaster set within an expanse of modern white *intonaco*. The consequence for the reading of the image is decisive: the entire upper register — the head and shoulders of the angel, the descending dove, the celestial segment, and any *titulus* or scroll bearing the *Ave gratia plena* — is irrecoverably lost. What remains is the lower half of a two-figure composition, preserved to roughly the height of the Virgin's shoulders on the right and to the angel's forearm on the left.

The surface elsewhere shows the characteristic pathologies of a fresco long concealed and then uncovered: abrasion of the *a secco* finishing layers, generalised loss of chromatic saturation, scattered lacunae exposing the *arriccio*, and — particularly across the blue passages of the lower right — accidental splashes of modern white distemper deriving from the whitewashing of the surrounding wall.

The scene follows the canonical Trecento disposition: the angel enters from the left, the Virgin is seated at the right, and the two are separated, but not divided, by an architectural armature that occupies the centre of the field. That armature is the most Giottesque element of the design. A shallow structure articulated by orthogonal members — posts and horizontal beams rendered in a warm brick-red — recedes obliquely into the picture space, functioning less as description of a specific building than as the *scatola prospettica* that measures depth and locates the figures within a coherent interior. Behind and above it appear the remains of a further architectural volume: a wall in rose-pink masonry, a projecting cornice, and, at the upper right, an arched niche or arcaded aperture with a dark recessed interior, terminating against the vertical painted border that closes the composition on the right. That border, with its cornice and a band of inset roundels or quatrefoils, is the original decorative frame and confirms the present right-hand edge as the intact terminus of the scene.

Only the lower two-thirds of the angel survives. The body is enveloped in a mantle of deep rose-red, falling in ample, weighty folds that pool on the ground and establish the figure as a solid mass rather than a linear silhouette — the plastic conception of drapery inherited directly from Giotto. Beneath the mantle a paler, greyish-white garment emerges at the lower right of the figure; whether this belongs to the tunic or represents the lower edge of a wing cannot be determined from the present state, and the identification should not be asserted. The right arm, sheathed in a sleeve of blue-green, is extended horizontally across the intervening space, the hand open with fingers slightly separated in the gesture of salutation and speech. The pose is that of the kneeling or genuflecting Gabriel rather than the standing type, though the loss of the upper body makes this a reading of probability rather than of certainty.

The figure of the Virgin is the best-preserved passage of the fresco and the only surviving head. She is seated in three-quarter view, turned toward the angel, her face youthful, full-cheeked and beardlessly smooth, framed by fair hair drawn back beneath a fine white veil. The halo is rendered as a disc with a radiating striated pattern, incised and modelled rather than tooled in relief. Her right hand is raised, palm outward at the height of the breast — the gesture of *turbatio*, the startled recoil of Luke 1:29 — while the left rests in her lap upon a pale rectangular object, most plausibly the open book of Isaiah, though the abrasion of the passage leaves the alternative reading of a fold of white cloth open.

Her upper garment now appears as a warm off-white, its original tonality almost certainly altered by the loss of surface glazes; the mantle covering the lower body retains a substantial area of blue, evidently azurite applied *a secco*, which has suffered the granular disaggregation typical of that technique. Behind her, a bench or seat-back is articulated by a horizontal panel carrying a delicate Gothic ornamental frieze of cusped or foliate motifs — a passage of miniaturist refinement that contrasts instructively with the broad handling of the drapery.

The fresco belongs to the mature Giottesque idiom as it was inflected at Assisi in the second quarter of the Trecento: volumetric figures conceived as bodies occupying measurable space, restrained and legible gesture, architecture employed structurally rather than decoratively. Against this Florentine armature the colour is markedly softer and more luminous than Florentine practice — rose, blue-green, ochre, pale azure — and the ornamental sensibility of the bench frieze points toward local Umbrian taste. It is precisely this tension between Giottesque structure and Umbrian chromatism that has governed the placement of the work within the artist's career.

The attribution to Puccio Capanna is effectively unanimous in the modern literature, and the frescoes were recognised as the work of a major follower of Giotto immediately upon their discovery: uncovered by the restorer Domenico Brizi, they were inspected in 1911 by Umberto Gnoli, who judged them of great interest and the work of one of Giotto's finest followers, proposing their acquisition for the local Pinacoteca; the proposed *stacco* was fortunately never carried out. Earlier criticism had assigned them elsewhere: Sirén (1908, 1917) grouped the Sant'Apollinare Annunciation and Crucifixion with works he referred to Giottino.

The chronology, by contrast, remains genuinely open, and all proposals are stylistic rather than documentary. Boskovits (1981) placed the Sant'Apollinare frescoes at the beginning of the painter's activity, alongside the *cantoria* of San Francesco; Lunghi (1986) argued for a date close to 1334; Volpe (1983) for c. 1340; while Todini and Zanardi (1980) assigned them to a later moment, when Puccio's Umbrian roots reassert themselves over his formation in Giotto's workshop. A published caption should therefore give a range — *c.* 1330–1340 — with the four positions set out in a note, and should avoid attaching the work to the documented dates of 1341 or 1347, which bear on other, lost commissions.

## Style and technique

Puccio's stylistic personality, as reconstructed from the documented fragment and its stylistic neighbours, is that of a Giottesque of the second generation who developed the pictorial, atmospheric possibilities latent in Giotto's Assisan work — above all the Magdalen Chapel of the Lower Church — rather than the monumental, sculptural line of the Peruzzi Chapel in Santa Croce.

Boskovits (DBI, 1975) characterises Puccio's staging as simple, built with a firm sense of perspective, the forms absorbed into a light that softens contours but confers a rare plastic evidence and reality; his direction is measured, even slow, and beneath a controlled surface lie true human warmth and, at times, harrowing drama. Todini (EAM, 1993) stresses Puccio's capacity to define form through the dense impasto of colour ("il denso impasto del colore"), integrating surface truth and subtle modelling within broadly Giottesque structures, and calls him "forse il più grande, certo il più moderno discepolo di Giotto." Coletti had earlier called the anonymous master (his "Maestro colorista di Assisi") the greatest colourist, the "pittore più pittore," of the entire Italian Trecento; Scarpellini saw in him a precursor of Masaccio.

Puccio's formation is placed within the climate of the Lower Church workshop, alongside the "Maestro dell'Infanzia di Cristo" (behind whom Todini suspected the real Stefano Fiorentino) and the Umbrian "Maestro delle Vele." Scholars have also noted the possible impact of Pietro Lorenzetti's frescoes in the left transept, and reciprocal affinities with Ambrogio Lorenzetti around 1330 (Volpe suggested that by then Puccio was already a formed painter capable of influencing others). Longhi and Coletti observed that Puccio's manner of modelling with colour found its closest echoes not in Umbria — where the plague of 1348 cut short local continuity — but among the Lombard-Paduan Giottesques (the Master of the San Gottardo Crucifixion, Giusto de' Menabuoi, Giovanni da Milano), raising the possibility, which the *Dizionario Biografico* does not dismiss, that Puccio's career ended in northern Italy; his qualities were also developed, perhaps via works left in Florence, by the author of the San Remigio Pietà (Uffizi) — probably Giottino.

On technique: the frescoes were executed in *buon fresco* with substantial *a secco* finishing. Puccio's documented gate commission specifies "bono acçurro et cinabrio" (good azure and cinnabar) — the azurite and vermilion that, being unstable in true fresco, required application *a secco*, and whose loss explains the surface abrasion of many of the works. The "Assisi Problem," addressed magisterially by Alastair Smart in *The Assisi Problem and the Art of Giotto: A Study of the Legend of St Francis in the Upper Church of San Francesco, Assisi* (Oxford, Clarendon Press, 1971) and, on the technical side, by Bruno Zanardi's restoration-based studies (culminating in *Giotto e Pietro Cavallini. La questione di Assisi e il cantiere medievale della pittura a fresco*, Milano, Skira, 2002), concerns primarily the Upper Church *Legend of St Francis*; but the same methodological questions — of *giornate* (the day-by-day plaster patches), sinopie (the underdrawings), and the division of hands within a large workshop — bear directly on the reconstruction of Puccio, whose personality was recovered precisely through Zanardi's close technical examination and restoration of the semi-illegible 1341 fragment.

## The Question of Identity and the Limits of the Sceptical Position

Three positions are conceivable with respect to "Puccio Capanna": that he denotes a historically real artistic personality; that he denotes a corpus assembled by modern connoisseurship and secured to a name only by convention; or that he is a conflation, a critical fiction generated by the sources themselves. The evidence supports the first, though with qualifications substantial enough that the sceptical position retains real force — provided it is directed at the right object.

A painter of this name is attested at Assisi in 1341 and again in 1347. A single work, the gate fragment of 1341, is secured to him by documentary anchorage. To this extent the historical existence of the artist is not in question.

Everything beyond that fragment rests on stylistic attribution. The internal coherence of the group is genuine and widely conceded; what remains fragile is its attachment to the name. That attachment depends entirely upon Marcucci's identification, proposed in 1963, of the surviving fragment with the work stipulated in the 1341 contract. Should that identification fail, the group would revert to the anonymous condition it occupied for decades, when it circulated variously under the names of "Stefano," Maso di Banco and Giottino. Here, and here alone, scepticism is properly exercised: not against the man, but against the security of the corpus beyond its single documented anchor.

The Vasarian "Puccio Capanna" is, by contrast, demonstrably a conflation — of the Assisan Puccio, of the Florentine Puccio di Simone, and of works by at least five further masters. In this precise sense the figure who appears in the *Lives* is a scholarly fiction, and any reconstruction that accepts Vasari's catalogue as a working list necessarily reproduces that fiction.

### Distinctions from the anonymous masters

Puccio requires careful separation from a series of conventional personalities with which his corpus has at various times been entangled.

The Maestro Espressionista di Santa Chiara, author of the frescoes in the right transept and crossing vaults of Santa Chiara and of works at Gubbio, is an earlier and distinct Giottesque active c. 1315–1330, characterised by Longhi (1963) as an *agrodolce espressionista di radice fiorentina* and identified by [Enrica Neri Lusanna](/scholars.html#neri-lusanna-enrica) (1977) with Palmerino di Guido, Giotto's documented Assisan collaborator, recorded alongside "Iocto Bondoni Florentie" in a loan repayment of 1309; the Palmerino identification has been adopted by the 2026 Perugia exhibition. This master is chronologically prior to Puccio and must not be assimilated to him. He is in turn to be distinguished from the Maestro di Santa Chiara, a pre-Giottesque panel painter named from the historiated panel of St Clare, with whom he is occasionally confused. The Maestro delle Vele, the Umbrian Giottesque of the Lower Church crossing vaults, constitutes a third and separate personality.

The Maestro colorista di Assisi stands on a different footing: it is Coletti's conventional designation (1950) for what is substantially the group subsequently recognised as Puccio's, and therefore functions not as a rival personality but as an alternative and now abandoned label for Puccio himself. Finally, the Maestro della Cappella Velluti, a Florentine Giottesque named from the Velluti Chapel in Santa Croce, is a distinct hand; no identification with Puccio has gained acceptance, and the two should not be run together.

### Distinctions from the homonyms

The name itself is a source of persistent confusion. Puccio Capanna is not to be confused with Puccio di Simone, the Florentine painter documented between 1346/48 and 1360, trained in the orbit of Bernardo Daddi, matriculated in the Compagnia di San Luca under 1357 and later active in the Marches alongside Allegretto Nuzi, who is generally identified with the Master of the Fabriano Altarpiece and is the probable author of the lost signed Crucifixion at Pistoia — the very painter with whom Vasari conflated him. Nor is he to be identified with Puccio di Landuccio, a Pisan architect and sculptor of the later Trecento; nor with Puccio di ser Firenze construed as a Florentine, an ambiguity that appears to lie at the root of Vasari's error; nor with Pace di Bartolo d'Assisi, a later Assisan painter; nor with Cecco di Pietro, a Pisan of the later fourteenth century. His documented associate Cecce (Cecco) di Saraceno remains all but unknown, surfacing independently only in a document of 1356.

## Legacy and afterlife

Puccio left, in Boskovits's judgement, no deep imprint on local Umbrian painting, both because the mid-century plague cut short the tradition and because his idiom was too personal to found a school. His work was, however, admired by later Umbrians — Todini traces its echoes in the Master of Fossa and the Master of the Silver Cross, in Cola Petruccioli, and in the young Niccolò Alunno in the fifteenth century — and, more consequentially, echoed among the Lombard-Paduan Giottesques. The "afterlife" that most defines him, however, is historiographic: for four centuries he was a footnote to Giotto whose masterpieces bore other names, and his recovery — from Venturi's tentative "Stefano" hypothesis (1907), through Longhi's dazzling but misdirected reconstruction (1951), to Abate's document (1956), Marcucci's intuition (1963), Scarpellini's synthesis (1969), Boskovits's *DBI* entry (1975), and Zanardi's restoration and essay (1978) — stands as one of the exemplary cases of twentieth-century Italian connoisseurship married to archival discovery. The 2026 Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria exhibition, which made his 1341 fragment its emblem and its cover image, marks the culmination of that rehabilitation.

Puccio Capanna was a real Assisan painter of the second quarter of the fourteenth century, a follower of Giotto formed in the ambience of the Lower Church of San Francesco, documented in 1341, 1347, and (in Florence) 1350, and responsible — on the evidence of the one documented survival and its stylistic family — for some of the most pictorially advanced Giottesque frescoes of his generation. He was not the Florentine that Vasari made him, and the large Vasarian catalogue is a fiction compounded of at least three real painters and several attributions since corrected. The secure Puccio is small: one documented fragment, one panel diptych, and a tightly argued group of Assisan frescoes. The rest is attribution — coherent, widely accepted, but attribution nonetheless. The honest verdict is that Puccio Capanna is historically real, artistically major, and documentarily impoverished: a painter whom we know far better through the eye than through the archive, and whose very name we can attach to his masterpieces only because a friar in the 1570s remembered what Vasari chose to forget, and because a document surfaced in 1956 that a fragment on a demolished city gate could confirm.

## Conclusion

The case of Puccio Capanna is, at bottom, a case about the relative authority of different kinds of evidence, and about how long a printed error can outlive the documents that refute it. Vasari's *Lives*, reprinted, translated, and absorbed into the canon of art-historical narrative for nearly four centuries, asserted a Florentine painter of considerable range, working from Rimini to Bologna and signing himself "Puccio di Fiorenza." Set against this authoritative-seeming text stood, until 1926, only a manuscript by a provincial friar that few scholars had read, and, until 1956, an archival record that no one had yet found. The imbalance in circulation between the two kinds of witness — one printed and endlessly available, the other buried in a local library and an ecclesiastical archive — did as much to shape the historiography of Puccio Capanna as any genuine disagreement about style. It is a reminder that the persistence of an error is often a function of publication history rather than of evidential weight.

The recovery itself is worth restating as a sequence, because it is unusually legible as intellectual history. Adolfo Venturi's cautious 1907 suggestion that a group of Assisan frescoes might belong to Ghiberti's "Stefano fiorentino" set the terms of debate for half a century. Roberto Longhi's 1951 essay, brilliant in its connoisseurship but anchored to an undocumented name, represents the high point of stylistic attribution operating without archival control — and its very success in persuading a generation of scholars (Bettini, Volpe, Previtali, Bellosi, Gnudi, Bologna) shows how far pure connoisseurship can carry conviction even when it is, in the end, wrong about the name. The correction came not from a rival connoisseur but from an archivist, P. Giuseppe Abate, whose 1956 publication of the 1341 gate commission introduced a documented painter, "Puccius Capanne," who had nothing to do with Ghiberti's Stefano. That the two threads — Longhi's stylistic group and Abate's document — were not tied together until Luisa Marcucci's observation of 1963 illustrates how a single missing inference can keep a documented fact and a stylistic corpus apart for years, even after both are individually known.

This history also clarifies what kind of claim is actually secure and what kind is not. The existence of a painter named Puccio Capanna, active at Assisi in the 1340s, is beyond doubt: he appears in a communal contract of 1341 and a private sale of 1347, and very probably in the Florentine painters' roll of 1350. The attribution of a specific surviving fragment — the Madonna and Child from the Porta San Rufino, now in the Pinacoteca Comunale — to that contract is equally solid, resting on a straightforward correspondence between text and object. Everything that follows, however — the *cantoria* frescoes of the Lower Church, the chapter-house Crucifixion, the Sant'Apollinare Annunciation, the two devotional panels — belongs to a different evidentiary category: a stylistic family built outward from the one documented anchor by scholars (Scarpellini above all) who judged, on grounds of handling, palette, and figural type, that these works shared a single hand with the 1341 fragment. That judgment has held up well under sustained scrutiny since 1969, but it remains a judgment, not a document, and it should be presented to readers with that distinction intact.

Vasari's specific errors repay one further comment, because they are not random. He conflated at least three distinct painters — the Assisan Puccio, the Florentine Puccio di Simone (matriculated in the same confraternity roll under a later year), and the presumed author of a lost, signed Pistoia Crucifixion — into a single Florentine biography, and he did so under the pressure of an explicit ideological programme: the *Lives* were written to establish Florentine primacy in the arts, and a Puccio born and formed at Assisi, however excellent, was less useful to that programme than a Puccio who could be claimed for Florence and merely stationed, marriage and children included, at Assisi. Vasari's own local informants — in all likelihood Dono Doni and Ludovico da Pietralunga themselves — seem to have told him of an Assisan tradition that he then overrode rather than reported. The episode is a small but exact illustration of how a historiographical agenda can reshape testimony even when the testimony contradicts it only partially.

The stylistic personality that emerges once the Vasarian accretions are stripped away is coherent and, by common critical consent, of real distinction. Puccio's particular contribution to the second generation of Giottesque painting lay in the direction he took from Giotto's own late Assisan work — the atmospheric, coloristic possibilities of the Magdalen Chapel — rather than from the monumental line of the Peruzzi Chapel frescoes in Florence. Boskovits's description of a light that softens contour while conferring plastic evidence, Todini's emphasis on the "denso impasto del colore," and Coletti's judgment that this was the "pittore più pittore" of the whole Trecento, all converge on the same point: a painter who used density and warmth of colour, rather than sculptural line, to carry Giotto's structural lessons forward. That this manner found its closest later echoes not in Umbria, cut short by the plague of 1348, but among the Lombard-Paduan Giottesques, suggests a career that may have ended far from where it began — a suggestion the documents can neither confirm nor rule out.

The corpus itself, considered work by work, displays remarkable consistency of condition as much as of style: nearly every surviving piece attributed to Puccio has come down abraded, its azurite blues degraded to near-black, its *a secco* finishing layers lost, its gilding depleted. This is not incidental. The documented gate contract of 1341 specifies "bono acçurro et cinabrio" — good azure and cinnabar — pigments that, because they required *a secco* application over the true fresco, were always the first casualties of time, damp, and later whitewashing. The poor condition of the Sant'Apollinare Annunciation, the chapter-house Crucifixion, and the Perugia parchment tablet is therefore of a piece with the technical practice the one surviving document itself describes, and it should temper any reading of Puccio's palette that relies on present, rather than reconstructed, colour.

The distinctions drawn throughout this account — from the Maestro Espressionista di Santa Chiara (probably Palmerino di Guido), from the Maestro delle Vele, from the Maestro della Cappella Velluti, and above all from the homonymous Puccio di Simone — are not pedantry but the necessary residue of the very confusion Vasari created. Because Puccio Capanna's modern identity was reconstructed by peeling his work away from at least four other names, any account of him that does not also state what he is not risks sliding back into the pre-1956 condition in which his corpus circulated as a folder of orphaned attributions under whichever name a given scholar preferred that decade. Precision about the boundaries of the corpus is, in this case, inseparable from precision about the man.

Taken as a whole, the Puccio Capanna dossier stands as a model instance of how documentary discovery, archival patience, and stylistic connoisseurship must work together, and of how badly they can fail when only one of the three is available. For four hundred years, connoisseurship without documents produced a sequence of plausible but incompatible names — Stefano, Maso, Giottino, "the colourist master of Assisi" — for a body of work whose real author had been named in an archive since 1341 and in a friar's manuscript since roughly 1570, neither of which anyone had thought to consult against the paintings themselves. The eventual resolution required an archivist to publish a contract, a connoisseur to notice that the contract matched a surviving fragment, and a restorer to clean that fragment well enough to compare it with the rest of the group. What remains, after all this work, is properly modest: a painter securely named and dated by two documents, a single surviving fragment tied to those documents beyond reasonable doubt, and a wider body of pictorially superb Assisan painting attached to his name by the converging judgment of the best twentieth-century connoisseurs — coherent, well argued, and, in the strict sense, still an attribution rather than a certainty.

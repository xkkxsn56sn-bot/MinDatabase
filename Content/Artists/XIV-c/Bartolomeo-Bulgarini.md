## The Historiographic Genesis: From "Ugolino Lorenzetti" to the "Master of the Ovile Madonna"

The modern reconstruction starts with an act of connoisseurship and a deliberately hybrid label. In 1917 <a href="/scholars.html#berenson-bernard">Bernard Berenson</a> grouped a set of Sienese panels whose author, he believed, had been formed by two masters: Ugolino di Nerio, the most faithful heir of Duccio, and Pietro Lorenzetti. He joined their names into the *Notname* "Ugolino Lorenzetti". The label described a style, not a person, and Berenson never presented it otherwise. His articles appeared in *Art in America* across volumes 5 and 6 (1916–1918). He reprinted the argument in *Essays in the Study of Sienese Painting* (New York, 1918), where, among other works, he included a small *Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints and Angels* bought by Isabella Stewart Gardner in 1911 as a Pietro Lorenzetti. The initial group numbered nine paintings, a figure confirmed by <a href="/scholars.html#meiss-millard">Millard Meiss</a> himself in his 1931 *Art Bulletin* article, which notes that the 1923 author "included six of these nine paintings in his reconstruction of the 'Master of the Ovile Madonna.'"

The second stage came in 1923. In the first volume of *Art Studies*, a young American scholar published "The Master of the Ovile Madonna", splitting much of Berenson's group off under a new anonymous name. The eponymous work is the critical point, because it is often misidentified. It is not an *Assumption*. It is a large *Madonna and Child with Four Angels*, formerly in the church of San Pietro a Ovile in Siena and now in the Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra in the Oratorio di San Bernardino. It is dated variously to the third quarter of the fourteenth century or to a broader span. The phrase "Ovile Assumption", which sometimes circulates, conflates two separate works: the Ovile *Madonna*, which gave the anonymous master his name, and the *Assumption of the Virgin* now in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena (no. 61), which came from the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala. The 1923 author held that the works he grouped showed a fundamentally Lorenzettian painter distinct from Berenson's Ducciesque-Lorenzettian hybrid. For about a decade the literature therefore carried two anonymous personalities whose catalogues partly overlapped; as Meiss summarized the state of the question in 1931, "critical opinion varies from acceptance of Berenson's group, or acceptance of [the Ovile group], to denial of the unity of either."

Meiss resolved the duplication in two steps. The first was "Ugolino Lorenzetti", published in *The Art Bulletin* 13 (1931), pp. 376–397. It originated, as recent historiography of his career shows, as a master's thesis topic assigned by his teacher at New York University and was discussed in person with Berenson at I Tatti. In it Meiss argued that the two groups were the work of one artist seen at different stages. On his account the painter's earliest manner was closest to Ugolino di Nerio; it then moved toward Pietro Lorenzetti's weight and spatial depth, and finally toward a more decorative idiom with a marked interest in Simone Martini. Meiss kept Berenson's label for this unified personality. The second step was the article whose title is often misquoted. Its form is "Bartolommeo Bulgarini altrimenti detto 'Ugolino Lorenzetti'?", published in *Rivista d'arte* 18 (1936), pp. 113–136. The question mark is part of the title and matters. Meiss offered the identification as a hypothesis, not a demonstration, and the hypothesis needed corroboration for half a century.

His reasons, as he set them out himself in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry he wrote in 1972, were circumstantial but converging. A Biccherna cover in the Archivio di Stato di Siena showed the style of "Ugolino Lorenzetti", and Bulgarini was documented as having been paid for Biccherna covers. Documents recorded two important altarpieces by Bulgarini for the church of the Ospedale di Santa Maria della Scala, and in the nineteenth century the hospital had deposited in the Sienese gallery three large altarpieces by the anonymous hand (Pinacoteca nos. 61, 76 and 80). Bulgarini lived in the parish of San Pietro a Ovile, where the eponymous *Madonna* of the "Ovile Master" still hung. Vasari had seen a work by "Bologhini" on an altar in Santa Croce in Florence, and a polyptych by the anonymous hand survived in the Santa Croce museum. Finally, Bulgarini was the only follower of Pietro Lorenzetti whom Vasari named, and "Ugolino Lorenzetti" was clearly the ablest of Pietro's known pupils.

The literature records that several scholars remained unconvinced for decades. The identification only became secure when evidence emerged that tied a surviving painting directly to Bulgarini's name. That evidence, discussed below, concerns the Saint Victor altarpiece of Siena Cathedral. Today the identification is effectively universal in museum practice: the Louvre, the Harvard Art Museums, the National Gallery of Art in Washington, the Uffizi, the Yale University Art Gallery and the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum all catalogue the relevant works under Bulgarini's name. Some institutional records still list the older names as alternatives. The Louvre, for instance, keeps an author heading "Maître de la Madone San Pietro d'Ovile" next to Bulgarini's name on its *Crucifixion*.

## An Earlier False Trail: The Tivoli Triptych

Before Berenson's intervention, scholars had tried to attach a work to Bulgarini's documented name, and that attempt is an instructive error. In 1905 a study of the church of Santa Maria in Vulturella at Tivoli published a modest triptych in the church of San Francesco at Tivoli, signed "Bartolomeus de Senis" and dated 1373, and identified its author with Bulgarini. Several writers accepted the identification for some years. In his 1936 article, Meiss pointed out that the Tivoli triptych was by a weak painter who could not have stood beside the masters with whom Bulgarini was ranked in contemporary documents. Since then the proposal has been abandoned. The episode shows the pull of a documented name for scholars looking for an oeuvre. It also explains why the 1373 date can drift into accounts of Bulgarini's late career, a point taken up again below.

## Documentary Biography: What the Archives Record

The documents for Bulgarini's life are unusually rich for a Sienese painter of the Trecento. They were mostly published in the nineteenth century and assembled by Meiss in 1936. They are not all reported consistently in the secondary literature.

His birth is not documented. The estimate of "c. 1300–1310" used by the Louvre and others is an inference from the start of his recorded career, and the National Gallery of Art gives "c. 1300". The Uffizi's catalogue entries give his dates as "Siena, before 1338-1378". No baptismal or fiscal record establishing a birth year has been published, so all such figures are estimates. His family is better attested. The Bulgarini were an established Sienese lineage, and <a href="/scholars.html#steinhoff-judith">Judith Steinhoff</a>'s research stresses his family connections, including kin who held office under the regime of the Nine. Her monograph treats these connections as a factor in his access to civic and institutional patronage. Her argument is persuasive, but it remains an interpretation of patronage patterns, not a documented fact about any particular commission.

The earliest secure document is disputed in a minor but telling way. Meiss's 1972 entry calls the oldest *surviving* document one of 1345, and reports that, according to nineteenth-century Sienese antiquarians, a codex since lost recorded a payment in 1337 for covers of the Biccherna books of that year. The Washington catalogue says documents mentioning him begin in 1338, with payments for Biccherna covers in that and later years. The difference may come from a lost register's dating, or from the conversion of the Sienese year, which began on 25 March. "1337/1338" is therefore the range of his first recorded activity. Steinhoff's monograph gives his active span as 1338 to 1373, the formula quoted in the 2007 *Renaissance Quarterly* review and on Cambridge University Press's page for the 2014 paperback.

The documents from 1345 to 1349 show a painter working for the Commune. The Washington catalogue reports a payment in 1345 for painting a cloth (*pictura unius tovaglie*) for the Palazzo Pubblico, and a Madonna painted in 1349 for the Porta Camollia, one of Siena's city gates. Meiss's entry likewise records a payment of 1345 for a picture and another notice of 1349. The most revealing document of these years, however, comes from outside Siena. An undated memorandum in the archive of the Pistoiese church of San Giovanni Fuorcivitas, now in the Archivio di Stato di Pistoia (Patrimonio Ecclesiastico C 449), lists the best painters available in Florence, Siena and Lucca to complete the altarpiece that Alesso d'Andrea had begun in 1348 and left unfinished, probably because he died in the plague. Bulgarini appears among the "masters of Siena" next to the Sienese Iacopo di Mino and the Florentines Taddeo Gaddi, Andrea Orcagna and Nardo di Cione. The document is usually dated to 1347–1350 or, more narrowly, to 1349 or soon after. It is the clearest contemporary evidence that he was regarded as one of the leading painters in Tuscany at mid-century.

The 1350s and early 1360s bring further civic duties. In 1353 he received payment for Biccherna covers. The cover for January–June 1353, now in the Archivio di Stato di Siena and showing the *camarlingo* and his scribe in their office, is generally attributed to him. Meiss's own 1972 account contains an inconsistency. One sentence records the 1353 Biccherna payment. Another bases the identification on a Biccherna cover of the first half of 1351 in the "Ugolino Lorenzetti" style, saying Bulgarini was paid for a cover in that year and probably that semester. Whether the panel on which the identification rested is the 1351 or the 1353 cover, or whether both payments exist, remains open; the evidence is discussed in the 1936 article (pp. 128–131) and in Meiss's later note of 1955. Most modern summaries link the attribution to the 1353 cover, and that cover is the one most commonly reproduced under his name.

In 1362 Bulgarini held a public office. In the same year, with two colleagues recorded as "Lucha" and "Iachomo", he was paid for moving "la Madonna" within the cathedral. Meiss read this as Duccio's *Maestà*, and the Washington catalogue describes the role as advisory supervision of its relocation. The tentative identification of the colleagues as Luca di Tommè and Iacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio is a scholarly conjecture, signalled as such in Meiss's own text by "forse". In 1363 his name appears in the *Libro delle Capitudini delle Arti*.

The record then turns to the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala. Here the published sources disagree. Meiss's 1972 entry, citing a 1927 list of Sienese painters, states that Bulgarini became an oblate of the hospital in 1363, the same year as the guild register. The Uffizi's catalogue entry for his *Saint John the Baptist* says he "entered as an oblate with his wife in 1366". The National Gallery of Art says he became an oblate, a lay member, in 1370. The Thyssen-Bornemisza collection's biography says that in 1370 "the artist and his wife Bartolomea are registered as members of the confraternity" of the hospital. It is the only published source that names his wife.

One plausible explanation for the 1370 date can be stated, though it should not be taken as settled. Meiss documented that in 1370 Bulgarini gave his goods and lands to the hospital, on condition that his wife and daughter keep the usufruct for life. Later writers may have merged this donation with the act of oblation itself. The documentary basis for 1366 is not given in the published summaries; it may come from Steinhoff's dissertation or monograph. The evidence can be summarised as follows. Bulgarini was certainly bound to the hospital as an oblate by the late 1360s or 1370. He donated his property to it in 1370, reserving the usufruct for his wife and daughter. The year of his oblation is variously given as 1363, 1366 or 1370.

His status as a member of the hospital community is confirmed by a document of 1376. In it "Frate Bartolomeo Bolgharini" sat on a council concerning the chapel then being built at the foot of the Palazzo Pubblico on the Piazza del Campo. The title *frate* is consistent with the status of a lay oblate of the hospital, and Steinhoff's monograph discusses him under that title. He kept a house in the *popolo* of San Pietro a Ovile, where he lived until his death on 4 September 1378. That date is one of the few in his biography on which all sources agree.

## The Problem of the Compagnia della Vergine

Bulgarini's membership of a "Compagnia della Vergine" at the hospital is sometimes asserted. None of the published accounts of Bulgarini names a confraternity of that title in connection with him. That includes Meiss's dictionary entry, the Washington catalogue, the Uffizi entry and the Thyssen biography. The Thyssen biography speaks only of "the confraternity" of the hospital. Separately, a recent Sienese-Florentine publication on the city's confraternities documents a *compagnia della Vergine Maria* meeting in the vaults beneath the hospital from at least February 1365. The temptation to link the two is obvious. But no published source makes that link explicitly.

The word *compagnia* has also become ambiguous in Bulgarini studies for another reason. Steinhoff's article of 2000 uses "compagnia" for a loose professional association of painters in the 1350s. That is a business and workshop arrangement, not a devotional confraternity. The two senses are easily confused in summaries. What can be said is that Bulgarini, his wife and, by implication, his household entered the community of Santa Maria della Scala as oblates or confraternity members. Whether he belonged specifically to the *compagnia della Vergine Maria* meeting at the hospital remains unverified.

## The Question of Vasari's "Bologhini"

Vasari is the reason Bulgarini's name never quite vanished from art-historical memory, and his text deserves to be read exactly. The passage comes at the end of the Life of "Pietro Laurati", Vasari's name for Pietro Lorenzetti. In the 1568 edition it reads: "Fu discepolo di Pietro, Bartolomeo Bologhini sanese, il quale in Siena et in altri luoghi d'Italia lavorò molte tavole; et in Fiorenza è di sua mano quella che è in sull'altare della capella di S. Silvestro in S. Croce." So Vasari states that Bartolomeo was Pietro's pupil, that he painted many panels in Siena and elsewhere in Italy, and that the altarpiece in the chapel of San Silvestro in Santa Croce in Florence was by his hand. In the first edition of 1550 the cognomen appears as "Bolghini". In the same Life, Vasari adds that his own portrait of Pietro was taken from one by Bartolomeo Bologhini "in una tavola in Siena".

Three points follow. First, Vasari's "Bologhini"/"Bolghini" is a corrupted form of the cognomen, very probably a misreading of a painted inscription, possibly the signature on the Santa Croce altarpiece itself. That explanation is proposed in the Washington catalogue and is reasonable but unprovable. Second, Vasari's claim that Bulgarini trained under Pietro is a sixteenth-century assertion, not a contemporary document. It agrees with the stylistic evidence of the reconstructed oeuvre, but it cannot on its own settle the question of training. It is also the source of the frequent statement that Bulgarini was the only Sienese painter of his generation commemorated by Vasari. Third, the identification of Vasari's Santa Croce panel with the polyptych by the "Ugolino Lorenzetti" hand once shown in the Museo dell'Opera di Santa Croce is more fragile than often assumed. Berenson and all later scholars have accepted the attribution of that polyptych to the same hand. It was displayed in the Santa Croce museum until 1966 and is now in the storerooms of the Gallerie Fiorentine. But a curator at the Uffizi has indicated, in information reported by the Washington catalogue, that the polyptych apparently did not belong to Santa Croce originally. If so, one of Meiss's 1936 links weakens, although the identification as a whole no longer depends on it.

## Artistic Formation between Ugolino di Nerio and Pietro Lorenzetti

The question of Bulgarini's training has three layers. The documented layer is thin: Vasari's statement, two centuries after the fact, that he was Pietro Lorenzetti's pupil. The accepted layer is stylistic. Berenson's coinage, Meiss's 1931 analysis and later catalogues agree that his earliest manner is deeply Ducciesque and close to Ugolino di Nerio, and that by the 1340s his figures had taken on the mass, gravity and spatial ambition of Pietro Lorenzetti. Statements that he was an apprentice or assistant in Pietro Lorenzetti's workshop, or trained among Duccio's pupils, are reconstructions from style, not facts from documents.

The hypothetical layer is wider. The Washington catalogue goes further than most, suggesting that Bulgarini's lasting nostalgia for Duccio's figurative tradition makes it "possible" that he began in Duccio's own workshop. Since Duccio died in 1318 or 1319, that would require a birth date near the earliest estimates, and it remains speculation. The Uffizi entry adds that Bulgarini's later ornamental refinement drew on techniques "perfected in Simone Martini's workshop". This is a statement about stylistic and technical affinity. There is no documented apprenticeship with Simone.

What can be said with confidence is that the works assigned to Bulgarini's early phase restate Ducciesque formulas with what the Washington catalogue calls "a nervously accentuated linearism". These works are a Biccherna cover dated 1329 in the Berlin Gemäldegalerie, the polyptych formerly in the Santa Croce museum, and the triptych from Fogliano divided between the Pinacoteca Nazionale and the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Siena. Including the Berlin Biccherna of 1329 is an attribution, not a documented commission. If correct, it would place Bulgarini's independent activity almost a decade before his first recorded payment.

The point at which the two catalogues meet is a single work: the triptych of the *Virgin and Child with Saints Paul and Peter* from the oratory of San Pietro in Villore at San Giovanni d'Asso, now in the Contini Bonacossi Collection of the Uffizi. <a href="/scholars.html#stubblebine-james-h">James H. Stubblebine</a> (1979) assigned it to "Ugolino Lorenzetti", that is, to Bulgarini. <a href="/scholars.html#kanter-laurence">Laurence Kanter</a> returned it to Ugolino, on the evidence of its technique and of its punches, none of which belongs to the small set found in every work of the "Ugolino Lorenzetti" group. The Uffizi catalogues it as Ugolino's with the probable collaboration of Bulgarini, to whom it gives the scowling expressions and sharper emotional charge of the figures. If that division of hands is correct, the triptych would show the young Bulgarini at work beside Ugolino, as the stylistic evidence for his formation suggests. The panel is discussed and illustrated in the <a href="/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Ugolino-di-Nerio.html#triptych-from-san-giovanni-dasso-virgin-and-child-with-saints-paul-and-peter-florence-gallerie-degli-uffizi-contini-bonacossi-collection">catalogue of Ugolino di Nerio</a>.

## The Saint Victor Altarpiece and the Nativity in Cambridge

The central work for Bulgarini scholarship is the altarpiece for the altar of Saint Victor in Siena Cathedral, of which the large *Adoration of the Shepherds* (also catalogued as *The Nativity*) in the Harvard Art Museums/Fogg Museum is the central fragment. Its context is one of the most ambitious programmes in Trecento painting. In the first half of the fourteenth century the Commune and the cathedral works commissioned four altarpieces for the altars of the city's four patron saints, Ansanus, Savinus, Crescentius and Victor, arranged around Duccio's *Maestà* on the high altar. Each altarpiece showed an episode from the life of the Virgin, to whom the cathedral is dedicated. Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi's *Annunciation* of 1333 stood on the altar of Saint Ansanus. Pietro Lorenzetti's *Birth of the Virgin* of 1342 stood on the altar of Saint Savinus. Ambrogio Lorenzetti's *Presentation in the Temple*, also dated 1342, stood on the altar of Saint Crescentius. The fourth altarpiece, the *Nativity* for the altar of Saint Victor, was the last of the cycle and had long been considered lost or unidentified.

The reconstruction proceeded in three stages, and each stage has a different epistemic status. First, Berenson had published the Cambridge panel in 1917 as one of the core works of "Ugolino Lorenzetti". He noted its unusual iconography, including the Trinity, and its debts to Pietro's *Birth of the Virgin*. The Fogg had bought it that year through Duveen Brothers. The museum records an earlier provenance from a Dr. Bonnal of Nice and, with a query, Giuseppe Grassi of Rome. Second, in his 1984 corpus of Sienese altarpieces, <a href="/scholars.html#hendrik-willem-van-os">Henk van Os</a> recognized the Cambridge *Nativity* as the central panel of the Saint Victor altarpiece and proposed that two standing saints in the Statens Museum for Kunst in Copenhagen were its lateral panels. They show Saint Victor and a young female saint, identified as Saint Corona, Victor's companion in martyrdom. This stage rested on dimensions, iconography and style. Third, in 1986 Steinhoff and two colleagues, one of them a conservator, published "The St. Victor Altarpiece in Siena Cathedral: A Reconstruction" in *The Art Bulletin* 68, pp. 610–631. They brought documentary proof that turned the hypothesis into fact.

According to the published abstract, the proof is that a cathedral inventory made in 1591, when the Saint Victor altarpiece was about to be replaced, names its painter for the first time as Bartolommeo Bulgarini. A later inventory of 1594, cited in the Washington catalogue from Siena, Duomo, ms 871, fol. 3v, describes the work as "tavola della *Natività* dipinta da Bartolomeo Bulgarini da Siena". The two dates are sometimes presented as contradictory. They are not: both inventories name him, and the 1591 one is the earlier. The Louvre's provenance record adds that the altarpiece was in place in 1594, then recorded in the chapel of San Crescentino between 1610 and 1639, before its parts were dispersed.

The article also argues that a document of 1351, "if interpreted correctly", shows the work was then complete. Steinhoff's 2000 article explains that this is a record of payment for the final woodwork, first noted in a 1983 article in the *Rutgers Art Review*. The 1351 date is therefore a *terminus ante quem* derived from a payment for carpentry, as interpreted by modern scholars. It is not a dated inscription and not a payment to the painter. The Harvard Art Museums catalogue the panel cautiously as "c. 1350", and many secondary sources round the date to "1351". The confirmed facts, then, are that the altarpiece was by Bulgarini and that it hung on the altar of Saint Victor. That the painting itself was finished by 1351 is a strong inference, not something the documents state directly.

The surviving fragments are now widely scattered. The Cambridge panel (inv. 1917.89) is catalogued in tempera on panel and measures 172.4 × 123.2 cm. It has been cut down at the sides and restored several times. The Harvard record describes it as "the last to be completed" of the four patron-saint altarpieces. The predella's central panel is the *Crucifixion* in the Louvre (INV 312), which entered the museum in 1828 from the collection of the Lyonese painter Pierre Révoil. The Louvre's record gives its dimensions as 43.6 × 77 cm, or 61 × 92.4 cm with its frame. The national Joconde database gives 39.5 × 71 cm. The difference probably reflects measurement with or without engaged framing elements. Oddly, the same Louvre record dates the panel to the "second quarter of the fourteenth century (1325–1350)" while also stating that the altarpiece was paid for in 1351. A second predella panel, *The Blinding of Saint Victor* in the Städel Museum in Frankfurt, is dated by the museum to about 1351. The museum notes that the painter modelled the blinding on the iconography of the Kiss of Judas.

The attribution of the Copenhagen laterals (inv. KMS3624, *Saint Corona*, and KMS3625, *Saint Victor*) is a live dispute, and one of the most interesting in Bulgarini studies. The 1986 reconstruction gave them on stylistic and technical grounds, especially halo tooling, to the Simonesque painter known as the Master of the Palazzo Venezia Madonna. That anonymous artist is named after a *Virgin and Child* formerly in the Palazzo Venezia in Rome and now in the Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica. The Statens Museum for Kunst still catalogues them under that name, describing them as side panels flanking Bulgarini's central *Adoration*. The Louvre's record, by contrast, describes them as "often attributed to the Master of the Palazzo Venezia but doubtless by Bulgarini". The Städel also lists them as Bulgarini fragments, giving dimensions of 155 × 48 cm for *Saint Corona* and 158 × 47.5 cm for *Saint Victor*.

In the Steinhoff reading, the altarpiece is a deliberate collaboration in which two painters with distinct styles kept their independence within a single commission. Bulgarini painted the centre and predella and the Palazzo Venezia Master the laterals. Reviewers have treated this as a central case for her thesis of post-plague pluralism. If the Louvre's view is right and the laterals are Bulgarini's, much of that argument would have to be rethought. The dispute is therefore not marginal. It goes to the heart of how the 1350s in Siena are interpreted.

The Cambridge *Nativity* itself shows why the painter fascinated Berenson. The Washington catalogue describes the painting as a turning point. It combines a Lorenzettian feeling for depth and monumental form with a rigorous two-dimensional organization of the surface and a greater decorative refinement, closer to Simone's followers. Its iconography, centred on the Virgin rather than the Child, fits a cathedral dedicated to Mary and the Marian theme of the whole cycle. Steinhoff's monograph discusses an inscription on the Virgin's halo in this panel. As the fixed point of the oeuvre, the *Nativity* is the yardstick against which every other attribution to Bulgarini is now measured.

## The Assumption of the Virgin from Santa Maria della Scala

The second great surviving work is the *Assumption of the Virgin with Saint Thomas Receiving the Girdle* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena (no. 61). As noted above, it is not the "Ovile" painting. It is one of the three large panels the hospital deposited in the Sienese gallery in the nineteenth century, and Steinhoff reproduces it as a colour plate in her monograph. The Virgin rises in a mandorla and drops her girdle to the apostle Thomas, who is shown, unusually, from behind, kneeling to receive it. The emphasis on the girdle has been linked to the hospital's purchase of Marian relics from Constantinople. The relics of the Virgin's veil, girdle and head-cover are all named in the original contract of sale of 1359. On this basis the painting is generally dated around 1360 or to the early 1360s. Recent scholarship on the hospital's relic chapel suggests, cautiously, that the altarpiece "might have been destined for" that chapel.

The statement that the *Assumption* was "commissioned in 1339" for the altar holding the girdle relic is wrong. A commission in 1339 for an altar honouring a relic acquired in 1359 is chronologically impossible. The figure is almost certainly a typographical corruption of 1359 or of a date in the 1360s. The reading of Thomas's turned back as a surrogate for the worshipping viewer, standing in for the hospital community before the newly acquired relics, has been proposed in a 2007 essay. It is an attractive interpretation, not a documented intention. The panel's later influence is better established: Giovanni di Paolo's Staggia polyptych repeats its iconography closely, which shows that Bulgarini's composition had become a recognizably Sienese model of the Assumption.

The *Assumption* also bears on a disputed question of chronology. Meiss and later writers related the two hospital altarpieces documented in the 1370s to the works deposited by the hospital. If the *Assumption* dates to the early 1360s, as the relic purchase suggests, it cannot be either of the documented late altarpieces. The documented works must then be sought among the other panels, or regarded as lost, as the Washington catalogue in fact concludes when it states that "none of his documented works have survived".

## The Late Hospital Altarpieces: 1371 or 1373, and the Unfinished Work of 1379

The documents record two altarpieces painted by Bulgarini for the church of Santa Maria della Scala in the 1370s. Here too the sources disagree. Meiss's 1972 entry states that one was signed and dated 1371, and that it was also recorded by the Sienese chronicler Neri di Donato in his *Cronaca senese*, published in the second edition of the *Rerum Italicarum Scriptores*, XV, 6, p. 653. The Washington catalogue says that he "executed and signed an altarpiece in 1373". Neither published source gives the inscription. The 1373 date appears in the literature in at least one other context, the Tivoli triptych wrongly linked to Bulgarini, so a slip between the two cannot be excluded. That is only a hypothesis about how the discrepancy arose. The chronicle text is the place where the question between 1371 and 1373 can be settled.

The second late altarpiece was begun shortly before Bulgarini's death and left unfinished. The Washington catalogue says it was handed to another painter to complete in 1379. Meiss suggested that the finisher was "probably" Andrea Vanni "or another painter". That remains a conjecture, and no published source confirms the identity of the painter who completed the work.

## Other Principal Works and the Shape of the Oeuvre

Beyond the Saint Victor altarpiece, Bulgarini's catalogue rests on stylistic attribution, and the works can be grouped only provisionally. The account given in the 2016 Washington catalogue offers the most systematic recent framework. It divides the oeuvre into three phases that broadly follow Meiss's 1931 scheme.

The earliest works, as noted, are the Berlin Biccherna of 1329, the former Santa Croce polyptych and the Fogliano triptych. To these the literature adds a *Virgin and Child with Saints Ansanus and Galganus* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale, sometimes thought to come from the Palazzo Pubblico. That provenance has been proposed but is not documented.

The second phase is described as "more massive and voluminous" and "firmly Lorenzettian". It includes the triptych from Sestano in the Pinacoteca Nazionale and a dismembered polyptych from the church of San Cerbone at Lucca, now divided between the Museo Nazionale di Villa Guinigi in Lucca, the Pinacoteca Capitolina in Rome and the National Gallery of Art in Washington. The Washington panel, a *Saint Catherine of Alexandria* (inv. 1943.4.20, Samuel H. Kress Collection), is dated by the museum to c. 1335/1340. The Thyssen-Bornemisza biography dates the Lucca polyptych to around 1345. This is a real difference of five to ten years in the dating of a key early work. It reflects the general uncertainty of Bulgarini's chronology before 1350, where no fixed point exists.

The third phase begins with the Saint Victor *Nativity* and is marked by a more decorative surface, softer modelling and closer affinity with the followers of Simone. It includes several dispersed polyptychs. One, formerly in Sant'Agostino at San Gimignano and now in the Salini collection near Siena, was rediscovered and published by Steinhoff in the *Zeitschrift für Kunstgeschichte* in 1993. Its original location deserves a word of caution: the Washington catalogue gives the church as Sant'Agostino, while Steinhoff's 2000 article, in one passage, mentions a polyptych painted for San Domenico at San Gimignano. Whether this is a slip or a reference to a different work remains unclear. Another polyptych is divided between the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo in Pisa, which has four saints, and the Yale University Art Gallery, which has the central *Virgin and Child Enthroned with Two Angels* (inv. 1943.244), dated by Yale to c. 1355–1360.

A further dispersed polyptych shows how museum research continues to refine the catalogue. Its *Madonna and Child Enthroned* (Pinacoteca no. 76) came from the hospital. Its other surviving parts are a *Saint Gregory the Great*<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gregory-the-great" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> (no. 59) and a *Saint John the Evangelist* (no. 75) in the Pinacoteca, and a *Saint Peter* and *Saint John the Baptist* in the Uffizi (inv. 1890 nos. 6136 and 6137). The Uffizi's catalogue entry reconstructs these as an altarpiece of at least five sections, with half-length saints in a predella-like lower register, probably painted for an altar in the hospital church of the Santissima Annunziata, and dates the Uffizi panels to c. 1355–1360. The *Saint John the Baptist* measures 168 × 43.5 cm. The 2020 display of these restored panels in the Uffizi's room of the *Maestà* marked Bulgarini's first representation in the gallery. Press reports noted that he painted as many as five altarpieces for the hospital church. That figure also appears in the Thyssen biography as "at least five". The number is an inference from the surviving fragments and their hospital provenance, not from a single document.

Smaller devotional works fill out the picture. The *Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints and Angels* in the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum (P15n8), once the centre of a portable tabernacle, measures 65 × 46 × 10.5 cm and is dated by the museum to 1355–1360. Its wings were identified in 1980 with panels in the John G. Johnson Collection at the Philadelphia Museum of Art (no. 92). The Gardner's bibliography traces the whole attributional history: Pietro Lorenzetti in 1914; "Ugolino Lorenzetti" in 1918 and 1931; the Master of the Ovile Madonna in a 1972 census of North American collections; and Bulgarini from 1974. The Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza owns a *Virgin and Child Enthroned between Four Angels, a Martyr Saint and Saint John the Baptist* under Bulgarini's name, and the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam a small *Madonna of Humility*, probably the wing of a diptych, dated around 1353, in which the Christ Child stands to suckle. Works in the Wallraf-Richartz-Museum in Cologne and at Villa I Tatti are also cited in general surveys as attributions.

The occasional statement that the Louvre owns a *Saint Ansanus* by Bulgarini is mistaken. The Louvre's Bulgarini is the *Crucifixion* from the Saint Victor predella, and no *Saint Ansanus* by him is recorded there. The confusion probably comes from Simone Martini's *Annunciation with Saints Ansanus and Margaret*, the first altarpiece of the cathedral cycle, or from the early *Virgin and Child with Saints Ansanus and Galganus* in Siena.

## The Black Death, Meiss's Thesis and Steinhoff's Pluralism

Bulgarini is one of the few Sienese painters of the first rank whose career clearly spans the plague of 1348, and that is why his reconstructed oeuvre has carried so much interpretive weight. In *Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death* (1951), Meiss argued that the catastrophe, together with economic crisis and religious anxiety, produced a turn in Tuscan painting toward hieratic, otherworldly and conservative images. On his view this was a partial retreat from the naturalism of Giotto, Simone and the Lorenzetti. Bulgarini could serve such a narrative: a painter of archaizing tendency, nostalgic for Duccio, who outlived his teachers. The Uffizi entry still calls him, in a phrase consistent with that tradition, "a custodian of the artistic traditions of the first half of the century".

Steinhoff's monograph is a sustained rebuttal of the Meiss thesis, built on Bulgarini's career. Its argument, as quoted with page references in the 2007 *Renaissance Quarterly* review, is that later Trecento Sienese painting was neither reactionary nor mainly shaped by penitential religiosity. It was "profoundly and intentionally pluralistic in style and contents" and not "conservative" (p. 4). By "pluralistic" she means "combining earlier Sienese traditions along with newer approaches" (p. 26) and "artistic diversity" (p. 177), accepting diversity of style even within a single commission. She draws on economic and social historians to argue that the plague reshaped Siena's population, economy and political organization, and so created a new art market. In that market, patrons used style deliberately to convey their values, and painters formed new kinds of working relationships to survive in a shrunken and unstable market.

Her article of 2000 in *Renaissance Studies* gives the technical underpinning. Using stylistic analysis, shared punch tools in gold-ground ornament, and the limited surviving documents, she argues that between about 1350 and 1363 a group of Sienese painters worked as a loose *compagnia*. They shared tools and motifs and collaborated on commissions while each kept an individual style. In earlier decades, by contrast, collaboration within one workshop had required allegiance to a single style. The members named in her work and in reviews include Bulgarini, the Master of the Palazzo Venezia Madonna, Niccolò di ser Sozzo, Luca di Tommè, Iacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio, Naddo Ceccarelli and Bartolo di Fredi, along with the Lombard Giovanni da Milano. Bulgarini emerges as a pivotal figure who greatly expanded his stock of punch tools in these years.

Her proposed partnership of Bulgarini with the Palazzo Venezia Master ran from the Saint Victor commission to about 1353. The terminal date of 1363 coincides with a further plague outbreak and with the death of Niccolò di ser Sozzo, who in 1362 had signed a polyptych jointly with Luca di Tommè. The question mark in the article's subtitle, "c. 1350–1363(?)", is Steinhoff's own acknowledgement that the span is uncertain.

The status of these claims is uneven. Shared punch tools are physical evidence that the same tools were used in more than one shop. The existence of a formally constituted partnership is an inference from that evidence and is not documented by any contract. A "documented 1350s compagnia with Niccolò di ser Sozzo" is sometimes mentioned, but no surviving document records a partnership contract between Bulgarini and Niccolò. The relationship is reconstructed from technical and stylistic evidence. Steinhoff's index records the pairings Bulgarini–Palazzo Venezia Master, Bulgarini–Luca di Tommè, Bulgarini–Niccolò di ser Sozzo and Bulgarini–Pellicciaio. Its documented core is the joint signature of Niccolò and Luca, not a deed involving Bulgarini.

Reviewers have been broadly positive but have marked the limits of the method. A review in *Renaissance Quarterly* (60.3, 2007, pp. 914–915) praised "solid scholarship on an understudied Sienese painter, Bartolommeo Bulgarini, active from 1338 to 1373" but warned that "basing a broad discussion of Sienese painting heavily on one artist is problematic". A review for the College Art Association (caa.reviews no. 1431), which identified the Saint Victor Altarpiece as "a central work in her argument", asked whether the book describes a city-wide trend or only the practice of one closely knit group. It also noted that Bulgarini worked almost exclusively in panel painting and so represents only one dimension of Sienese production. A further review in the *American Historical Review* summarized the work as an argument "against Meiss" for stylistic and iconographic pluralism. Steinhoff's evidence undermines the cruder form of the Black Death thesis as applied to Siena. Her reconstruction of the *compagnia*'s membership and duration is best read as a well-argued model, not an established institutional fact.

## Workshop, Technique and Style

Bulgarini was, as far as the surviving record shows, almost exclusively a panel painter. No frescoes have been convincingly attributed to him, which is unusual among the leading Sienese painters of his time. His documented output covers the full range of the Sienese panel trade: Biccherna covers, painted cloths for the Commune, a gate image, cathedral and hospital altarpieces, and, by attribution, polyptychs for Lucca, San Gimignano, Pisa and Florence, along with small devotional tabernacles.

The Uffizi's description of the restored Peter and Baptist panels mentions an earlier aggressive cleaning that has impoverished the paint surface while leaving the gold ground well preserved. That condition is common to many of his works and makes judgement of his handling of colour harder. Technical study of the Copenhagen laterals was published in the *SMK Art Journal* in 2006, and the Fogg holds X-radiographs of the *Nativity*. Bulgarini's characteristic halo and textile patterns, discussed in Steinhoff's work and visible in details she reproduces from the San Gimignano polyptych and the Washington *Saint Catherine*, are among the most reliable criteria for grouping his panels.

His style is best described as a synthesis of three inheritances, and the order in which they appear underpins the conventional chronology. The Ducciesque-Ugolinesque foundation shows in his figure types, his gentle Marian faces and his conservative compositional formulas. The Lorenzettian layer brings bulk, gravity and an interest in measurable space, most clearly in the Lucca polyptych and the Cambridge *Nativity*. The Simonesque layer appears in his later decorative refinement, his softer modelling and the elaborate ornament of haloes and fabrics. In the Steinhoff reading it is linked to his association with the Palazzo Venezia Master. Whether the order is strictly sequential is debatable, and the relative dating of works before 1350 depends almost entirely on stylistic reasoning. The fixed points of the chronology are few: the attributed Biccherna covers (1329 if accepted, and 1351 or 1353), the Saint Victor altarpiece (completed by 1351 on the interpretation of the woodwork payment), and the *terminus post quem* of 1359 for the *Assumption*. Everything else is placed relative to these.

## Legacy and Critical Fortune

Bulgarini's critical fortune follows an unusual curve. In his lifetime he was clearly prominent. He was listed among the best painters in Tuscany around 1349, entrusted in 1362 with the relocation of Siena's most venerated image, given civic offices, and commissioned for the patron-saint cycle of the cathedral and repeatedly by the city's greatest charitable institution. In the sixteenth century Vasari still remembered him, if under a garbled name, as Pietro Lorenzetti's pupil and the author of an altarpiece in Santa Croce. By then his cathedral altarpiece was being removed from its altar, and within a few decades it had been dismantled and dispersed. His name, already corrupted by Vasari, came loose from his surviving works. Nineteenth-century archival scholarship recovered his documents but could attach no paintings to them, which led to the false trail of the Tivoli triptych.

The paintings, meanwhile, entered the market and museums under the names of Pietro Lorenzetti, the "school of Giotto" (as the Louvre's 1828 inventory had it), or anonymous Sienese masters. The twentieth-century recovery was an achievement of connoisseurship later confirmed by documents. Berenson recognized a personality and gave it a name built from its sources. Others split and renamed it. Meiss reunited it and proposed the documented name. Steinhoff and her colleagues supplied the inventory evidence that turned the proposal into knowledge and then used the artist to rethink a whole period.

What remains is a painter whose historical importance is secure and whose artistic personality, after a century of reconstruction, is coherent. His oeuvre is reliably anchored at only one point. Further technical study of the dispersed polyptychs, a fresh reading of the hospital documents, and the publication of the inventories cited in 1986 are the obvious routes to firming up the rest.

The panel, 73.5 by 41 centimetres in its painted surface, is the Washington part of the polyptych of San Cerbone at Lucca and the earliest work in this catalogue. It shows one of the female saints who flanked the central image, at half length and on a scale close to life.

Saint Catherine is shown at half length, frontally, against a gold ground under a pointed arch. She has long wavy fair hair and a broad, pale face with heavy-lidded eyes, a straight nose and a small mouth. Her large halo is punched with a dense pattern of flowers and rays, and a tall crown of gold, set with red and black stones and pearls, marks her royal birth.

She wears a red mantle patterned with small dark motifs, lined in a darker red and turned back at the shoulders, over a dress of white and blue. At her chest a large round clasp of gold, worked with a quatrefoil, carries an inscription with her name. The clasp, the crown and the halo are executed with the precision of goldsmith's work.

With both hands she holds a closed book with red ties, the sign of her learning, by which she confounded the philosophers sent to convince her. In her left hand she also holds a dark green palm of martyrdom, which rises diagonally across the panel to the level of her face.

In the foreground before her stands a large wheel, the instrument of her intended martyrdom, painted in ochre and yellow and seen at an angle, so that it projects towards the viewer. According to her legend, the spiked wheels on which she was to be torn apart burst asunder, and she was then beheaded.

The figure is broad and heavy, with a large head, a long neck and a simple, massive silhouette. This solidity, derived from Pietro Lorenzetti, together with the decorative brilliance of the crown, the clasp and the punched gold, which recalls Duccio and Simone Martini, characterises Bulgarini's works of the years before 1350.

In the original polyptych Catherine stood beside the central image with other saints, each in a pointed compartment of the same shape. The panel has lost its frame and its upper parts, so that the arch is now defined only by the edge of the gold ground and the punched border.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground is worn at the top, the surface is crossed by a network of cracks, and there are small losses along the edges. The colours of the mantle and the dress, the gold of the crown and the punched decoration of the halo are well preserved.

The small panel is in the Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza in Madrid, which catalogues it under Bulgarini's name. It is dated around 1340–1345, in the years before the Saint Victor altarpiece, and was made for private devotion. Its pointed shape and its single field suggest that it was an independent image or the central part of a small portable altarpiece.

The Virgin sits frontally on a throne before a cloth of honour of red and gold brocade, which fills the field behind her and is held up at the top by two angels in blue with joined hands. She wears a dark blue mantle, edged with a gold border, over a dress of gold, and looks out at the viewer with a still, grave expression. Her halo is punched, and a small gold crown or diadem rests on her mantle.

The Child sits on her lap in a blue tunic over orange sleeves. He holds a small bird, probably a goldfinch, the sign of the future Passion, and turns his head up to look at his mother. The Virgin supports his feet with her left hand.

Two further angels, in dark garments with gold haloes, stand behind the throne at the sides and look towards the centre. The four angels, two above and two at the sides, frame the Virgin in a symmetrical arrangement.

In front of the throne, at the left, stands a young female saint in a blue dress and a red mantle, holding a palm of martyrdom; she has no other attribute that identifies her. At the right stands Saint John the Baptist, gaunt and bearded, in his camel-hair garment and an orange mantle, holding a scroll and pointing towards the Child.

The throne stands on a step decorated with marbled ornament, above a floor painted with veined stone. In the gable, against the gold ground worked with punched borders, is a round gilded boss, perhaps once set with a medallion or a glass.

The broad faces, the solid figures and the rich brocades connect the panel with Bulgarini's works of the 1340s, before the Saint Victor altarpiece, in which a Lorenzettian solidity is combined with decorative refinement. The strong contrast between the dark blue of the mantle and the gold and red of the brocade gives the small image a strong presence.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground and the brocade are well preserved, with fine cracks across the surface. The marbled floor is worn and crossed by a crack, and the dark garments of the angels have lost some of their modelling.

The triptych comes from the church of Sestano, in the Chianti, near Siena, and is now in the Pinacoteca Nazionale. It is placed in the second phase of Bulgarini's career, before 1350, together with the polyptych of San Cerbone at Lucca. The three panels show Saint Peter enthroned at the centre, between Saint Paul and Saint John the Evangelist.

The central panel, taller than the laterals, shows Saint Peter seated frontally on a throne with a high back and a patterned cushion. He has short grey curly hair and a short beard, and looks out at the viewer with a grave, direct gaze. He wears a red tunic and a broad orange mantle, and over his shoulders a pallium embroidered with crosses, the sign of his authority as head of the Church.

On his knee he holds a closed red book with metal clasps, and in his right hand, held against his body, his key. The seated pose, the pallium and the book present Peter as the first bishop of Rome enthroned, rather than as a standing apostle.

At the left Saint Paul, bald and with a long dark beard, wears a blue tunic and a red mantle. He holds the sword of his martyrdom diagonally across his body and a book in his left hand. His heavy head and dark beard follow the traditional type of the apostle.

At the right Saint John the Evangelist is shown as an old man, bald, with a long white beard, in a red mantle over a grey garment. He holds an open book with lines of writing, the Gospel, which he presents to the viewer. The representation of John as an old man refers to him as the author of the Gospel and of the Apocalypse.

The figures are massive and voluminous, with large heads, broad shoulders and heavy drapery that falls in simple folds. Their weight and the depth of the throne show Bulgarini's debt to Pietro Lorenzetti, while the faces, with their long noses and staring eyes, keep a trace of the Ducciesque tradition from which he started.

The church of Sestano, a small rural parish, shows that Bulgarini worked not only for the institutions of the city but also for the churches of its territory. The choice of Peter enthroned as the central figure may reflect the dedication of the church.

The colours of the garments have darkened in places. The gold grounds are worn, and the frames of the three panels are lost, so that the arched shapes are defined only by the edges of the gold.

The panel, 172.4 by 123.2 centimetres, is the central part of the Saint Victor altarpiece and the only work in this catalogue tied to Bulgarini's name by documents. It has been cut at the sides, as the severed hand at the right edge shows, and its original wooden support has been replaced by a modern backing.

The scene is set in a shelter of pale blue architecture, seen frontally and divided into three bays by two thin white columns with Corinthian capitals. The columns correspond to the divisions of a triptych, so that the central panel reproduces, in a single field, the tripartite structure of the other altarpieces of the cycle. A low step runs across the front, and a dark interior opens behind the figures, where the ox and the ass stand at the manger.

The Virgin sits at the centre, slightly turned to the left, wrapped in a dark blue mantle over a red dress with a gold border. She crosses her hands on her breast and lowers her eyes towards the Child, in an attitude of meditation rather than activity. Her halo bears an inscription with the opening words of the angelic salutation. The emphasis on the Virgin rather than on the Child suits a cycle dedicated to the life of Mary.

At the left Saint Joseph sits in an orange mantle, his hand at his chin in the traditional gesture of reflection. Behind him the ox and the ass look over a wooden manger in which the swaddled Child lies, his face turned towards the viewer. In the foreground stand a gold ewer and a basin, the vessels of the first bath. At the right a shepherd in a sheepskin, with a staff, kneels and gazes at the Virgin; above him the raised hand and part of the body of a second shepherd are cut off by the edge of the panel.

At the top of the composition God the Father, in blue, appears in a glory of red and gold seraphim, and a white dove descends from him towards the manger. Together with the Child below, they form an image of the Trinity, an unusual addition to the Nativity that links the birth of Christ to the doctrine of the Incarnation. Two groups of angels with gold wings hover at the sides beneath the roof.

The composition is built on Pietro Lorenzetti's *Birth of the Virgin* of 1342 for the altar of Saint Savinus: the painted columns continue the divisions of the frame, the architecture opens into depth, and the figures are set within a measurable space. At the same time the large, flat areas of colour, the dark blue of the Virgin and the orange of Joseph, and the decorative angels give the painting a two-dimensional order and a refinement closer to the followers of Simone Martini.

The figures are broad and solid, with heavy, simple drapery and faces modelled in green underpaint and pale flesh tones. The Virgin's oval face, with its long nose and small mouth, and the rough, expressive head of the shepherd show the range of Bulgarini's figure types. The gold is limited to the haloes, the angels' wings, the vessels and the borders of the garments, against the cool blues and greys of the architecture.

The panel has suffered losses and repeated restorations. The surface is worn, particularly in the Virgin's mantle and in the architecture, and the green underpaint shows through the flesh tones. The original width of the composition can no longer be judged.

The panel shows Saint Victor, the titular saint of the altar, and stood at one side of the *Adoration of the Shepherds*. It measures 158 by 47.5 centimetres and came to the Statens Museum for Kunst in 1923 as a gift, together with the *Saint Corona*.

The saint stands frontally against a gold ground, beneath a cusped arch, on a strip of ground painted to imitate pink stone. He is shown as a young man with curly fair hair, rosy cheeks and a short beard, and looks out at the viewer with a calm, slightly melancholy expression. His halo is punched with a fine border.

He wears the dress of a young nobleman or knight: a long pink tunic buttoned with small gold buttons and fastened with a gold belt, lined with grey-white fur that shows at the hem; a red cloak with a blue lining thrown over both shoulders; and blue hose. The rich clothing presents Victor, a Roman soldier according to his legend, as a figure of the Sienese aristocracy.

In his right hand he holds an olive branch with dark fruit, rather than the usual palm of martyrdom, and with his left hand he holds the hilt of a sword in a black scabbard decorated with gold, its point resting on the ground. The olive branch, emblem of peace, may allude to the role of the patron saints as protectors of the city.

The figure is tall and slender, with sloping shoulders and long legs, and the drapery of the cloak falls in broad, smooth folds. The face, with small features and green-tinged flesh, is drawn with a fine, continuous outline, and the borders of the tunic and cloak are worked with delicate patterns in gold.

Compared with the *Adoration*, the two laterals have smaller heads, longer proportions, smoother faces and a lighter, more decorative palette. The same differences can be read as the signs of a second hand or as the adjustment of one painter to the different demands of standing saints and of a crowded narrative.

The frame of the panel, with its gable, cusped arch and pilasters, matches that of the *Saint Corona*, and the two panels form a pair.

The panel is in good condition. The colours of the garments are bright, and the gold ground is crossed by cracks and slightly worn. The pink stone of the ground at the bottom is abraded, and there are small losses at the edges.

The panel shows Saint Corona, the companion of Saint Victor in martyrdom, and stood on the other side of the *Adoration of the Shepherds*. It measures 155 by 48 centimetres. Before the reconstruction of the altarpiece it was given to Simone Martini and to Lippo Memmi.

According to her legend, Corona was a young woman who, seeing Victor's constancy under torture, declared herself a Christian and was martyred with him. Her presence beside Victor on his altar completes the story told in the predella, where the *Blinding of Saint Victor* shows one of his tortures.

The saint stands frontally, turned slightly to the left, against a gold ground beneath a cusped arch. She is young, with fair hair bound by a band, a long neck and a small oval face, and looks out at the viewer with a still, grave expression. Her halo is decorated with a punched border.

She wears a dress of gold brocade and a large blue mantle, lined with pink and red and edged with gold borders, which falls in broad, heavy folds from her shoulders to the ground. The contrast between the blue of the mantle, the pink of its lining and the gold of the dress gives the figure a luminous quality.

In her hands she holds a gold crown set with stones and two blue palm fronds. The crown alludes to her name, *Corona*, and to the crown of martyrdom; the palms are the usual sign of the martyrs. The double palm may refer to the joint martyrdom of the two saints.

The figure is elegant and slender, with sloping shoulders and long, falling drapery that gathers in deep folds at the feet. The oval face, the long neck and the fine gold borders of the mantle give her a courtly refinement.

The two Copenhagen saints form a pair, in frames of the same design. Victor turns slightly to the right and Corona slightly to the left, so that both incline towards the central panel that once stood between them.

The panel is in good condition. The blue of the mantle and the gold of the brocade are well preserved, with a fine network of cracks across the surface. There are small losses in the gold ground and at the bottom of the panel, where the ground beneath the saint's feet is worn.

At the right of the cross Saint John the Evangelist, in a pink and blue mantle, stands with his head bowed. Further right a centurion on a white horse raises his arm towards the cross, surrounded by soldiers with spears, a red banner and a group of bearded men in turbans and hoods.">
  <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>The Crucifixion</em>, c. 1350, central panel of the predella of the altarpiece of the altar of Saint Victor, Siena Cathedral. Tempera on gold ground on panel, 43.6 × 77 cm; with frame 61 × 92.4 cm. Paris, Musée du Louvre, INV 312; acquired in 1828 from the collection of Pierre Révoil. © Musée du Louvre, image for non-commercial educational use.</figcaption>

The panel was the central compartment of the predella of the Saint Victor altarpiece, beneath the *Adoration of the Shepherds*. Révoil's catalogue, from which it came to the Louvre, described it as a "belle peinture grecque du XIVe siècle", a sign of how Sienese gold-ground painting was perceived in the early nineteenth century.

The composition is arranged symmetrically around the cross, which rises against the gold ground to the top of the panel. The figures stand on a dark strip of ground, and at the left a dark hill with small trees closes the scene. The gold background, the absence of a landscape and the frieze-like arrangement of the figures suit the narrow format of a predella.

Christ hangs dead on the cross, his head fallen on his shoulder and his body thin and pale, with blood running from the wounds. The cross is set into a small mound of stones. The body is modelled with soft transitions of tone and stands out against the gold.

At the left the holy women form a dense group around the fainting Virgin, who sinks backwards, supported by two women. A young woman with long fair hair, probably Mary Magdalen, turns towards her with outstretched arms. The mantles of blue, pink, yellow and white form a block of colour against the gold. Behind them an old bearded man looks on, and on the hill above soldiers on horseback carry spears.

To the right of the cross Saint John the Evangelist stands alone, his head bowed and his hands clasped beneath his mantle. His isolated figure, between the cross and the crowd at the right, balances the group of women on the other side.

At the right the centurion on a white horse raises his arm towards the cross, the gesture of his recognition of Christ as the Son of God. Around him crowd soldiers with spears and shields, a red banner and a group of bearded men in turbans and hoods, who discuss among themselves. The contrast between the grief of the left side and the agitation of the right side is a traditional feature of Sienese Crucifixions, which Bulgarini renders with lively variety of types and costumes.

The panel shows the qualities of Bulgarini's narrative painting: bright, clear colours, a taste for rich costumes and varied heads, and a firm arrangement of the figures in groups. The fainting Virgin and the centurion on the white horse derive from the Crucifixions of Pietro Lorenzetti and Simone Martini, adapted to the small scale of the predella.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground is worn in places, with the red bole visible beneath, and the edges of the panel show small losses. The colours of the garments are well preserved.

  <img src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-03.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-03-zoom.jpg" alt="Almost square predella panel.   <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>The Blinding of Saint Victor</em>, c. 1351, panel of the predella of the altarpiece of the altar of Saint Victor, Siena Cathedral. The panel belonged to the predella of the Saint Victor altarpiece, where the stories of the saint flanked the central *Crucifixion*. It measures 41 by 39.6 centimetres and is dated by the museum to about 1351. It came to the Städel in 1977 as a bequest of the collector Robert von Hirsch, in memory of the former director of the museum, Georg Swarzenski.

Saint Victor was one of the four patron saints of Siena, a Roman soldier martyred under the emperor Antoninus, according to his legend, together with the young Corona. The episode of his blinding is one of the tortures he suffered before his death. The predella of his altar told his story beneath the *Nativity*, linking the patron saint to the Marian cycle of the cathedral.

The scene takes place before a pink building with pointed arches on thin columns, its upper storey decorated with painted panels and lozenges. The building is set obliquely and opens towards the viewer, and its interior is painted red and gold. At the top, the judge, in a red and white robe, leans over the balustrade of the upper storey and points down to the execution, ordering the torture.

At the centre Saint Victor, haloed, in a blue mantle over a red robe, is held by an executioner in a pale blue tunic, who throws his arm around him and presses his fingers into his eyes. As the museum notes, the painter modelled the composition on the Kiss of Judas, so that the martyrdom of the saint is linked visually to the Passion of Christ. The embrace of the executioner turns an act of violence into a sinister parody of affection.

Two soldiers in brown scaled armour, red and blue hose and blue helmets stand at the left; one holds a club. At the right two soldiers with tall spears, one carrying a large red shield with a blue border, watch the scene. Their heavy boots, armour and weapons are painted with care and give the scene the character of a contemporary event.

At the far right a group of onlookers in yellow, blue and red mantles, among them a woman in a white veil and a man in a turban, turn towards each other and talk, indifferent to the torture. The group, partly cut by the edge of the panel, recalls the crowds of the Lorenzetti and introduces into the scene the reaction of the city.

The bright colours, pink, blue, red, yellow and gold, the solid figures and the clear architectural setting connect the panel with the narrative style of Pietro Lorenzetti, while the finely worked armour and costumes show Bulgarini's taste for decorative detail. The building, set at an angle and opened towards the viewer, creates a convincing space for a crowded scene within a small format.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground is crossed by cracks and worn in places, the edges show small losses, and the surface has a fine network of cracks, but the colours remain bright. The panel has been cut at the right, as the onlookers truncated by the edge suggest.

### Madonna of Humility (Amsterdam, Rijksmuseum)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-13.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-13-zoom.jpg" alt="Small tall panel with a steep pointed gable in its engaged gilded frame. Above, a column of dark blue seraphim descends, and in a quatrefoil at the top of the gable is the bust of Christ in profile.">
  <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>Madonna of Humility</em>, c. 1353, probably one wing of a diptych. Tempera on panel, 57 × 24.5 cm with engaged frame. Amsterdam, Rijksmuseum, SK-A-4002. Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, public domain.</figcaption>
</figure>

The small panel in the Rijksmuseum is a Madonna of Humility, probably one wing of a diptych. It measures 57 by 24.5 centimetres, painting and frame forming a single piece, and the museum dates it to about 1353. Its provenance includes the collection of Adolf von Beckerath in Berlin, where it was sold in 1916 as a work of Bartolo di Fredi, and that of Otto Lanz in Amsterdam. In 1941 it was acquired for the projected Führermuseum at Linz; recovered after the war, it was assigned to the museum in 1960.

The Virgin sits low, on a cushion placed on the ground, before a low wall decorated with rectangular panels. She wears a blue mantle drawn over her head, with a white veil beneath it, over a dress of gold brocade worked in fine sgraffito. Her head is inclined and her eyes are lowered towards the Child, in an attitude of quiet tenderness.

In her left hand she holds an open book inscribed *Deus in adiutorium meum intende; Domine ad adiuvandum me festina*, the opening verse of the canonical hours. The book makes the Virgin a model of devotion for the owner of the diptych, who would have recited the same words in prayer.

With her right arm she embraces the Christ Child, who stands beside her, unusually upright rather than seated on her lap. He wears a short pink tunic with a blue belt, and he grasps her breast with both hands and suckles. The standing, nursing Child gives the scene a naturalism and a sense of movement rare in early Madonnas of Humility.

Above the group, against the gold ground, a column of dark blue seraphim descends from the gable towards the Virgin. In a quatrefoil at the top of the gable the bust of Christ appears in profile, turned to the right, perhaps towards the image in the other wing of the diptych, now lost.

The Madonna of Humility, the Virgin seated on the ground and nursing the Child, had been formulated in Siena and Avignon in the 1340s. This panel shows how Bulgarini adapted the type to a small private image, combining the humility of the seated Virgin with the precious materials of the brocade and the gold.

The low wall behind the Virgin, with its rectangular panels, separates the humble seat on the ground from the gold of heaven above, where the seraphim descend. The wall marks a boundary between the earthly and the celestial zones of the image.

The panel is in good condition for its size and history. The gold ground is worn and crossed by fine cracks, and the frame, integral with the panel, shows losses of gilding. The blue of the mantle has darkened in places, but the brocade, the faces and the seraphim are well preserved.

The panel is the painted cover of the register of income and expenditure of the Biccherna, the chief financial office of the Commune of Siena, for the first half of 1353. It is preserved in the Archivio di Stato di Siena among the series of Biccherna covers. It is the cover generally attributed to Bulgarini on the basis of his payment for Biccherna covers in that year.

The cover is divided into two parts by a dark iron band, which held the board to the spine of the register. The upper part contains the painted scene and the coats of arms; the lower part, of almost equal height, carries the inscription. The division reflects the function of the object: an image of the office above, and the official record of the register below.

At the top, under a gilded vault decorated with foliage, hang four coats of arms, those of officials named in the inscription. Their shields, with fleurs-de-lis, bars, bands and waves in black, white, red and gold, are set in the spandrels of a painted arcade, which frames the room below as a stage.

The scene shows the office of the Biccherna as a plain room with ochre walls and a shelf on the right. Two men sit behind a long counter. At the left the scribe, in a red hood over a dark garment, points with his finger towards his colleague. At the right the *camarlingo*, the treasurer, in a dark blue robe and a red cap, raises one hand in conversation and with the other opens a chest set against the wall. Their faces, in three-quarter view, are individualised and turned towards each other.

On the counter lie the instruments of their work: two books bound with straps, an inkwell with a pen, a sheet of paper and two piles of coins, one dark, probably silver, and one gold. The objects are painted in strong outline and set out in a row along the counter, like a still life of administration.

The inscription, in large black Gothic capitals on a white ground, records that this is the "libro de l'entrata e de l'escita de la Biccherna del Chomune di Siena", from the calends of January 1352 to the calends of July 1353 in Sienese reckoning, that is the first half of 1353 in modern style, and gives the names of the officials of the Biccherna, among them the *camarlingo*, Buonaventura di messer Manfredi.

The simple space, the solid half-length figures and the direct exchange of gestures belong to the tradition of Biccherna covers established by the Lorenzetti generation, in which the officials of the Commune are shown at work. The broad faces and the firm modelling of the two men recall the figures of the Saint Victor altarpiece, completed only a few years before.

The cover is worn but legible. The paint of the scene is abraded, particularly in the counter and the walls, and the gilding of the vault is rubbed. The inscription is well preserved. The iron band and the nails that fixed the cover to the register survive, a reminder that the painting was made to protect and identify an account book.

  <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>The Assumption of the Virgin with Saint Thomas Receiving the Girdle</em>, early 1360s, from Santa Maria della Scala, Siena. Tempera and gold on panel, 205 × 112 cm. Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale, no. 61. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>

The panel, 205 by 112 centimetres, is one of the large altarpieces from the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala in the Pinacoteca Nazionale. It is generally dated to the early 1360s.

The panel is shaped as a tall pointed arch, framed by a moulding and a dentil cornice, with spandrels painted above. The whole field is dominated by gold: the ground, the mandorla, the Virgin's dress and the garments and wings of the angels are all worked in gold, so that the figures are distinguished more by line, punching and tooling than by colour.

The Virgin sits frontally on a cloud at the centre, her hands joined in prayer and her eyes fixed on the viewer. She wears a mantle and dress of gold brocade patterned with small motifs and edged with borders, and her face is framed by a white veil. Behind her rises a cusped mandorla of gold, set with red seraphim, which reaches to the apex of the arch.

Crowds of angels surround her in tiers, filling the lower half of the panel. Dressed in gold, with gold wings, they turn towards the Virgin, pray or play musical instruments, among them a tambourine and a lute. Their heads, set close together and repeated in many variations, form a dense heavenly court that carries the Virgin upwards.

At the bottom centre the apostle Thomas, in a blue tunic and a pink mantle, is seen from behind with his arms raised towards the Virgin, to receive the girdle she lets fall to him. The figure turned away from the viewer is unusual and draws the eye from the earth into the scene, so that Thomas acts as an intermediary between the viewer and the vision.

In the spandrels above the arch, at either side, groups of prophets and saints in coloured garments, some holding scrolls, lean towards the scene. Their bright blues, reds and pinks contrast with the gold of the main field and frame the Assumption with witnesses from the Old and New Testaments.

Thomas's raised arms and the Virgin's position directly above him make clear the moment of the gift of the girdle. The contrast between the single coloured figure of the apostle and the golden heaven above concentrates the meaning of the scene on the relic.

The panel is worn. The gold surface is rubbed and darkened in places, the punched decoration is partly lost, and there are losses in the mantle of the Virgin and in the lower part of the panel. The condition of the painting makes the reading of the angels' faces difficult in places.

The panel was the centre of a small portable triptych whose wings, with the Annunciation and eight saints, are in the John G. Johnson Collection of the Philadelphia Museum of Art. It measures 65 by 46 by 10.5 centimetres and is dated by the museum to 1355–1360. Isabella Stewart Gardner bought it in 1911.

The painting is set in an elaborate tabernacle frame with a steep gable, crockets and a foliate finial, side pinnacles and twisted columns. The frame turns the panel into a miniature architecture: under a cusped arch, whose vault is painted dark blue with gold stars, the throne of the Virgin rises before a gold ground, and narrow side panels with standing saints are set between the columns.

In the gable, in a trefoil, Christ blesses with his right hand and holds an open book inscribed *Ego sum lux mundi; qui sequitur me…*, "I am the light of the world; he that followeth me…" (John 8:12). The image of Christ as light of the world crowns the throne of his mother below.

The Virgin, in a dark blue mantle over a gold-patterned dress, sits on a broad throne covered with a red cloth patterned in gold and inclines her head towards the Child. The Child, in a pink garment, sits on her lap and turns towards her. Five angels with gold haloes look over the back of the throne, their heads arranged in an arc.

Four saints stand on either side of the throne. At the left are Saint Paul, with a sword and a book, and in front of him the Magdalen in a red mantle over blue, holding her ointment jar. At the right are Saint Peter, with the key, and in front of him Saint Catherine of Alexandria, crowned, with her wheel. The female saints, in the front row and closer to the Virgin, are painted in light, bright colours.

In the narrow side panels under the arch, set one above the other, stand four further saints: Saint John the Evangelist and Saint Anthony Abbot at the left, Saint John the Baptist and a bishop saint at the right. The Baptist holds a scroll inscribed *Ecce agnus Dei*, and the names of the Evangelist and the Baptist are inscribed below them in abbreviated Latin.

The throne stands on a step above a floor patterned with lozenges, which projects towards the viewer and gives the small scene a measurable depth. The combination of a coherent space, derived from the Lorenzetti, with the decorative richness of the patterned fabrics and the gold is characteristic of Bulgarini's works of the 1350s.

The panel is in good condition. The gold and the patterned fabrics are well preserved, while the Virgin's dark mantle has lost some of its modelling. When the triptych was complete, the Philadelphia wings were hinged to the sides of the frame and closed over the central image.

The two wings in the John G. Johnson Collection of the Philadelphia Museum of Art belonged to a small portable triptych whose central panel, the *Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints and Angels*, is in the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston. They are now set together in a single frame, measuring 63.8 by 42.9 centimetres, and are dated by the museum, like the Boston panel, to about 1355–1360.

When the triptych was open, the wings flanked the Virgin and Child, and their upper compartments, shaped to fit the gable of the central panel, carried the Annunciation. The two wings are now set side by side, so that the angel and the Virgin face each other across a painted ornament with the crossed keys of Saint Peter.

In the left compartment of the Annunciation the angel Gabriel kneels in a lilac robe over a gold tunic, his wings spread, holding a scroll inscribed with the opening words of the angelic salutation, *Ave gratia plena*. In the right compartment the Virgin sits on a bench in a dark blue mantle, a book open beside her and a vase of lilies before her, and raises her hand to her face in a gesture of reflection and modesty.

Below, each wing contains four standing saints in two rows, each in his or her own arched compartment against gold. On the left wing the apostle Andrew holds his cross and a book; a bishop in mitre and red cope, perhaps Saint Savinus, one of the patron saints of Siena, holds a crozier; Saint Dominic, in the black and white habit of his order, holds a lily and a book; and Saint Francis, in a grey-brown habit, looks up towards a red seraph, the vision of the stigmata.

On the right wing the apostle Bartholomew, in a violet mantle, holds his knife and a red book; Saint Lawrence wears a deacon's dalmatic of blue and gold and holds a book; Saint Lucy holds a dish and a sword; and Saint Agatha, in pink, holds a dish and the pincers of her martyrdom. The selection of saints, with the founders of the two mendicant orders and the virgin martyrs, suggests a private patron with wide devotions.

The figures are small, slender and carefully drawn, with delicate faces and richly patterned garments: the bishop's cope with its embroidered orphreys, the dalmatic of Lawrence and the brocaded dresses of the female saints. The gold ground is punched with fine borders around each compartment. These qualities place the wings among the refined devotional works of Bulgarini's later phase, close in style to the followers of Simone Martini.

When the triptych was closed, the wings covered the central image; when it was opened, the saints of the wings joined those around the Virgin's throne, so that the owner prayed before a large company of saints and angels. The Annunciation above framed the whole as an image of the Incarnation.

The wings are in good condition. The gold ground and the colours of the garments are well preserved, with some wear in the darker areas, such as the Virgin's mantle and Dominic's black cloak. The two wings have been joined in a single frame, so that their original hinged arrangement is no longer visible.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels (Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale)

  <img src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-15.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-15-zoom.jpg" alt="Tall panel with a pointed arch carved with tracery, framed by pilasters with twisted colonnettes.   <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels</em>, c. 1355–1360, probably the central panel of the polyptych from Santa Maria della Scala whose lateral saints are in the Uffizi. Tempera and gold on panel. Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale, no. 76 (?). The panel is in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena, where it is catalogued under Bulgarini's name, as the work formerly known under the name of "Ugolino Lorenzetti". Its subject, size and style correspond to the *Madonna and Child Enthroned* from the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala that is reconstructed as the centre of the polyptych whose lateral saints are in the Uffizi, and it is probably that panel. It is generally dated to about 1355–1360.

The panel is framed by a pointed arch carved with tracery and cusps, supported by pilasters with twisted colonnettes and crowned by a dentil cornice. The carved frame encloses the gold ground and gives the image the form of a Gothic shrine.

The Virgin sits frontally on a large throne of white marble, with a gabled back and side panels inlaid with coloured stone, covered with a cloth of red and gold brocade. She wears a deep blue mantle, edged with a gold border, which covers her body and falls to the floor in broad folds, over a dress striped in white and gold. A gold crown set with stones rests on her head, and her halo is punched with a dense border.

With her right hand raised in a gesture of presentation, she holds the Child on her left knee. The Child wears a gold brocaded tunic with a patterned hem, holds a small scroll in his left hand and raises his right, while looking out at the viewer with a grave expression. His solid body and round face contrast with the slender, elongated figure of the Virgin.

Around the gable of the throne ten angels crowd in two arcs, their heads with golden curls set close together, their hands joined in prayer or crossed on the breast. Two further angels stand at the sides of the throne, in gold garments, their arms crossed. In the spandrels above the arch, smaller angels appear in rows against the gold, so that the whole upper part of the panel is filled with a heavenly court.

The design is built on the contrast between the large dark mass of the Virgin's mantle and the surrounding gold. The mantle, almost without modelling, forms a single block of colour at the centre of the panel, while the throne, the angels and the brocades are worked with fine punching and sgraffito. The effect recalls the great enthroned Madonnas of the first half of the century, adapted to the scale of a polyptych.

The crowned Virgin, the abundance of angels and the precious materials make the panel an image of the Queen of Heaven, suited to the main altar of a hospital chapel. The raised hand of the Virgin, turned towards the Child, directs the viewer from the queen to her son.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground and the punched decoration are well preserved, but the blue of the mantle has lost much of its modelling and is crossed by small losses. The faces of the angels are slightly abraded, and the frame shows losses along its edges.

### Saint Peter and Saint John the Baptist, from a polyptych for Santa Maria della Scala (Florence, Gallerie degli Uffizi)

  <img src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-09.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Bartolomeo-Bulgarini/bartolomeo-bulgarini-09-zoom.jpg" alt="Two tall narrow panels side by side, each with a gold ground under a carved cusped arch, and below each a small compartment with a half-length figure in a quatrefoil. In the right panel Saint John the Baptist, gaunt, with long dark hair and a camel-hair garment under a red mantle tied with a blue belt, points upwards and holds a scroll inscribed ECCE AGNUS DEI; his name is inscribed at his feet. In the quatrefoil below Peter is a haloed bearded apostle with a red book, perhaps James the Great; below John is a bald bearded saint with a book and a sword, Saint Paul.">
  <figcaption>Bartolomeo Bulgarini, <em>Saint Peter</em> and <em>Saint John the Baptist</em>, with half-length saints below, c. 1355–1360, lateral panels of a polyptych from the hospital church of Santa Maria della Scala, Siena. Tempera and gold on panel; <em>Saint Peter</em> 167 × 44 cm, <em>Saint John the Baptist</em> 168 × 43.5 cm. Florence, Gallerie degli Uffizi, inv. 1890 nos. 6136 and 6137. The two panels are the Florentine part of the hospital polyptych whose other sections are in the Pinacoteca Nazionale in Siena. They are now displayed in the Uffizi in the room of the Lorenzetti and Simone Martini.

Each panel is crowned by a carved cusped arch against a gold ground, with foliate ornament in the spandrels and a dentil cornice above. Below the main field, separated by a second cornice, a small compartment contains a half-length saint in a quatrefoil against a dark ground. This lower register, painted on the same plank as the standing saint, gives the polyptych the character of a two-tier structure.

Saint Peter stands at the left, turned slightly to the right, with grey curly hair and a full beard. He wears a broad orange mantle over a pink robe, falling in heavy, simple folds to his bare feet, and holds a blue book against his chest and a large gold key. His name, *S. Petrus*, is inscribed at his feet, and further letters are inscribed on the shaft of the key.

Saint John the Baptist stands at the right, gaunt and dark-skinned, with long dishevelled hair and beard. He wears a camel-hair garment under a red mantle tied with a blue belt, and his thin bare legs and feet stand on a pale ground. With his right hand he points upwards, and with his left he holds a long scroll inscribed *Ecce agnus Dei*, the words with which he announced Christ. His name is inscribed at his feet.

In the quatrefoil beneath Peter an apostle in tunic and pallium holds a red book; the Uffizi suggests, from his features and the colours of his garments, that he may be James the Great; in the quatrefoil beneath John, Saint Paul, bald and bearded, holds a book and the sword of his martyrdom. The half-length figures, smaller in scale and darker in tone, belonged to the lower register of the polyptych, which the Uffizi reconstruction describes as predella-like.

The two saints show Bulgarini's broad, heavy figures, with large heads and simple, solid drapery, in the manner derived from Pietro Lorenzetti. The contrast between the warm orange and pink of Peter and the red and dark brown of John, and between Peter's calm frontality and John's emphatic gesture, gives the pair a strong presence.

The inscriptions at the feet of the saints, *S. Petrus* and the abbreviated name of the Baptist, identify the figures beside their attributes. Such inscriptions are common in Sienese polyptychs of the period and help to reconstruct the original sequence of dispersed panels.

The panels have been restored. The gold ground is worn and scratched in places, and the dark ground of the lower compartments is abraded. The colours of the garments are well preserved, though the Baptist's flesh tones have darkened, and the edges of the planks show losses where the panels were separated from the rest of the altarpiece.

The panel is the central part of the dismembered polyptych whose saints are in Pisa. It came to the Yale University Art Gallery in 1943 with the bequest of Maitland F. Griggs. It measures 69.3 by 60.1 centimetres, and the museum dates it to about 1355–1360. The Virgin, seated on a throne, is shown to the knees.

The Virgin is shown at half length, turned slightly to the right, in a dark blue mantle over a red dress. She holds the Child on her left arm, supports him with her right hand and inclines her head towards him. Her halo is richly punched with a band of circular motifs. Her face, broad and softly modelled, has the heavy features and small mouth of Bulgarini's Virgins.

The Child, in a lilac mantle that leaves his chest and arms bare, turns his face upwards to his mother and pulls at the white edge of her veil. The gesture, playful and affectionate, connects the two figures in a close embrace and belongs to the tradition of the tender Madonnas of the Lorenzetti.

Behind the group hangs a cloth of honour of red brocade patterned with green and gold scrolls, which fills the field behind the throne. The patterned textile, painted with silver and gold, gives the panel a rich, warm surface and shows Bulgarini's attention to decorative detail.

Above, in the lobes of the carved cusped arch, two angels with joined hands look down at the Virgin and Child. At the bottom the seat of the throne, covered with a patterned cloth, is visible at the sides, its corners decorated with small knobs. The arrangement shows that the Virgin was originally enthroned, a monumental image at the centre of a polyptych.

The broad face of the Virgin, the heavy hands and the solid body of the Child show the Lorenzettian side of Bulgarini's art, while the patterned brocade and the punched haloes show his taste for ornament.

The panel is in poor condition. Large areas of paint are lost, particularly in the Virgin's mantle, in the lower part and along the edges, where the bare wood and the preparation show. The faces are abraded, the silver of the brocade has darkened, and the carved arch is damaged. The quality of the surviving parts, the punched haloes, the brocade and the faces of the angels, can still be judged.

The condition of the panel nonetheless allows its technique to be studied: the losses expose the layers of preparation and the punched gold,. The panel thus serves as a document of Bulgarini's working methods as well as of his style.

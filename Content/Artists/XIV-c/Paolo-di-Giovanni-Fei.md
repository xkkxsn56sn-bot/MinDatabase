## Introduction: a painter between memory and renewal

The history of Sienese painting in the decades after the Black Death has long been told as an epilogue. In this telling the great generation of Duccio, Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti brothers had vanished. Their heirs repeated, varied and sometimes coarsened a patrimony of images that the city regarded as part of its civic identity. Paolo di Giovanni Fei sits uncomfortably within that narrative. He was without doubt a painter of memory. His best-known altarpiece, the *Birth of the Virgin* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena, consciously restages Pietro Lorenzetti's *Birth of the Virgin* of 1342. His Washington *Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple* presupposes a lost Lorenzettian fresco on the façade of the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala. Yet the same works, read closely, reveal a painter who reorganised inherited compositions with real inventiveness. He brightened the Sienese palette, multiplied anecdotal observation and opened up interior spaces in ways that anticipate the first generation of the Sienese Quattrocento.

Fei's career is best reconstructed from the documents outwards: first what the archives say, then the works that bear his signature or can be tied to payments, and only then the larger body of paintings assembled around these anchors by twentieth-century connoisseurship. The distinction between document, consensus attribution, hypothesis and speculation matters because Fei's literature is unusually layered. Early twentieth-century catalogues attributed to him a heterogeneous group of pictures, many later reassigned to contemporaries. Dates and biographical details corrected by scholarship decades ago are still widely repeated.

Three modern scholars have shaped the study of Fei. Michael Mallory wrote the fundamental dissertation (Columbia University, 1965), the article "Toward a Chronology for Paolo di Giovanni Fei" (1964) and the monograph *The Sienese Painter Paolo di Giovanni Fei (c. 1345–1411)* (1976). <a href="/scholars.html#meiss-millard">Millard Meiss</a> first recognised Fei's hand in the Washington *Presentation* and framed the historical problem of Sienese painting after 1348. <a href="/scholars.html#brandi-cesare">Cesare Brandi</a>'s studies of 1933 on the distinction between Fei and Francesco di Vannuccio, and his catalogue of the Sienese Pinacoteca, were decisive for separating Fei's personality from those of his contemporaries.

## Historiography and critical fortune

Fei does not appear in the canonical early modern biographies of artists as a personality with a defined body of work. His name survives above all in the local Sienese tradition of church descriptions and antiquarian notes. The principal early source is the list of paintings, sculptures and buildings of Siena compiled in 1625–1626 by Monsignor Fabio Chigi, the future Pope Alexander VII. It was published in the *Bullettino senese di storia patria* in 1939. Chigi recorded a work by Fei on the Mannelli altar in the church of San Maurizio dated 1391, and an altarpiece on the Accarigi altar in San Domenico dated 1387. The eighteenth-century antiquarian G. C. Carli, as reported in the 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* ("nel 1381, anno in cui, secondo G. C. Carli … il F. eseguì una tavola per l'altare della famiglia Mannelli in S. Maurizio"), instead gave 1381 as the date of the Mannelli panel. The discrepancy has never been resolved, and it lies at the root of one of the most persistent errors about the painter, discussed below. In the nineteenth century the publication of Sienese archival documents brought the cathedral payments to light. Above all this meant <a href="/scholars.html#milanesi-gaetano">Gaetano Milanesi</a>'s *Documenti per la storia dell'arte senese* of 1854, together with the later supplements of 1898. These remain the documentary foundation of every subsequent account.

The modern reconstruction of Fei's oeuvre began in the first decade of the twentieth century. From 1903 onwards connoisseurs working in the Anglo-American and Italian traditions attributed to him a growing number of panels. These culminated in the lists published in the early 1930s, in the Thieme–Becker lexicon (1932), in <a href="/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond">Raimond van Marle</a>'s *The Development of the Italian Schools of Painting* (1924) and in <a href="/scholars.html#berenson-bernard">Bernard Berenson</a>'s lists of Italian pictures of the Renaissance (1932, revised posthumously in 1968). The claim that Fei's artistic personality was first elucidated by a single connoisseur is therefore an oversimplification. The early corpus was the product of several overlapping proposals. As the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry of 1996 observed, these proposals produced an artistic profile that was "forse poco convincente". That corpus included pictures now given to Francesco di Vannuccio, such as the *Madonna and Child* in San Domenico in Siena and a small panel in the Wallace Collection. It also included works by Niccolò di Buonaccorso, such as the *Madonna and Child with Saints* in Dresden, and by Bartolo di Fredi, notably the *Coronation of the Virgin* in the Pinacoteca of Siena.

The decisive corrective came in two stages. In 1933 Cesare Brandi published a study devoted precisely to the distinction between Francesco di Vannuccio and Paolo di Giovanni Fei. He also published his catalogue of the Regia Pinacoteca di Siena, which set the Sienese holdings on a sounder critical footing. In 1939 the Sienese archivist <a href="/scholars.html#bacci-peleo">Peleo Bacci</a> gathered and published the documents for Fei's civic and professional life, at pp. 165–206 of a volume devoted to unpublished paintings in Siena and its territory (*Dipinti inediti e sconosciuti di Pietro Lorenzetti, Bernardo Daddi etc. in Siena e nel contado*). That volume is still cited for almost every biographical datum. In 1951, in his book on painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death and in related opinions, Millard Meiss identified Fei's hand in the Washington *Presentation of the Virgin*. The panel had appeared in London in 1928 as a work of Bartolo di Fredi. It entered the Kress Collection under that name, and was still catalogued as Bartolo's in Washington in 1956 and 1959.

The second stage was Michael Mallory's research. His 1965 dissertation, the 1964 *Art Bulletin* article on chronology, a 1966 article on an early Quattrocento *Trinity*, and the 1976 monograph established the documentary basis and a coherent sequence for the mature works. Mallory was the first to recognise that the Washington *Presentation* was identical with the panel documented as painted by Fei for the chapel of San Pietro in Siena Cathedral. He also proposed the attribution of frescoed angels in the Malavolti chapel in San Domenico. A later study of that chapel, "Die Malavolti-Kapelle des Bartolo di Fredi und Paolo di Giovanni Fei in S. Domenico in Siena", published in *Pantheon* 45 (1987): 39–53, confirmed the attribution on documentary grounds and fixed the frescoes to 1397. Subsequent contributions have refined rather than overturned this framework. They include <a href="/scholars.html#torriti-piero">Piero Torriti</a>'s Pinacoteca catalogues of 1977 and 1990, the Siena exhibition *Il gotico a Siena* (1982), the *Kirchen von Siena* volumes, the recovery of a painted *Crucifix with the Mourners in Humility* exhibited in 2005, a 2008 proposal on the Mannelli altarpiece in *Prospettiva* ("Paolo di Giovanni Fei: una proposta per la pala Mannelli", nos. 130–131, pp. 168–182), and the National Gallery of Art's systematic catalogue (2016).

Critical judgement on Fei has swung between two poles. The Treccani *Enciclopedia Italiana* of 1936 called him "mediocre, ma che non fu senza conseguenze su artisti del secolo XV". More recent scholarship has spoken of the Washington *Presentation* as one of the masterpieces of late Trecento Sienese painting. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* goes so far as to describe him as the most renowned and sought-after of the "modern" Sienese painters of the last quarter of the century, "colui che meglio riesce a rivivificare l'arte di Simone Martini". The truth, as so often, lies in the details of individual works rather than in such global verdicts.

## The documentary biography

The bare facts of Fei's life are better documented than those of many contemporaries. The documents, however, concern his civic role far more than his painting. He was the son of Giovanni di Feio, a blacksmith (*fabbro ferraio*). The name by which art history knows him is thus a patronymic chain: Paolo, son of Giovanni, son of Feio. This simple fact disposes of an old suggestion, found in the 1932 *Enciclopedia Italiana* and still echoed in authority files that list the alias "Frederici", that "Fei" might be an abbreviation of "Federici". No document supports that reading, and the documented grandfather's name renders it superfluous.

The earliest secure notice is a document of 1 July 1369. In it "Paulus Iohannis pictor" is named a member of the General Council of the Commune of Siena for the Terzo di Città, for a term of six months. The weight of the office led the 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* to suggest that he must have been over twenty at that date, and hence born in the early 1340s. The National Gallery of Art's biography argues more strictly that Sienese citizens attained their majority at twenty-five, which places his birth before 1344–1345. The conventional bracket "c. 1340/45" reflects that inference. It is not a documented birth date. The National Gallery's own heading gives an even broader "c. 1335/1345". No baptismal or birth record is known.

A widely repeated error concerns his birthplace. Several biographies and authority records state that Fei was born in San Quirico d'Orcia, the town in the Val d'Orcia south of Siena. The documents say nothing of the kind. What they record is that Fei repeatedly represented, as councillor, the *popolo* of San Quirico di Castelvecchio. This was one of the urban parishes of Siena itself, in the Terzo di Città, and he is documented as resident there for the rest of his life. The Metropolitan Museum's label formula, "San Quirico, active by 1369–died 1411", is accurate as far as it goes. It is easily misread, and the leap to San Quirico d'Orcia appears to be a conflation of an urban parish with the better-known town of the same dedication. No document places his birth outside Siena, and the San Quirico d'Orcia claim is an error.

From 1369 onwards Fei's civic career was continuous. He served again as councillor in the following two years. He was *vessillifero* (standard-bearer) of the Società d'armi of San Quirico in 1371–1372 and again in 1381. At the start of the July–December semester of 1380 he was named one of the four *Provveditori di Biccherna*, the officials of the city's principal financial magistracy. Between 1386 and 1408 he repeatedly held the office of *camerlengo* of the Compagnia della Madonna sotto le Volte of the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala. This lay confraternity was attached to the great civic hospital opposite the cathedral, and his association with it may help explain his later work for the hospital complex. These offices are the backbone of the 1939 documentary publication, and they show a painter fully integrated into the political life of the Commune. That integration was neither unusual nor trivial in a city whose regimes rested on broad participation by the middling *popolo*.

Only in 1389 does Fei appear in the *Ruolo dei pittori*, the roll of painters enrolled under the *Breve dell'Arte dei Pittori* of Siena. The date has sometimes been taken as the start of his independent career. That is plainly wrong, since he was described as *pictor* twenty years earlier. The surviving roll is a list compiled at a particular moment rather than a cumulative register of first admissions, so 1389 is best read as a *terminus ante quem* for his membership.

The *lira* (tax assessment) of 1392 records him in the district of San Quirico di Castelvecchio together with his wife, Bartolomea di Francesco Memmi. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* infers that by this date he lived independently within his father's district. He remained there until 1411, when his assets were reassessed. The surname of his wife inevitably invites speculation about kinship with the painter-family of Lippo Memmi. No document establishes any such connection.

The end of his life is unusually well fixed. On 1 June 1411 Fei made his will, naming as heirs his wife and seven children. Sources differ slightly on how precisely his death is bracketed. The National Gallery of Art states that he was dead by October 1411. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* places his death between June and December of that year. Both rest on the 1939 documentary publication, and the discrepancy concerns only the *terminus ante quem* recorded in different documents. The formula "died 1411" is therefore secure. An older statement in the 1932 *Enciclopedia Italiana*, that notices of him run "fra il 1372 e il 1410", is superseded at both ends.

## Siena in the second half of the Trecento: the post-plague artistic context

Fei's career unfolded in a city transformed by catastrophe and political instability. The plague of 1348 killed, it is generally assumed, Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti. Simone Martini had already died at Avignon in 1344. The oligarchic regime of the Nove, under whose patronage the great civic commissions of the first half of the century had been realised, fell in 1355. It was followed by a succession of governments, the Dodici and then the Riformatori, and by recurrent factional upheaval. At the very end of Fei's life, between 1399 and 1404, Siena placed itself under the lordship of Gian Galeazzo Visconti, duke of Milan. A Biccherna cover of 1402 associated with Fei's workshop belongs to precisely those years. In 1407–1408 the presence in Siena of Pope Gregory XII and his court brought cardinals and curial patrons into the city. A significant late commission of Fei's career derives directly from that sojourn.

Millard Meiss's thesis, in his 1951 study of painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death, was that the trauma of 1348 and the social changes that followed favoured a retrospective, hieratic and religiously intense art. It turned away from the naturalism of the Lorenzetti towards older, more dogmatic modes. The thesis has been much debated, and it suits Florence better than Siena. Yet it remains a useful frame for Fei. Much Sienese painting after 1348 shows a deliberate re-use of the great models of the first half of the century. This happened not out of mere lack of invention but because those images had acquired quasi-civic and devotional authority. The cathedral chapters and confraternities that commissioned altarpieces were sometimes explicit about this. The National Gallery's catalogue recalls that half a century after Fei, Sano di Pietro would be contractually obliged to reproduce a Lorenzettian composition of the *Presentation of the Virgin* in his own panel. Whether Fei was under a comparable obligation in 1398 is not known. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* characterises the period with a phrase worth quoting: Fei was active "in un periodo in cui gli artisti locali erano per lo più semplici emulatori dell'arte di Simone Martini e di Pietro e Ambrogio Lorenzetti". His distinction lies in the degree to which he transformed that emulation.

The generation among whom Fei worked was crowded. Bartolo di Fredi, his elder, and Andrea Vanni dominated the 1360s and 1370s, together with Luca di Tommè, Francesco di Vannuccio and Niccolò di Buonaccorso. Taddeo di Bartolo, younger than Fei, would emerge in the 1390s as the dominant painter of the next phase, with commissions across Tuscany, Liguria and Umbria. Fei's position as a "conservative" painter is to be measured against this backdrop. He was conservative in his allegiance to the Sienese models of the early Trecento, but not in his handling of space, colour and narrative incident.

## Formation and the question of his teacher

No document records where or with whom Fei trained. Every statement about his master is therefore a hypothesis based on stylistic affinity, and the hypotheses have shifted considerably. The oldest made him a pupil of Andrea Vanni. The idea arose because Fei was long confused with Francesco di Vannuccio, Vanni's associate. Brandi's 1933 study separated Fei's personality from that of Francesco di Vannuccio. Once that was done, the Vanni hypothesis lost its basis, and it plays no role in current scholarship.

The hypothesis that now carries most weight connects Fei with Bartolo di Fredi. It rests on documented collaboration in 1397 in the Malavolti chapel of San Domenico, where Bartolo painted the altarpiece and Fei frescoed the crowning choir of angels. It also rests on the formal affinities that Mallory recognised between those angels and the angels of Bartolo's *Coronation of the Virgin* of 1388 in the Pinacoteca. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry of 1995 states that it "si ritiene" that Fei had worked earlier in Bartolo's workshop. The Pinacoteca Nazionale's current catalogue entry for the *Birth of the Virgin* goes further and states flatly that Fei "fu allievo di Bartolo di Fredi". That formulation turns a well-founded hypothesis into a fact. The National Gallery of Art's biography keeps open the alternative that Fei trained with Francesco di Vannuccio, "with whom his earliest works also reveal affinities". It also records, in its second note, the opinion communicated privately by an American curator ("kind personal communication") that Naddo Ceccarelli, a follower of Simone Martini, "must have been Paolo's master". That last suggestion has not been argued at length in print and remains speculation.

What can be said with confidence is that Fei's formative culture was Sienese and retrospective. His early half-length Madonnas presuppose Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi. His narrative compositions presuppose Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti. Throughout his career he drew on Bartolo di Fredi's lively surfaces and crowded narration. The first two decades of his activity, from 1369 to about 1387, remain obscure in the strict sense: no surviving painting can be dated securely within them. The National Gallery's 2016 catalogue states this plainly, noting that "the earliest of Paolo's securely datable paintings dates to 1387, and hence the first two decades of his career still remain obscure". Any reconstruction of his early style is therefore inferential.

## The signed and documented works: establishing the anchors

The foundation of any responsible account of Fei is the very small group of works whose authorship rests on something other than style. It is smaller than often supposed. The statements that there are signed and dated works from 1381 onwards, or that his earliest signed and dated work is of 1381, are false. The 1381 date belongs to a lost panel known only through antiquarian sources, and it conflicts with the 1391 date recorded for the same work by Chigi in 1625–1626. No surviving painting by Fei bears that date.

The one surviving signed work is a polyptych of the *Madonna and Child Enthroned with Saints* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena, generally cited as no. 300. It came from the church of Sant'Andrea, familiarly Sant'Andreino, at Serre di Rapolano. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* records the signature in the form "Paulus Iovanne". On the polyptych in the Pinacoteca with the saints John the Baptist, Andrew, Francis and the prophet Daniel, a band of lettering on the base of the central panel, now illegible in photographs, may preserve it. The same source describes the polyptych as rather conventional and hard to date, partly because of the damage it has suffered. The National Gallery's catalogue places it in the early 1390s. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* places it shortly before the *Presentation* of 1398, and finds in it a precise recollection of Pietro Lorenzetti in the solemn composure of the figures. The presence of Saint Andrew suits a provenance from Sant'Andreino, which makes the identification of that polyptych with no. 300 probable. Whatever its aesthetic standing, this polyptych is the touchstone for the attribution of every other picture, and it is a salutary reminder that the attributional edifice rests on one signed and partly damaged altarpiece.

The second anchor is documentary. Payments by the Opera del Duomo to Paolo di Giovanni Fei "per la tavola di sancto Piero et sancto Pavolo, per sua fatiga e colori" were made between 1398 and April 1399. The work was destined for the chapel of San Pietro in Siena Cathedral. Cathedral inventories describe it as a "tauola dipenta colla Ripresentationi al Tempio di Nostra Donna et di sancto Pietro et di sancto Pauolo et di più altri sancti e sancte". Mallory recognised this altarpiece in the central panel now in the National Gallery of Art in Washington. The identification is universally accepted, and it makes the *Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple* (Samuel H. Kress Collection, 1961.9.4) the only surviving painting by Fei that can be matched with a payment record. Neither the National Gallery's catalogue nor the Treccani entries gives the sum paid.

Around these two anchors lies a penumbra of works whose dates are documented but whose surviving remains are fragmentary or whose attribution is debated. Chigi's 1625–1626 list records a work by Fei dated 1387 in the Accarigi chapel of San Domenico, originally dedicated to Saint Thomas Aquinas<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-thomas-aquinas" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, without specifying its nature. Brandi proposed identifying it with the panel of the *Madonna and Child* still in situ. Other scholars, however, attribute that panel to Francesco di Vannuccio, and the identification remains controversial. Mallory suggested instead that the altar may have carried a panel of *Saint Thomas Aquinas in his Study*. He also recognised Fei's hand in the nearly illegible fresco remains in the intrados of the chapel arch, among them a bishop's head inscribed in an octagonal frame, which he identified with Saint Thomas. The National Gallery's catalogue dates these fragments to 1387, which accounts for its statement that Fei's earliest securely datable painting belongs to that year. The date is documentary, but the attribution of the surviving fragments rests on stylistic judgement applied to very damaged material.

The frescoed choir of angels in the Malavolti chapel in San Domenico has a firmer basis. Mallory proposed it on stylistic grounds, and a later documentary study confirmed that Bartolo di Fredi painted the chapel's altarpiece in 1397. That altarpiece showed the *Visitation*, Saints Christopher and Dominic, and the *Trinity* in the lunette above, and is now dismembered in the Musée des Beaux-Arts at Chambéry. Fei's angels, painted above the altar beneath the arch, thus acquire a precise date. Finally, the Opera della Metropolitana announced in 2022 that the removal of a seventeenth-century altarpiece had revealed a fragment of fresco with busts of haloed female saints. The altar in question was formerly dedicated to Saint Anthony Abbot. The Opera connected the fragment with a cathedral payment of 6 April 1400, by which Fei received fifteen gold florins "per chagione di cierto lavorio che fecie a la cappella di sant'Antonio in duomo, cioè, di dipintura, per oro e azurro, e ogni altra sua spesa". The document is verbatim and secure. The attribution of the surviving fragment to Fei is stylistic, though strongly supported by the coincidence of place and date.

## The *Birth of the Virgin* (Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale)

The large triptych of the *Birth of the Virgin* is Fei's best-known painting and the centrepiece of his reputation. The Pinacoteca records it as tempera on panel, 259 × 204 cm, inventory no. 116. It came from the suppressed church of San Maurizio in Siena. The central scene is flanked by Saints James and Catherine of Alexandria on one side and Saints Bartholomew and Elizabeth of Hungary on the other. It is unsigned and undated, and the attribution rests on style. No serious modern voice disputes it.

Its relationship to Pietro Lorenzetti's *Birth of the Virgin* is explicit and has always been recognised. Pietro's panel was signed and dated 1342 and painted for the altar of San Savino in Siena Cathedral; it is now in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. It was one of a cycle of four Marian altarpieces for the cathedral's principal altars, and its central innovation was to treat the tripartite frame as if it were the architecture of the room. The painted space continues behind the frame's colonnettes, and a tiled floor unifies the three fields. The Pinacoteca's catalogue describes Fei's dependence precisely: "Da Pietro deriva l'idea di utilizzare il formato tripartito del supporto per creare uno spazio articolato, unificato dalla pavimentazione a piastrelle, con un gioco fra l'architettura della cornice e quella dipinta." Fei, however, did not simply copy. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* notes that he reformulated Pietro's spatial conception by compressing the architectural structure into a narrower space. This shifts the perspectival scheme upwards. He also introduced a double window opening onto a flowering garden, an index of his particular attention to a pleasant, worldly setting. The episode is played by a dense group of figures and enriched with minute observations of furnishings and dress, rendered in what the same source calls "scintillanti toni pastello". The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* attributes this pastel brightness to the example of Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi rather than of Pietro. The triptych thus fuses the two great currents of the early Sienese Trecento: Lorenzettian spatial construction and Martinian chromatic refinement.

Within the scene Fei displays a taste for domestic incident that the National Gallery's catalogue calls "rich in observations taken from daily life". This includes a motif that would return in the Washington *Presentation*: a kneeling figure seen from the back and half-hidden by a pillar, as spontaneous as a snapshot. Such details are what distinguish Fei from the simple emulators of the Lorenzetti. He accepted the authoritative composition as a framework and filled it with a narrative density that belongs to the late Gothic.

The dating of the triptych is often confused. It is frequently labelled "1381" as if that were documented. It is not. The 1381 date belongs to the lost Mannelli panel of San Maurizio as recorded in the eighteenth-century antiquarian tradition, and Chigi's earlier list gives 1391 for the same work. The triptych can carry either date only if it is identical with the Mannelli altarpiece. Some scholars have argued for that identification on the strength of its San Maurizio provenance, and others have rejected it. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* reports the identification as held "da alcuni", and in another passage describes the Mannelli panel as "certamente perduta" with the eighteenth-century rebuilding of the church, an internal inconsistency that well illustrates the state of the question. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry of 1995 states that the triptych is "forse" the Mannelli altarpiece, and that its date "rimane incerta tra il 1381 e il 1391". The National Gallery of Art's 2016 biography treats the San Maurizio panel "dated 1381 or 1391" as lost, and dates the *Birth of the Virgin* on stylistic grounds to "the early or mid-1380s". The photographic archive of the Fondazione Zeri, following the 1977 Pinacoteca catalogue, records a chronological bracket of 1385–1390. A 2008 article in *Prospettiva* (nos. 130–131, pp. 168–182), "Paolo di Giovanni Fei: una proposta per la pala Mannelli (con una nota sull'iconografia di San Maurizio a Siena)", advanced a new proposal concerning the Mannelli altarpiece. The state of the question can be summarised as follows. The triptych is an unsigned, undated work of the 1380s, probably from San Maurizio. Its identification with the Mannelli altarpiece is a hypothesis, and the dates 1381 and 1391 attach to that hypothesis, not to the painting itself.

## The Washington *Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple* and the work for Siena Cathedral

If the *Birth of the Virgin* demonstrates Fei's capacity to renew an inherited model, the *Presentation of the Virgin* (Washington, National Gallery of Art, 146.1 × 140.3 cm painted surface) shows him at the height of his maturity. It is also his only surviving picture documented by payment. Its history as an object is instructive. The panel remained on the altar of the chapel of San Pietro at least until 1482. It was probably removed between 1580, when the Congrega di San Pietro commissioned a new marble altar, and 1582, when that altar was completed. Its whereabouts between 1582 and 1928 are unknown. A handwritten note on a photograph in the Berenson library at Villa I Tatti suggests that it may have passed through the eighteenth-century collection at Corsham Court in Wiltshire. That is a hypothesis recorded in the National Gallery's provenance notes, not an established fact. It reappeared at the Olympia exhibition in London in 1928 as a work of Bartolo di Fredi, was with Wildenstein by 1950, and was sold to the Kress Foundation in 1954, still under Bartolo's name. That same year it was transferred from its original wooden support to hardboard and cradled. It was treated again between 1996 and 1998 to remove discoloured varnish and retouching.

The attribution history is a textbook case of documentary verification of a connoisseur's eye. Meiss identified Fei's hand in 1951, against the then-dominant attribution to Bartolo di Fredi. Mallory later found the documents of 1398–1399 that confirm the attribution and reveal the panel as the centre of a larger complex. According to the scheme prescribed for the cathedral's chapel altarpieces, a central Marian feast would have been flanked by full-length standing saints, including Peter, the titular of the altar, and Paul. Above would have been gables with half-length saints. None of these lateral or upper elements has been identified. The cathedral inventory's reference to "più altri sancti e sancte" confirms that the complex once contained additional figures.

One documentary complication remains open. A payment of 1393 to Bartolo di Fredi "per la tavola d'altare di San Pietro che fa" has led some scholars to suppose that Bartolo began the altarpiece and that the commission was later transferred to Fei. Others have proposed that two panels of Saints Peter and Paul by Bartolo were the laterals of Fei's *Presentation*, but that hypothesis has since been shown to be mistaken. As the National Gallery's catalogue concludes, "the problem of the collaboration between the two Sienese painters still remains open". The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry on Bartolo di Fredi records, moreover, that Bartolo had already painted a panel for the Compagnia di San Pietro in 1381–1383. Distinguishing between successive commissions for the same dedication requires caution.

Pictorially, the *Presentation* is Fei's most complex interior. Mallory's judgement is quoted in the National Gallery's catalogue: it "is the most ambitious and successful Sienese painting of the late Trecento as regards interior setting". He observed that its sequence of spaces is "more complex than anything that he attempted in the Nativity or in the Visitation". The latter is Fei's frescoed lunette in San Francesco in Siena. He added that the architecture is rendered "accurately enough so that we can understand the plan of this church with its polygonal apse and vaulted chapels". Fei almost certainly followed an illustrious prototype. This was the scene painted by Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti in 1335 as part of the Marian cycle on the façade of the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala, which is now lost. Its composition can be inferred from its many derivatives, among them Sano di Pietro's later version in the Pinacoteca Vaticana. That model showed Mary at the top of a flight of steps within a frontal architectural structure, received by priests, looking back towards her mother. The group of girls who would be her companions in the Temple stood to one side. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* likewise links Fei's iconography to "un affresco perduto dei Lorenzetti sulla facciata dello Spedale di S. Maria della Scala".

Fei departed from this tradition in one decisive respect, and the National Gallery's catalogue rightly emphasises it. Following Ambrogio Lorenzetti's *Presentation of Christ in the Temple*, painted for the altar of San Crescenzio in the cathedral and now in the Uffizi, he represented the Temple not as a centrally planned building but as "an imposing Gothic church with a nave and two aisles". Equally original is his colour. In place of the predominantly dark tones of his predecessors, enlivened by splashes of deep red, he adopted "an altogether brighter, sunnier palette". Salmon pink, light blue and sage green predominate, and cinnabar is used sparingly. Small inventions animate the scene. Two foreshortened heads of young women crane their necks at either side of the background. Two children in the foreground stand silhouetted against the light-flooded steps as a kind of *repoussoir*. The kneeling woman seen from behind returns from the *Birth of the Virgin*. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* even detects elements derived from the Florentine artistic environment, absorbed into an "idioma ormai del tutto autonomo".

Technical examination confirms the refinement of the execution. The gilded areas were tooled with twelve different punches. Brocades were created by sgraffito, and incised lines guided the placement of figures and architecture. Infrared reflectography reveals freely sketched underdrawing, and the flesh was laid over green underpaint in the traditional manner. When the panel was stripped of its frame, the two wooden colonnettes that once divided the field into three were lost. In the process, the painted columns they partly covered were damaged.

The *Presentation* was one item among many in Fei's work for the cathedral. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* counts sixteen documents between 1395 and 1410, published in the 1976 monograph, recording his engagement with the decoration of the choir, several chapels and the sacristy, as well as lesser tasks. In 1395 and 1398 he was paid for painting two wooden statues for the choir, representing Saint Paul and Saint Ansanus, both now lost. In 1400 he received five gold florins for colouring a statue of Saint Savinus carved by Fra Guido di Giovanni. That statue has been recognised in a sculpture now in the Museo dell'Opera, an identification proposed in 1939 and maintained in the 1979 guide to the cathedral. In 1402–1403 he painted *Stories from the Old Testament* in part of the choir, completing frescoes allotted to Taddeo di Bartolo in 1401. These were covered between 1608 and 1611 by Ventura Salimbeni's paintings. In 1403 he gilded the she-wolf to be set on the column in front of the cathedral, now lost. In 1407–1408 he frescoed a *Crucifixion* for the chapel of the Passion. In 1410 he painted an altarpiece of the *Madonna and the Four Crowned Martyrs* for the chapel of the Maestri della Pietra, also known as the chapel of San Daniele. Both of the last two are lost. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* adds the decoration of the cover of the Commune's *Porto d'armi* register of 1395. The record is that of a trusted painter on whom the Opera called repeatedly for prestigious commissions and routine finishing work alike. Most of it, as so often with medieval cathedral decoration, has perished.

## The *Assumption of the Virgin* (Washington, National Gallery of Art)

The small *Assumption of the Virgin with Busts of the Archangel Gabriel and the Virgin of the Annunciation* is in the National Gallery of Art in Washington (Samuel H. Kress Collection, 1961.9.71). Its location sometimes causes confusion, so it is worth stating plainly that the panel is in Washington, not in the Yale University Art Gallery or in Siena. Since its introduction to the literature in 1904 its attribution has never been doubted. Its date, by contrast, has moved substantially. Older data still in circulation give "1385" or "probably c. 1385". The National Gallery's own concordance of titles and dates records that the older catalogue's "probably c. 1385" has been revised to "c. 1400/1405". That revision follows the stylistic analysis of the 2016 systematic catalogue and, before it, Mallory's argument that the *Assumption* is later than the *Presentation* of 1398–1399. Citing the 1385 date today is an error.

The reasons for the later date are compelling. The Virgin is not enclosed, as usual, in a mandorla of light or seraphim. She sits on a throne of clouds, "whose step has the consistency of cotton-wool", supported by two fluttering angels and surrounded only by rays incised in the gold. Below, the apostles are not compacted into a single block, as in Taddeo di Bartolo's Montepulciano polyptych of 1401. They are freely distributed around a sarcophagus that blooms with flowers in place of the Virgin's body. One youth thrusts himself between two companions, leaning eagerly on Saint Bartholomew's shoulder and on the edge of the tomb, his face sharply foreshortened. Saint Thomas kneels apart on a rocky outcrop representing the Mount of Olives, his head bent back to receive the girdle. That relic is venerated at Prato. The foreshortened sides of the sarcophagus measure the depth of the space and lead the eye back to him. The National Gallery's catalogue places the panel between the *Presentation* and the Naples triptych of 1407–1408. Mallory had proposed placing it in the very last years of Fei's life. The catalogue finds that proposal questionable, because the Naples figures show a further step towards International Gothic elongation and pathos that the *Assumption* has not yet taken. The two positions differ by only a few years, and the bracket c. 1400–1408 is secure.

The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* traces the iconography to a Martinian prototype known through a later workshop version in the Alte Pinakothek in Munich, in which the Virgin is encircled by a ring of angels. It calls the Washington panel "tra le espressioni più alte della sua arte". The Annunciation medallions in the spandrels suggest that the panel was conceived as a self-sufficient devotional image rather than part of a polyptych. The engaged frame, the pastiglia arcade on the inner side of the arch, the punched borders and the sgraffito brocades all survive, and the paint surface is well preserved apart from scratches in the lower group. The panel was cradled in 1948 and cleaned in 1949.

## Madonnas and devotional panels

A large part of Fei's attributed catalogue consists of images of the Virgin and Child, independent or within small portable altarpieces. None is signed or documented, and their chronology depends entirely on stylistic sequence. The National Gallery's 2016 biography summarises the consensus. Art historians "have agreed in ascribing to his initial phase" two half-length *Madonna and Child* panels, one formerly in the J. Paul Getty Museum and the other in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, together with the *Madonna and Child Enthroned with Saints* in the High Museum of Art in Atlanta. In these works "the rather stereotyped rigidity of poses and the repetitiveness of the facial masks reveal some uncertainty, characteristic of the production of artists at the start of their careers".

The Metropolitan Museum's *Madonna and Child* (Bequest of George Blumenthal, 1941, acc. no. 41.190.13) is the best known of these. The museum describes it as "among the masterpieces of fourteenth-century Sienese painting" and dates it to the 1370s. It measures 87 × 59.1 cm overall with its engaged frame, and 68.6 × 42.9 cm for the painted surface. It shows the Virgin nursing the Child, a *Madonna del Latte*, in a frontal pose that the museum notes "seems to harken back to an earlier period". The picture is exceptional for the survival of its original engaged frame, richly decorated with gilded pastiglia, small cabochon stones and medallions of *verre églomisé*: gold-backed glass painted with the Annunciation, saints, and the head of Christ on the Virgin's brooch. The Metropolitan's text observes that the range of technical skill displayed "may indicate collaboration with another artist". This is a hypothesis worth noting, since the alternative view that Fei himself executed the glass medallions has also been advanced. Mallory's 1964 article already classed the picture as an early work, and the 1976 monograph treats it at length. The "1370s" date is a stylistic inference: no document attaches to the panel.

The second early half-length panel requires correction in several standard references. The *Madonna of Humility* that the 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* and the 1995 *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* cite as in "Londra, collezione Wildenstein" is the same picture as the "Getty Madonna" of the National Gallery's 2016 biography. According to the Fondazione Zeri's photographic archive, this tempera on panel of 70 × 42.7 cm passed from Wildenstein & Co. to the J. Paul Getty Museum (inv. 69.PB.8), where it remained from 1969 until 1992. It was then sold at Christie's, New York, on 21 May 1992 (lot 20), and is now recorded in the Chimei Museum at Tainan, Taiwan. The three apparently distinct references thus describe one object at different stages of its provenance. To say that the picture is "in the Getty Museum" is outdated.

Among the Madonnas of Fei's middle years, the *Madonna of Humility* now set within the Piccolomini altar in Siena Cathedral occupies a special position. The altar is a marble structure built for Cardinal Francesco Todeschini Piccolomini by Andrea Bregno and his workshop between 1481 and 1485, and later adorned with statues by Michelangelo. Bregno's workshop was to frame Fei's late fourteenth-century painting at the centre of the new altar. The incorporation of a picture already a century old into a prestigious Renaissance altar in the cathedral says much about the devotional authority that Fei's images retained. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* dates the painting to the early 1390s and describes it as a re-proposal of the Martinian iconography of the *Madonna dell'Umiltà*. Other sources give "c. 1390" or "c. 1385", and describe it variously as a Madonna of Humility or a nursing Madonna (*Madonna che allatta*). The two descriptions are not mutually exclusive, since Madonnas of Humility frequently suckle the Child. The date remains a stylistic estimate. A separate *Madonna del Latte* by Fei is in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. It should not be confused with the Piccolomini picture, nor with the panel of the same subject in New York.

The 1995 *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* assigns to the years 1390–1395 a group in which Fei reached "un raggiunto equilibrio formale nel grande come nel piccolo formato". The group includes the *Madonna of Humility* in the Museo dell'Opera, the *Madonna and Child with Angels* in Santa Maria della Scala, the diptych from the Osservanza in the Pinacoteca, and the *Maestà with Eve* in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum. The last of these (acc. no. 1975.1.23) is a small portable altarpiece dated by the museum to the 1390s. It shows the Madonna enthroned among Saints John the Evangelist, Peter, Agnes, Catherine of Alexandria, Lucy, an unidentified female saint, Paul and John the Baptist, with Eve and the serpent below and the Annunciation above. Its overall dimensions with engaged frame are given as 87 × 59.1 cm, identical to those of the Blumenthal *Madonna*. The museum's records confirm the coincidence of the overall dimensions; the painted surfaces differ slightly (70.8 × 43.8 cm against 68.6 × 42.9 cm). The *Eve* motif sets the Virgin as the new Eve redeeming the fall of the first, an iconography with a distinguished Sienese pedigree in the circle of Ambrogio Lorenzetti. The Metropolitan also owns a portable diptych with the *Madonna and Child Enthroned with Two Angels and Saints* and the *Crucifixion*, which it attributes to Fei's workshop and dates c. 1400–1410. It is an important witness to the production of devotional objects in series by his shop.

The *Madonna and Child with Angels* in the chapel of the Manto at Santa Maria della Scala is described in the 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* as datable no later than the end of the 1390s. In it, Martinian echoes in the elongated face and sinuous drapery coexist with a growing "umanizzazione dei personaggi". Fei's long service as *camerlengo* of the hospital's confraternity makes his presence in the hospital's chapels historically plausible, though no document links him to this particular panel. The National Gallery's 2016 biography also mentions a polyptych and a painted crucifix executed for the hospital complex in the mid-1380s. It adds that the latter was "unfortunately the victim of a restorer's excessive zeal".

Two other groups complete the picture of Fei's devotional production. The first is the series of dispersed polyptychs and saints. A *Saint John the Baptist* in the Musée du Petit Palais at Avignon and a *Saint Catherine of Alexandria* in the Städel at Frankfurt have been associated with a single lost polyptych of the early 1380s. A *Saint John the Baptist* in the Pinacoteca Stuard at Parma and a *Saint Lawrence* on the art market have been associated with another, of the late 1390s, together with a small predella panel in the Fogg Art Museum at Cambridge, Massachusetts, showing the Baptist leaving the city and preaching. The second group is the polyptych of the oratory of San Bernardino in Siena, a *Maestà* with kneeling saints. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* dates it between 1385 and 1395. Here Fei divided into a polyptych a composition that Duccio and Simone had resolved in a single field, and the influence of Bartolo di Fredi's Perugia triptych is perceptible. Further attributions have been disputed. They include the triptych of the *Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine* and the lateral saints *James, John the Baptist and Vitalis* in the Pinacoteca, which Mallory considered workshop productions while the 1977 Pinacoteca catalogue restored them to the master. They also include a refined *Trinity* now in the Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria at Perugia, close to the Naples triptych. The *Madonna Enthroned* in the National Museum of Serbia at Belgrade, and the Madonna and Child with Saints Francis and Louis of Toulouse in Atlanta, belong to the same broad consensus catalogue, though their precise dates vary in the literature.

## The Naples triptych and the late works

The most securely contextualised work of Fei's last years is a small portable triptych with the *Crucifixion* in the Capece Minutolo chapel of Naples Cathedral. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* records that Fei painted it in 1407–1408 for Cardinal Enrico Minutolo. The cardinal was present in Siena between September and January of those years in the following of Gregory XII, and he carried the altarpiece to Naples. The DBI entry on Giovanni da Gaeta states that it was "eseguito a Siena tra il 1407 e il 1408 e subito portato a Napoli dal cardinale E. Minutolo, per essere collocato nella propria cappella del duomo". Its central panel fuses the Crucifixion with the Trinity in an unusual way. Christ crucified between the Virgin and Saint John is enclosed in a mandorla that includes God the Father and the Holy Spirit. The DBI calls this formulation "originale invenzione del F.", perhaps partly inspired by Luca di Tommè's panel of 1366 in Pisa. Mallory's 1966 article, "An Early Quattrocento Trinity", is devoted to it.

Two points about the triptych require caution in print. The first is its location. The 1996 *Dizionario Biografico* places it on the altar of the Minutolo chapel. The 1995 *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* records it in the storerooms of the Museo e Gallerie Nazionali di Capodimonte. Recent material from Naples Cathedral illustrates the triptych in the restored Minutolo chapel, where it appears to have been displayed again in recent years. The second is the identity of the saints in the lateral panels. The *Dizionario Biografico* names them as Pilgrim, Catherine, John the Baptist and Januarius (Gennaro). Other descriptions name Saints John the Baptist, Louis of Toulouse, Pilgrim and Anastasia. Saint Anastasia was a co-titular of the Minutolo chapel. The two lists cannot be reconciled from the published descriptions.

Stylistically, the Naples triptych marks the furthest point of Fei's development. The National Gallery's catalogue points to the very elongated proportions of the figures, the accentuated *hanchement* of their poses, the multiplication of drapery folds and the exaggerated pathos of the expressions. It reads these as "a step in the direction of the artistic ideals of the International Gothic". The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* adds that the late works acquire "un accento marcatamente espressionistico" and a palette of pure colours "saturi come smalti". The same years produced another triptych with the *Crucifixion*, the *Ascent to Calvary* and the *Deposition*, sold at the Hôtel Drouot in Paris on 30 March 1998. A *Crucifixion* fresco at Santa Maria della Scala attributed to Fei in 1984 is placed in the years immediately preceding his death. The National Gallery's catalogue assigns to this final phase "considerable originality and modernity in compositional schemes and an increasing richness of realistic detail".

A significant recovery of the present century belongs, according to the National Gallery's biography, to the 1390s rather than to the very end. In the spring of 2003 a seventeenth- or eighteenth-century oil painting of the *Crucified Christ* between Saint Catherine of Siena and Saint Francis was found in the storerooms of the Museo Civico in Siena. Haloes with punched decoration and remnants of gold ground were visible through abrasions in the surface. About a year of restoration revealed an original tempera *Crucifix with the Mourners in Humility* beneath the later repainting. It was attributed to Fei and exhibited at the Pinacoteca Nazionale from 21 April to 12 June 2005. The accompanying catalogue, *Il Crocifisso con i dolenti in umiltà di Paolo di Giovanni Fei: un capolavoro riscoperto*, discusses the unusual iconography, in which the mourning Virgin and Saint John are seated on the ground in the attitude of humility. Integrations were carried out on the basis of comparison with Fei's securely attributed works. The attribution has been widely accepted.

Fei's mural painting survives only in fragments and needs particular mention. The lunette with the *Visitation* in San Francesco in Siena is cited from Brandi's 1933 study onwards as one of the rare well-preserved examples. The frescoes of the Donati chapel, annexed to the sacristy of San Domenico, include the *Annunciation*, saints, members of the Donati family and stories of Christ and the Baptist. They pose a characteristic chronological problem. The chapel was built after a bequest by Niccolò Donati of 1373. Mallory, hampered by their poor condition, accepted only one scene as a mature work. A later study in *Die Kirchen von Siena* (1982) argued on documentary grounds for a much earlier date, close to the bequest, and attributed the whole decoration to Fei. If correct, that would furnish rare evidence for his activity in the 1370s. The question remains open.

## The Biccherna covers and civic painting

Fei's civic career found a natural artistic counterpart in the painted covers of the registers of the Biccherna and other Sienese magistracies. These *tavolette* are now gathered in the Archivio di Stato di Siena. The National Gallery of Art's biography records two covers attributed to Fei, dated 1388 and 1394. The cover of July–December 1388, number 20 in the Archivio's series, showing the *camerlengo* and his scribe in their office, is labelled in different sources as attributed either to Fei or to Taddeo di Bartolo. The attribution of the 1394 cover rests on Mallory's 1964 article. A Biccherna panel of 1402 has been associated with Fei's workshop. It shows the *camerlengo* Niccolò di Leonardo della Gazaia, his scrivener and three taxpayers, and was made under the Visconti lordship; it is now in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London (414-1892). That association appears to have been proposed only around 2002, after the panel had previously been catalogued as anonymous. No signed Biccherna by Fei with the subject of the Presentation is known, and the occasional claim that the Washington panel follows such a cover appears to be a conflation.

## Workshop, chronology and style

The reconstruction of Fei's chronology rests, as has been seen, on very few fixed points. These are the 1387 Accarigi date, if the fresco fragments are his; the 1397 Malavolti angels; the 1398–1399 *Presentation*; the 6 April 1400 Sant'Antonio payment; and the 1407–1408 Naples triptych. To these is added the undated but signed Serre di Rapolano polyptych. Everything else is placed by stylistic interpolation. The two principal syntheses, Mallory's monograph and the National Gallery's 2016 catalogue, agree on the broad lines while differing on details.

The National Gallery's catalogue, in its entry on the *Assumption*, offers a compact recent summary of the sequence. In works presumably of the 1370s, Fei "proposed solemn and static compositions", with figures "virtually immobile and symmetrically distributed", reserved in expression and wrapped in "thick starched draperies with the consistency of leather". The Metropolitan and Atlanta Madonnas and the *Saint Lawrence* formerly with the Matthiesen Gallery belong here. Around 1380 Fei animated his protagonists and observed their gestures more closely, in the fragmentary polyptych in the Museo d'Arte Sacra at Asciano and in the portable triptych no. 137 and diptych no. 146 in the Pinacoteca. His narratives grew more detailed and crowded and his interest in spatial setting increased, though the faces remained "almost impenetrable to human emotions". By the mid-1380s these developments yielded the *Birth of the Virgin* and the large works for Santa Maria della Scala. The 1390s brought the full maturity of the *Presentation*. After 1400 came the expanded spaces and foreshortenings of the *Assumption*. The final phase, with the Naples triptych, moved towards the elongation and pathos of International Gothic.

This chronology is coherent, but the 1370s works are placed there by inference. No painting can be securely dated before 1387. The alternative chronology implied by the early dating of the Donati frescoes would, if confirmed, provide a rare anchor for the earlier phase. It might also force a reconsideration of works now placed in the 1380s. Equally, the identification of the *Birth of the Virgin* with the Mannelli altarpiece would, if accepted, fix the triptych at either 1381 or 1391. Those two dates lie a decade apart and would sit very differently in the sequence. A date of 1391 would bring the triptych close to the signed polyptych and only seven years before the *Presentation*, compressing the stylistic distance between them in a way that existing chronologies do not anticipate.

Certain constants of Fei's style recur across the attributed corpus and serve as working criteria for attribution. Reference works describe his figures as having a slightly jowly, long-cheeked and long-nosed facial type, with eyes drawn as narrow almonds under swollen lids. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* finds that this gives the figures an air of "patetico straniamento". The hands are large and nervous, with characteristically bulbous fingertips. The National Gallery's catalogue observes that Fei "skillfully exploits the expressive potential" of these hands "to express the resolution, wonder, or doubt of his protagonists", even when the faces remain impassive. The gold grounds are richly punched; the *Presentation* uses twelve different punches. Brocades are rendered in sgraffito, and engaged frames are decorated with pastiglia and, in the New York *Madonna*, with *verre églomisé*. His palette moved from the pastel brilliance of the *Birth of the Virgin* to the "sunnier" light of the *Presentation*, and then to the saturated, enamel-like colours of the last works.

The existence of a workshop is evident from the sheer volume of documented commissions. It also appears in the repetition of types in small devotional panels, and in the disagreements between scholars over whether particular works are autograph or the product of assistants. The Metropolitan's workshop triptych, the Pinacoteca's *Mystic Marriage*, the laterals with Saints James, John the Baptist and Vitalis, and the 1402 Biccherna all sit on this boundary. No document names any of Fei's assistants or pupils. Statements that Sassetta or Giovanni di Paolo trained in his shop are hypotheses based on stylistic inheritance. The 1936 *Enciclopedia Italiana* entry on Sassetta said only "probabilmente" of such an apprenticeship, and the DBI entry on Giovanni di Paolo speaks of Fei's influence on the predella scenes and "nervosa linearità" of that painter's earliest dated altarpiece of 1426, not of documented training.

## Fei and his contemporaries: Andrea Vanni, Bartolo di Fredi, Taddeo di Bartolo

Fei's relation to Andrea Vanni has already been addressed. It is better understood as a historiographical confusion than as an artistic dependence. The confusion arose from the early conflation of Fei with Francesco di Vannuccio, Vanni's associate. Separating the two painters, as Brandi did, removed the main ground for linking Fei to Vanni's workshop. Vanni's own style, with its harder contours and more austere figures, offers few specific points of contact with Fei's mature works.

The relation to Bartolo di Fredi is much more substantial. Documented collaboration in the Malavolti chapel in 1397 is joined by the possible succession of the two painters on the San Pietro altarpiece in the cathedral. In the early twentieth century several of Bartolo's works were given to Fei, and the Washington *Presentation* was long given to Bartolo. That confusion is itself an index of real affinity: both painters favoured crowded narratives, bright and decorative colour, and lively anecdote. The differences are equally telling. Fei's architectural spaces are more coherent and his figures more composed, and his colour shifted towards the light, high-keyed harmonies of the *Presentation*. These were qualities in which the younger painter surpassed the elder.

With Taddeo di Bartolo, Fei shared the cathedral as a workplace. In 1402–1403 he completed choir frescoes allotted to Taddeo in 1401. The National Gallery's catalogue contrasts Fei's free distribution of the apostles in the Washington *Assumption* with Taddeo's compact group in the Montepulciano polyptych of 1401. That comparison captures a difference of temperament: Taddeo was more monumental and Fei more narrative and spatially inventive. The 1388 Biccherna cover that sources attribute variously to either painter shows how fine the boundaries between their hands can be in minor civic works. Taddeo's younger generation would dominate the first decades of the Quattrocento. Fei's late works were his last contribution to the transition, and their relation to Taddeo's has yet to receive close study.

## Legacy and conclusion

Fei died in 1411 as a painter repeatedly employed by the city's principal institution, the Opera del Duomo. His posthumous fortune proved uneven. The fact that his *Madonna of Humility* was installed at the centre of the Piccolomini altar in the 1480s shows that his images retained devotional authority long after his death. The National Gallery's catalogue argues that his late small panels "probably played an important role in stimulating the artists of the first generation of Sienese painting in the fifteenth century". It also suggests that the anonymous author of the sacristy frescoes in Santa Maria del Carmine in Siena understood the spatial premises of his *Assumption*. The influence on Giovanni di Paolo's earliest altarpiece of 1426 is noted in the DBI, and the possibility of a link with Sassetta has been raised repeatedly. Though these claims remain at the level of stylistic inheritance, they are plausible.

The more general verdict is nuanced. Fei was not an innovator on the scale of the great masters of the first half of the century, and he made no claim to be. His art was founded on deliberate dialogue with Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti and Simone Martini, a dialogue his patrons evidently valued. Within that frame, however, he made real contributions. He brought a new brightness of colour to Sienese narrative painting. He constructed coherent interiors of a complexity rare in late Trecento Siena. He multiplied anecdotal observation in a way that anticipates the International Gothic, and he invented new iconographic solutions, such as the fused Crucifixion and Trinity of the Naples triptych. The National Gallery's catalogue judges that the *Presentation* "epitomizes and at the same time renews the experiences of a whole century of Sienese figurative culture, projecting them towards what was about to be born in the new century". That judgement may stand as a fair summary of his whole career.

For the historian, Fei's case also teaches a methodological lesson. Biographical facts that survive are mostly civic; artistic facts that survive are mostly fragmentary; and the bridge between them is a single payment record for a single panel. Everything else depends on the stylistic judgement of connoisseurs. That judgement has proved durable in Fei's case, but it is not documentation. The persistence of a 1381 date for the *Birth of the Virgin*, of a 1385 date for the *Assumption*, of San Quirico d'Orcia as his birthplace and of Andrea Vanni as his teacher shows how quickly hypotheses harden into facts when their epistemic status is forgotten.

The panel, bequeathed by George Blumenthal in 1941, is in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. It measures 87 by 59.1 centimetres with its engaged frame and 68.6 by 42.9 centimetres in its painted surface. The museum dates it to the 1370s, on stylistic grounds, among Fei's early works. It was made as an independent image for private or small-scale devotion.

The Virgin is shown at half length, frontally, against a gold ground. A cusped arch and quatrefoils are tooled into the gold above her, and incised rays fill her halo, so that the ground itself takes on the appearance of an architectural setting. She looks straight out at the viewer with a grave, still expression, which gives the image the character of an icon.

She wears a black mantle with a border of gold embroidery and a small gold star on her right shoulder, over a dress of gold brocade. A white veil frames her face, falls on either side of her neck and is fastened on her chest by a round brooch of gold and glass with the head of Christ. Her face is long and pale, with a long straight nose, narrow eyes and a small mouth.

The Child sits on her left arm, supported by her right hand beneath his legs. He wears a pink mantle with a fine gold border, drawn around his body and legs over a pale blue lining. He feeds at her breast, which he presses with his right hand, and turns his eyes towards the viewer; with his left hand he holds his own foot. The playful gesture and the round, heavy face of the Child contrast with the solemnity of his mother.

The subject is the Virgin nursing the Child, the *Madonna del latte*, a type that stresses Christ's human nature and the Virgin's role as mother and intercessor. The frontal pose of the Virgin, unusual for a nursing Madonna of the late fourteenth century, recalls earlier models, while the naturalistic detail of the Child's gestures is of its own time.

The engaged frame is original and is the distinctive feature of the object. It is covered with raised foliate ornament in gilded pastiglia and set with small cabochon stones of different colours and with round medallions of *verre églomisé*, gold leaf engraved and painted under glass, showing tiny heads of saints. At the top and bottom centre are larger medallions. The frame turns the panel into a precious object comparable to goldsmith's work.

The quality of the tooling, the brocades and the glass medallions shows a workshop able to combine the techniques of the painter, the gilder and the glass-painter. Whether Fei himself executed the medallions or collaborated with a specialist remains an open question. The panel as a whole shows the young painter's command of the luxury techniques of Sienese painting.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground and the frame are well preserved, though some stones are missing from their settings and a few medallions are damaged. The black of the Virgin's mantle has lost some of its modelling, and the flesh tones are slightly worn. The survival of the panel with its original frame makes it an important witness to the appearance of Sienese devotional images in their own time.

The triptych is Fei's best-known painting and came from the suppressed church of San Maurizio in Siena to the Pinacoteca Nazionale. It is unsigned and undated, and its date in the 1380s rests on style; its identification with the lost Mannelli altarpiece, which would give it a date of 1381 or 1391, is a hypothesis. It consists of a wide central panel with the Birth of the Virgin, two narrow wings with pairs of standing saints, and a crowning of pointed gables and pinnacles that survives in large part.

The central panel represents the interior of Saint Anne's house as a continuous space seen through three arches, divided by thin painted columns that correspond to the colonnettes of the frame. The rooms are covered by rib vaults painted dark blue with gold stars, with a gold sun in a roundel at the centre, and a tiled floor in perspective runs beneath the figures. The architecture of the frame and the painted architecture are made to coincide, so that the frame appears to be the front of the house.

In the upper part of the central room Saint Anne sits up in a bed covered with a blue and white chequered cloth, beneath a hanging with a scalloped border. She wears a pale blue mantle and a white veil, and her halo is gold. A young woman in red, seated on the edge of the bed, offers her a cloth, and she reaches out to take it. The calm of the mother after the birth is set against the activity of the women around her.

At the left, two women enter through a curtained doorway, bringing a covered bowl and a striped towel, the gifts and refreshments brought to a woman after childbirth. In the foreground a woman in a red dress and a white headcloth holds the newborn Virgin, haloed and wrapped in white, on her lap beside a basin, testing the water of the bath with her hand. A kneeling woman in pale blue and a woman seen from behind, in an orange mantle, assist her. A dark metal ewer and basin stand on a chest between the bed and the bath.

In the room at the right, separated by a column, Joachim sits with a grey beard and a halo, in a pink mantle. A bald, bearded man and a fair-haired youth lean towards him, perhaps bringing him the news. Beyond them a low wall and a window open onto a garden with red roses. The secondary room, the attendants and the garden extend the scene beyond the confined space of the bedchamber and give it the character of a domestic chronicle.

In the gables above, painted in quatrefoils against a dark ground, Christ blesses from the central roundel, while the angel of the Annunciation and the Virgin Annunciate occupy the side gables. On the left wing Saint James the Greater, with a pilgrim's staff and a red book, stands beside Saint Catherine of Alexandria, crowned and holding her wheel and a quill. On the right wing a crowned saint in a dark veil, Elizabeth of Hungary, stands behind Saint Bartholomew, who holds a knife and a red book. The saints are set against gold under trefoil arches.

The palette is light and bright: salmon pink, pale blue, white and yellow predominate, set off by the reds of the dresses and the deep blue of the vaults. The faces are long and delicate, with small features, and the many details of furnishings and dress, the striped cloths, the chequered cover, the ewer and the roses, are described with minute care. The composition derives from Pietro Lorenzetti's *Birth of the Virgin* of 1342, but the narrower space, the denser groups and the brighter colour are Fei's own.

The panel has suffered several losses in the lower part of the central scene, among them areas of the bath group and of Joachim's mantle, which are filled with a neutral tone. The gold of the wings is worn in places, and the frame and pinnacles show losses and repairs. The upper part of the central panel, the architecture and the faces of the principal figures are well preserved.

The polyptych is in the Pinacoteca Nazionale in Siena. The presence of Saint Andrew suggests that it is the altarpiece from the church of Sant'Andrea, known as Sant'Andreino, at Serre di Rapolano, the only surviving work that bears Fei's signature; a band of dark lettering on the base of the central panel, now illegible in photographs, may be the remains of that inscription. The panel is in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum and is dated by the museum to the 1390s. At the centre the Virgin sits frontally on a throne set on steps, before a cloth of honour of red brocade patterned in gold. She wears a dark mantle, now very dark, over a gold dress and a white veil, and nurses the Child, who lies across her lap in a gold garment and turns his face towards the viewer. A crowd of angels fills the upper part of the panel around the throne, their heads set close together in tiers, with gold haloes, golden hair and joined or crossed hands. The panel is in good condition. The gold and the brocades are well preserved, and the colours of the saints' garments remain bright. The fresco is in the Franciscan basilica of San Lucchese, outside Poggibonsi, on the road between Siena and Florence. It is attributed to Fei, with some caution, in the descriptions of the church, and dated to the late fourteenth century. It reproduces in fresco the form of a painted triptych on panel, complete with gables and pinnacles, set in a niche of the wall.

The niche is framed by a broad painted border of small squares, stars and rosettes in red, white, green and gold, imitating the inlaid marble of cosmatesque work. In the intrados above, against a blue ground scattered with stars, appears the Holy Face, the image of Christ's features impressed on Veronica's veil. Painted pilasters with small figures, now faded, run down the sides of the niche.

Within the niche the painted triptych stands against a blue sky. Its dark frame, with three pointed gables flanked by tall pinnacles and crockets, imitates the carpentry of a contemporary altarpiece. The cusped arches over the three figures are gilded, and the haloes are worked in raised relief, so that the fresco reproduces the effect of gold on panel.

In the central field the Virgin, seated, wears a blue mantle with a small star on the shoulder, over a white dress. She holds the Child on her left arm and supports him with her right hand. The Child, in a green tunic, turns towards her and reaches up to touch her chin. At the left of the Virgin's head a small bird flies against the gold, the goldfinch that gives the image its name, a sign of the future Passion.

At the left stands Saint Anthony Abbot, the hermit saint, with a long white beard, in a hooded brown habit; he holds a red book and a staff. At the right Saint Christopher, with long fair hair and a short beard, carries the Christ Child on his shoulder and holds a staff in his right hand. The fresco is well preserved in its upper part, where the blue of the sky and the colours of the figures remain bright. The panel is the central part of the altarpiece painted in 1398–1399 for the chapel of San Pietro in Siena Cathedral, for which Fei received payments from the Opera del Duomo. It is the only surviving painting by him matched with a payment record. The lateral panels with Saints Peter and Paul and the upper parts of the altarpiece are lost or unidentified. The panel is now in the National Gallery of Art in Washington.

The composition is divided into three arched fields by two painted white pillars, which lay beneath wooden colonnettes now lost. Behind the pillars a single interior extends across the whole width: a Gothic church with a nave and aisles, pale grey and white walls, red horizontal mouldings, tall lancet windows and, at the top of the central field, a rose window. The arches of the frame are painted as part of the architecture, with white vaults and blue and red ornament.

In the central field the young Virgin stands at the top of a broad flight of white steps, between red columns that carry the canopy over the altar. She wears a dress of gold brocade and a gold halo, and turns slightly towards the viewer with her hands crossed. The high priest, haloed, with a long white beard, gold vestments and a mitre, bends towards her and takes her by the arm; beside him an older priest in pink and red raises his hands. Above the altar rises a gilded tabernacle with pinnacles.

In the left field Saint Anne, in a white veil and a dark blue mantle, and Joachim, with a grey beard and a pink mantle over blue, stand together, both haloed; Joachim stretches out his hand towards the steps, and Anne raises hers. Behind them are the heads of other women. In front of them a woman in a green mantle kneels with her back to the viewer, half hidden by the pillar.

In the right field, behind a carved white balustrade decorated with Gothic tracery, a group of young women with braided hair and books lean forward to watch. They are the girls who will be Mary's companions in the Temple. Below them two bearded men stand in long mantles, one in orange with a white headcloth, the other in pink over blue with a dark cap, his hands folded. Their calm attention contrasts with the movement of the girls above.

In the foreground two boys stand on a floor patterned with red and white tiles at the foot of the steps, one in a red tunic and stockings, the other in a pale tunic with red spots. One turns and points upwards towards the Virgin. Seen against the light of the white steps, they lead the eye into the space and up the steps to the meeting at the top.

The colour is light and luminous: salmon pink, light blue, white and pale grey dominate, enriched by the gold of the brocades and of the high priest's vestments and accented by the red of the columns and mouldings. The architecture is drawn with a clarity that allows the plan of the church to be read, and the figures are distributed in depth across it.

The panel is in good condition for a painting transferred from its support. The painted pillars, partly covered by the lost wooden colonnettes, show damage along their length, and the surface has a fine network of cracks. The gables and the upper edge of the architecture are well preserved.

The small panel of the *Assumption* is in the National Gallery of Art in Washington, in the Samuel H. Kress Collection. It measures 66.5 by 38.1 centimetres in its painted surface and 79.8 by 51 centimetres with its engaged frame, which survives with the panel. Its attribution to Fei has never been doubted; its date has been revised from about 1385 to about 1400–1405, after the *Presentation of the Virgin* of 1398–1399.

The scene is divided into two zones by the gold ground. In the upper zone, enclosed by a round arch of raised gilded ornament, the Virgin ascends to heaven among angels. In the lower zone the apostles gather around her empty tomb. The kneeling apostle on the rocky mount between the two zones links them, directing the eye from the tomb to the Virgin above.

The Virgin sits frontally on a bank of white cloud, her hands joined in prayer and her eyes raised. She wears a white mantle and dress patterned with small motifs in gold, edged with gold borders, a choice of colour that sets her apart from the brightly dressed angels around her and suggests the radiance of her glorified body. Rays incised in the gold spread from her figure.

Ten angels surround her. Two, in white robes patterned with gold and with long blue lower folds, fly horizontally beneath the cloud and hold it, their wings tipped with red. On either side three angels in pink and white play musical instruments: at the left a portable organ, a fiddle and another instrument, at the right a lute, a psaltery and a second lute or gittern. Two more angels at the top pray with joined hands. The concert of angels gives the Assumption the character of a celebration in heaven.

The kneeling apostle, seen from behind in a blue tunic and a pink mantle, raises his arms towards the Virgin from the summit of a dark, faceted rock. His position, apart from the others and closer to the Virgin, identifies him as Thomas, who according to legend arrived late and received the Virgin's girdle as proof of her bodily assumption.

The eleven apostles in the lower zone crowd around an open sarcophagus of pale stone, decorated on its front with green inlaid panels and filled with red and white flowers. Two apostles bend into the tomb in the foreground, one seen from behind; the others look into it, pray or turn their eyes upwards. An old apostle with a white beard at the left, probably Peter, and a young beardless apostle at the right, probably John, close the group. The ground beneath them is dark and scattered with small flowering plants.

In the spandrels above the arch are two roundels with the busts of the archangel Gabriel at the left and the Virgin of the Annunciation at the right, against gold. The Annunciation, the beginning of the Virgin's role in the Incarnation, frames the Assumption, its conclusion. The raised gilded ornament of the arch, the punched borders and the engaged frame with its small painted motifs make the panel a precious object for private devotion.

The panel is in good condition. The colours, the pink, red, blue and white of the garments and the gold of the ground, are fresh, and the gold patterns of the Virgin's mantle and the angels' robes are well preserved. A few scratches and small losses are visible in the lower group of apostles and in the frame. The survival of the engaged frame allows the panel to be seen in its original form.

The panel was painted in 1402 as the cover of a register of the Biccherna, the chief financial office of the Sienese commune, at the time when Siena was under the lordship of Gian Galeazzo Visconti, duke of Milan. It is now in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London (414-1892). Long catalogued as the work of an anonymous painter, it was associated with the workshop of Paolo di Giovanni Fei in 2002. It measures 44 by 32.4 centimetres.

The panel is divided into three zones, following the usual arrangement of Biccherna covers of the period. The upper zone shows a scene in the office of the Biccherna, the middle zone the coats of arms of the officials, and the lower zone a long inscription recording the register's contents, the period of office and the names of those responsible for it.

The scene shows the interior of the Biccherna office, which was on the ground floor of the Palazzo Pubblico. Behind a long counter stand two officials in red caps. At the right the *camarlingo*, the treasurer, dressed in red, places his hand in a bowl of coins and gestures towards the taxpayers. At the left his scrivener, in blue, holds up a slip of paper and records the transaction. On the counter lie inkwells with pens, papers and a knife.

In front of the counter three men in short tunics and coloured hose, blue, pink and white, approach the officials. One raises his hands in conversation, the second holds a coin, the third looks up at the *camarlingo*. The figures are small and lively, and their gestures convey the exchange of money and words that the register recorded.

The office is described with care. Along the back wall runs a shelf with a row of small white labelled boxes. Strongboxes stand on the bench at the left, on the bench at the right, where one is open to show its compartments, and on the floor, beside a small open door. A barred window lights the room from the right. Above the scene, at the top centre, hangs the *balzana*, the black and white shield of Siena.

The middle zone carries six coats of arms painted against a gold band decorated with foliate ornament, those of the families of the officials named in the inscription. Below them the inscription, in black Gothic capitals on a white ground, records that this is the book of the income and expenditure of the Biccherna of the Commune of Siena in the time of the officials, among them Niccolò di Leonardo della Gazaia, *camarlingo*, from January to June 1402, with their scribe.

The light colour, the clear, slightly simplified space of the office and the lively small figures connect the panel with the late work of Fei's workshop, whose master held civic offices and knew the Sienese magistracies from within. The panel continues the tradition of Biccherna covers, in which the officials of the commune were shown at work, and makes them a record of the administration of the city.

The panel has been restored, probably in the nineteenth century by the painter and collector Johann Anton Ramboux, and the inscription was partly repainted at that time. The surface is crossed by fine cracks and worn, particularly in the white ground of the inscription, which is damaged at the left. The registers that the cover once protected are lost.

The two small panels in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum formed a portable diptych, originally hinged so that it could be closed and carried. The museum catalogues it as a product of Fei's workshop and dates it around 1400–1410. Each wing measures about 45.5 by 19.7 centimetres with its engaged frame. The diptych illustrates the production of small devotional objects in series in the workshop of the painter's last years.

The two wings are read together. The left wing shows the Virgin and Child enthroned among saints and angels, the right wing the Crucifixion. The roundels in the gables, with the angel of the Annunciation at the left and the Virgin Annunciate at the right, span both wings and unite them in a single programme, from the Incarnation to the Passion.

In the left wing the Virgin, in a dark mantle over a red dress, sits on a throne covered with a red cloth patterned in gold. She holds the Child, dressed in pink, on her left knee; he holds a scroll with an inscription. Two angels in pink and red stand behind the throne. At the left Saint John the Baptist, bearded and barefoot, in his camel-hair garment and a red-lined mantle, holds a slender cross-staff; at the right Saint James the Great, in a yellow mantle over pink, holds a pilgrim's staff and a red book. The floor is green, with a stepped shape at the front.

In the right wing Christ hangs on the cross against the gold ground, his body thin and greenish, his head fallen on his shoulder, under a red tablet with the inscription. The Virgin, in a dark mantle, stands at the left with her hands joined in grief. At the foot of the cross Saint Mary Magdalen, in red, kneels and embraces the wood, her long hair falling on her shoulders. At the right Saint John the Evangelist, in a pink mantle over dark blue, joins his hands and looks up.

The figures are elongated and slender, with small heads and long, falling drapery, features of Fei's late manner that the workshop repeated. The Magdalen at the foot of the cross and the grieving Virgin and John follow the formulas of Sienese Crucifixions, simplified for the small format. The colours, red, pink, yellow and dark blue against gold, are bright and clearly separated.

The tabernacle frames, with steep gables, crockets and pine-cone finials, are carved and gilded over a red-orange bole. The museum's notes on the frames observe that the gable ornament, whose paterae have been regessoed, regilt and repunched, appears too bold for the paintings, while the pastiglia mouldings that frame the scenes are well balanced and in proportion with the roundels and figures. The frames have therefore been partly altered.

The diptych belongs to a category of objects, portable altarpieces for private devotion, that Sienese workshops produced in numbers in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. The repetition of types and compositions, the simplified modelling and the bright colour served a market of private patrons who wanted images close to the style of an established master.

The paint surface is in reasonably good condition, with some wear in the darker areas, such as the Virgin's mantle in the left wing, and in the flesh tones. The gilding of the grounds is well preserved. Small old metal labels with the attribution are fixed to the lower edges of the frames, a reminder of the diptych's history in a private collection.

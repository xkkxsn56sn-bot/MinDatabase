## A Painter Recovered from Silence

Few painters of the Italian fourteenth century have had a stranger posthumous fate than *Andrea di Nerio*. For more than six centuries his name lay in a handful of notarial records in Aretine archives, entirely detached from any surviving work, while the paintings now given to him circulated under other names: the young Spinello Aretino, a "Master of the Nativity of the Baptist" invented for the purpose, the anonymous Maestro del Vescovado, or simply "Aretine school". His case shows how the history of medieval painting is made. Documents and objects lead separate lives until a restoration, a cleaning or a newly noticed inscription brings them together. For Andrea that moment came in 1974, when, during the restoration of an Annunciation panel in the diocesan collections of Arezzo, a signature reading "Andrea f(ecit)", concealed in the decorated fabric of the painted interior, was discovered and published by [Anna Maria Maetzke](/scholars.html#maetzke-anna-maria) in the catalogue of the exhibition *Arte nell'Aretino. Recuperi e restauri dal 1968 al 1974*. A group of works already recognised as stylistically coherent finally acquired a name.

This essay sets out what is known and what is merely supposed about Andrea di Nerio, on the basis of the published documents, the early literature, and the specialist scholarship that has rebuilt his figure since the 1960s, and it keeps a firm line between the archival facts, which are few, and the attributions, which are many and not always agreed. Within a largely Italian literature three scholars stand out for their decisive role in the reconstruction. [Luciano Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano), from 1965 onwards, first gathered the key panels into a group; Anna Maria Maetzke published the signature in 1974 and so gave the group its name; and [Roberto Bartalini](/scholars.html#bartalini-roberto), above all in his 1996 essay in *Prospettiva* on the Tarlati and the Maestro del Vescovado and in his 2005 synthesis on Aretine painting from Gregorio and Donato to Andrea di Nerio, gave the painter his place within the history of the city. Other contributions are described here by their arguments rather than by their authors' names.

## Arezzo between the Tarlati and Florence

Andrea's career cannot be understood apart from the city in which it was almost entirely spent. In the first decades of the fourteenth century Arezzo was a Ghibelline<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ghibellines" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> commune dominated by the Tarlati di Pietramala, and above all by Guido Tarlati<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guido-tarlati" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, bishop of the city and in effect its lord, who died in 1327. His patronage made Arezzo, for a short period, one of the liveliest artistic centres of Tuscany. In 1320 the bishop and the canons of the Pieve di Santa Maria commissioned from the Sienese Pietro Lorenzetti the great polyptych for the high altar of the Pieve, whose central Virgin, clothed in a luminous pink robe lined with fur and turning intently towards her restless Child, became one of the formative models for local painters. In the same years the Florentine Buonamico Buffalmacco, the prankster of Boccaccio's<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giovanni-boccaccio" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> and Sacchetti's<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-sacchetti-franco" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> novelle, now widely identified with the Master of the Triumph of Death in the Camposanto of Pisa, worked in Arezzo for Bishop Guido; his Aretine period is usually placed between about 1321 and 1327. After the bishop's death the Tarlati monuments in the cathedral continued to proclaim the family's ambitions, from the grandiose cenotaph by the Sienese sculptors Agostino di Giovanni and Agnolo di Ventura, signed and dated 1330, to the chapel of Ciuccio Tarlati<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ciuccio-tarlati" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> commissioned in 1334 for the right aisle, with the fresco of the Crucifixion from which the Maestro del Vescovado takes his conventional name.

Bartalini has shown that these years of Tarlati patronage form the soil in which the main developments of Aretine Trecento painting took root, namely the activity of the Maestro del Vescovado and of Andrea di Nerio. The city's political fortunes then shifted repeatedly: the Tarlati signoria dissolved, Florentine influence grew, and in 1384 Arezzo passed definitively under Florentine rule. That transition coincides with the last phase of Andrea's career and with the years in which his presumed pupil Spinello began to work increasingly outside the city, first in Lucca and then in Florence. It has been suggested that the turbulence of the second half of the century may explain an apparent shift in local demand: around mid-century Andrea's catalogue is, as far as we know, the only one in the city to contain panel paintings, whereas later votive fresco painting seems to predominate. The hypothesis is attractive, but it rests on what has survived, and survival in Arezzo has been capricious. In the nineteenth century gold-ground panels from local collections were sold off to the Florentine market, and in one case remembered by an Aretine archivist they were bought by a carpenter in Piazza Grande to be burnt for the small quantity of gold they yielded.

## The Documentary Record

The archival evidence for Andrea di Nerio is slight but solid, and it permits a skeleton chronology rather than a biography. The earliest secure document dates from 1331, when the priest Goro di Isacco (also cited as Goro Isacci) commissioned Andrea to decorate a recently built chapel in the Pieve di Santa Maria, including a fresco of the Annunciation that has not survived. The document shows Andrea working independently, and with a commission of some standing in the city's principal church, by 1331. This implies that he was born no later than the first years of the century and that his training belongs to the 1320s, precisely the decade of Lorenzetti's polyptych and of Buffalmacco's presence in Arezzo.

A second document concerns his family. In an act of 30 September 1333, preserved in the Archivio di Stato di Firenze among the Notarile antecosimiano, the painter Donato di Rigo acknowledges that Andrea had restored half the dowry of Donato's late daughter Gemma, described as the wife of the late Nerio. Donato di Rigo is the "Donato" of the partnership of Gregorio and Donato d'Arezzo, the leading local painters of the generation before Andrea, so the document ties Andrea to the principal Aretine workshop of the early Trecento. The exact relationship is uncertain, since it is not clear whether Gemma was Andrea's mother or his stepmother, and the scholarship has prudently left the question open. It has reasonably been inferred that Andrea's first training may have taken place in or around the shop of Gregorio and Donato, but here the document itself is the only firm ground.

The best-known document is the one that kept Andrea's name alive in Aretine scholarship long before any work could be attached to it. In 1341 the operai of Arezzo cathedral commissioned two painters, Andrea and Balduccio di Cecco, to decorate certain arches "sicut facti sunt arcus capelle episcopatus Aretini per Bonamicum pictorem", that is, in the manner of those already painted by the painter Bonamico in the bishop's chapel; on the modern reading of the text the work consisted of painting the lateral vaults of an aisle of the cathedral with a starry sky. Published by Aretine archival scholarship in 1880 and later re-examined and corrected, the document has a double importance. It was one of the pieces of evidence that confirmed the historical existence of Buffalmacco as a painter active in Arezzo, at a time when some nineteenth-century critics doubted that he had existed at all; and it shows Andrea, ten years after his first recorded commission, entrusted with an official cathedral contract that explicitly took Buffalmacco's Aretine work as its model. Balduccio di Cecco, his partner, is known only from documents; no work by him has been identified, and the later painter Giovanni d'Agnolo di Balduccio was his *nipote*.

Also connected with the cathedral is the Bertoldini chapel, whose contract of 1340 concerned its sculptural apparatus and whose frescoes, discussed below, are dated shortly after that year. Another document of 1340, the will of the banker Feo di Tolomeo, leaves money to be spent on painting the tribune of the Pieve "quando dicta tribuna pingeretur", showing that the tribune had not yet been painted. This has led one recent study to question Vasari's<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> attribution of the lost tribune frescoes to Pietro Lorenzetti and to suggest that they could have been executed by Andrea and his team, an argument from probability without documentary support.

The end of Andrea's career is less clearly documented than its middle. He is commonly described as active in Arezzo between 1331 and 1369 and as having died before 1387, but the basis for the date 1369 has not been verified against the documents, and the terminus of 1387 is usually stated without the text being quoted. The Art Valorem sale catalogue for a pinnacle with the Virgin Annunciate attributed to Andrea (lot 200, sold on 29 March 2022) explains that he "disparaît sans doute avant 1387, date à laquelle il n'est plus cité dans les archives", which would make it an inference from silence rather than a record of death. Until the documents themselves, published in the appendices of the recent monographic literature on Spinello, are cited in full, these limits should be treated as provisional. An old claim that Andrea worked in Narni between 1331 and 1333 sits awkwardly with the Aretine commission of 1331, has not been taken up by recent scholarship, and should be regarded as unverified.

Finally, standard reference literature reports that Spinello's brother Niccolò, a goldsmith and sculptor, married Andrea's daughter Rebecca. If correct, this gives a social and professional basis to the stylistic relationship between Andrea and Spinello, and it would fit well with what is known of Aretine artisanal society, in which painters, goldsmiths and sculptors intermarried and shared workshops. The date of the marriage and the document recording it, however, have not been verified against the archival sources, and caution is again required.

## Vasari's Silence

It is often said, even in specialist contexts, that Vasari named Andrea di Nerio as the teacher of Spinello Aretino. He did not, and the claim needs correcting because it confuses the modern reconstruction with the early source. In the 1550 Torrentiniana edition of the *Vite*, Vasari introduces Spinello as the painter who revived the art in Arezzo "dopo la morte di Giotto e Taddeo Gaddi", stating that the old Greek manner had by then been forgotten in the city "per non avere atteso aretino alcuno, da Margheritone insino a Spinello, a quello esercizio", even though Giotto, Taddeo Gaddi and Jacopo del Casentino had worked there; Spinello, "addomesticatosi con Iacopo di Casentino, imparò da lui il disegno et il modo del lavorare". In the 1568 Giuntina the account shifts slightly. Spinello is now so gifted that "quasi senza maestro" he knew as a child what many trained under excellent masters did not; he had "avuto amicizia con Iacopo di Casentino mentre lavorò in Arezzo e imparato da lui qualche cosa", and before the age of twenty he was a far better master than the elderly Jacopo.

Vasari's account therefore does more than omit Andrea di Nerio: it explicitly denies that any Aretine practised painting between [Margarito](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Margarito.html) (Vasari's Margaritone) and Spinello, leaving no room for Gregorio and Donato, for the Maestro del Vescovado, or for Andrea, that is, for the whole local tradition the documents and the modern reconstruction have brought back to light. Vasari was a patriotic Aretine and usually well informed about his native city, and his construction must be understood as a historiographical choice. It makes Spinello the founder of an Aretine pictorial dignity, binds him to the Florentine line descending from Giotto through Taddeo Gaddi and Jacopo del Casentino, and, as the modern literature has observed, tends to credit him with practically the whole late medieval pictorial production of Arezzo. Some of the frescoes Vasari gave to Spinello are now attributed, at least by some scholars, to Andrea. In this sense Andrea's fate in the *Vite* is not simple neglect but absorption: his work survived in Vasari's text under his pupil's name. Vasari remains indispensable for the topography of lost Aretine painting, but for Andrea he is a witness by omission, to be read against the grain.

The panel that restored Andrea's identity is the Annunciation now in the Museo Diocesano di Arte Sacra (MuDAS) of Arezzo, housed since 2011 in the episcopal palace facing the cathedral. Its importance lies first in the signature. The inscription "Andrea f(ecit)" was not displayed in a cartouche or on the frame, as Pietro Lorenzetti had proudly signed his Pieve polyptych, but hidden within the painted decoration of the interior, where it remained unnoticed until the restoration completed in 1974. Its publication allowed the panel, and with it the works already linked to it on stylistic grounds, to be connected with the Andrea documented in Arezzo in 1331 and 1341. The identification rests on the reasonable assumption that the "Andrea" of the signature is the "Andrea" of the cathedral contract, the only painter of that name known to have held a leading position in the city in these decades. No document names the painting, but the identification has met with no serious challenge.

The panel comes from the destroyed church of San Marco a Murello in Arezzo, from which it entered the diocesan collections. Some scholars have suggested that it was originally painted for the Compagnia della Santissima Annunziata<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-compagnia-santissima-annunziata" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a>, a lay confraternity whose oratory was founded around the middle of the century and later absorbed into the Renaissance church of the Santissima Annunziata. The two pieces of evidence are not incompatible, since San Marco may record the last location of the panel rather than its first destination. The agreement of subject and date makes the confraternal hypothesis attractive, but no document supports it.

The picture field, crowned by a broad gable, unfolds the event as a continuous sacred narrative descending from heaven to earth. At the apex of the gable, within a glowing red glory of rays, a haloed divine figure entrusts a small kneeling angel with his mission, while lower on the left slope of the gold ground a second angel with dark wings is shown in flight, so that the commissioning and the journey of Gabriel precede the encounter below. The lower zone is divided by a slender twisted column into two adjoining spaces of unequal character. On the left Gabriel kneels in a vaulted antechamber, its barrel vault painted deep blue and sprinkled with stars, its back wall covered with a pink diaper pattern of small triangles and framed by inlaid panels of coloured marble around a gilded two-light window. He wears a golden robe patterned with rosettes and crossed by a dark stole. With one hand raised in salutation, he holds in the other a dark frond, presumably the branch borne by the messenger. On the right, in a chamber hung with red cloth sown with small flowers and closed above by a scalloped, striped valance, the Virgin sits on a long bench covered with chequered fabric. She is wrapped in a dark mantle, her head bowed, her right hand pressed to her breast in a gesture of humble acceptance, her left hand resting on an open book in her lap. Above her room an upper storey with blue starred arcades and a parapet cuts into the gold ground. From it a small winged figure in red, apparently a seraph, plunges downward along rays of gold that pass through the valance, where the dove of the Holy Spirit can be seen descending towards Mary. A strip of geometric inlay along the lower edge of the Virgin's chamber completes the precious, "cosmatesque" character of the painted architecture.

The panel is generally dated around 1350, the most precise proposal being 1348–50, while other accounts speak more broadly of the 1350s. Its rich architectural staging, with its contrast between the cool vaulted space of the angel and the warm, textile-lined room of the Virgin, shows how far Andrea had absorbed the spatial lessons of Pietro Lorenzetti and of Florentine painting after Giotto, without abandoning the decorative splendour typical of Aretine practice. The haloes are filled with finely tooled foliate scrolls, and the edges of the gable are bordered with ornamental motifs, in keeping with the growing attention to the working of the gold that technical studies have observed in Andrea's panels from the Stories of the Baptist onwards. The figures show the narrow, elongated faces, sharp profiles and slightly melancholy intensity that recur throughout the works now attached to his name: the angel's taut, pointed profile and the Virgin's long, gently inclined face correspond closely to one modern description of Andrea's personages as figures with irregular faces, of a slightly strange beauty, with drooping noses and pointed profiles.

## From Spinello to Andrea: The Reconstruction of a Catalogue

The reconstruction of Andrea's oeuvre began before his name was known and initially pointed in a different direction. In 1965, in a fundamental article in *Paragone* entitled "Da Spinello Aretino a Lorenzo Monaco", Bellosi published three panels that would become central to the problem: a half-length Virgin and Child then in the Harris collection at Oxnead Hall in Norfolk, which had passed through Colnaghi in London in 1954–55, and the two small panels with Stories of Saint John the Baptist in the Kunstmuseum Bern. He presented them as youthful works of Spinello painted in Arezzo in the 1370s, a reasonable conclusion given the state of knowledge, since Spinello was the only Aretine painter of the later Trecento with an established identity and the panels showed a refinement and a Giottesque solidity that seemed to anticipate his mature manner.

Doubts followed quickly. In 1966 it was observed that the Harris Madonna was by the same hand as the diocesan Annunciation, not yet known to be signed. In 1969, as *Il Giornale dell'Arte* recalled in its 2015 report on the Arezzo exhibition of the Madonna Sarti, a further study argued that the Harris panel and the Bern stories reflected an "antefatto di più antica cultura", a formation of the 1330s and 1340s preceding Spinello. In 1970 the conventional name "Master of the Nativity of the Baptist" was coined for the author of the Nativity in Avignon and related works, and in 1971 one critic gave the Harris Madonna and the Annunciation to the Maestro del Vescovado. In 1974 Bellosi himself, in his book on Buffalmacco and the Triumph of Death, revised his position, describing the Harris Madonna as the work of an Aretine painter distinct from the Maestro del Vescovado and not by Spinello; in the same year the signature on the Annunciation was published, and from then on the group could be given to Andrea di Nerio. The scholarship of the following decades extended and refined it: a Paris catalogue of 1976–77 described the Avignon Nativity as the work closest to the signed Annunciation, and the anonymous "Master of the Nativity of the Baptist" was absorbed into Andrea's catalogue, a move proposed in 1987 and argued systematically by Bartalini in 1996 and 2005.

In this process the boundaries of the catalogue were redrawn more than once. Works first given to Spinello passed to Andrea, works given to the Maestro del Vescovado were separated from his, and a third, minor personality, the Master of Pieve a Sietina, was defined to absorb part of the production of Andrea's circle. Throughout, the fundamental difficulty has been to decide how far the stylistic continuity of the Aretine tradition reflects one evolving hand and how far a workshop practice in which several hands worked in a shared idiom. The most recent contributions have stressed the second possibility, pointing to the dense collaborations and exchanges of hands visible in Aretine frescoes of the second half of the century and warning against an "insistito attribuzionismo" that imposes modern categories of individual authorship on medieval workshop production.

## The Formation of a Style: Buffalmacco, Pietro Lorenzetti and the Madonna Sarti

The work that best reveals the roots of Andrea's art is the half-length Virgin and Child formerly in the Harris collection, now with the Paris dealer Giovanni Sarti and therefore usually called the Madonna Sarti. Painted in tempera, gold and silver on panel and measuring 80 by 62 centimetres, it was exhibited at the Casa Museo Ivan Bruschi in Arezzo from December 2015 into 2016, in the first exhibition of the cycle *Ritorni*, devoted to the temporary return of works of Aretine origin.

The catalogue entry published by the gallery examines the panel's physical structure in a way that bears directly on its dating. The panel consists of two horizontal boards, and the horizontal grain implies that the lower board originally extended into lateral compartments, so that the complex would have been a rectangular altarpiece with a raised central gable, a "low dossal with central projection" characteristic of the late thirteenth century and largely abandoned in Florence by about 1330. The compartments were separated by flat pilasters with a strip of silver leaf decorated with star patterns, rediscovered under later regilding during restoration, and the gold ground is not punched but merely grained with a point, with foliate motifs standing out in reserve as a kind of imitation of textile. On these grounds the author of the entry argued for a date around 1330 and proposed that the lateral compartments may have contained four narrative scenes rather than busts of saints. Other specialists prefer a somewhat later date: Bartalini placed the panel more broadly in the 1330s or 1340s, and a more recent study has argued for the 1340s, reading the archaic features not as evidence of an early date of execution but as a reflection of a training in Buffalmacco's orbit during the painter's Aretine years, about 1321–27. The disagreement is real. The archaising structure supports an early date, while comparison of the Virgin's face with the Bertoldini chapel frescoes, generally dated soon after 1340, supports a later one.

On the panel's sources there is broad agreement. The model is the central panel of Lorenzetti's Pieve polyptych, from which Andrea took the restless Child and the luxurious fur lining of the Virgin's mantle, while eliminating every reference to a throne, so that the Madonna appears as a pure half-length image, and replacing Lorenzetti's tense lateral torsion with calm frontality. The Virgin looks straight out with her head slightly inclined, her hand supports the Child firmly but without the anxious grip of Lorenzetti's Madonna, and the Child raises an open palm as if suddenly arrested. The grey, hard chiaroscuro and sharp features have been traced to Buffalmacco, whose Aretine works for Bishop Guido Tarlati included, on the most widely accepted reconstruction, a fragmentary fresco of the Virgin and Child with saints in the cathedral and a Virgin and Child from San Donato in Pergognano now in the diocesan museum; the links are close enough for it to have been suggested that Andrea was Buffalmacco's direct pupil, though no document supports this. The broad folds of the mantle, flattened by the darkening of the azurite, have been read as a response to French Gothic sculpture, and the solid foreshortening of the Virgin's hand as a sign of attention to Giotto's mature works. The result is a style neither Florentine nor Sienese, which reworks both with a gravity and melancholy of its own. Bellosi, writing on Spinello's early works in 2005, recognised in these qualities the tradition on which Spinello had been formed, embodied in "l'opera del Maestro del Vescovado e di Andrea di Nerio".

### Virgin and Child (Madonna Sarti)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-02.jpg" alt="Gabled gold-ground panel by Andrea di Nerio showing the half-length Virgin facing the viewer in a dark mantle marked with a gold star over an orange-red gown, holding on her left arm the Christ Child in a pale rose tunic dotted with red rosettes, who grasps her raised right hand and lifts his other hand with open palm.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child (the "Madonna Sarti"), c. 1330. Tempera, gold and silver on panel, 80 × 62 cm. Paris, Galerie G. Sarti. Provenance: London, Colnaghi (1954–1955); Oxnead Hall, Norwich (Norfolk), collection of Mrs Jeremy Harris (1971).</figcaption>
</figure>

Few panels have played so decisive a part in the recovery of an anonymous painter as this half-length Virgin and Child. It is known today by the name of the Paris dealer in whose hands it now rests, and earlier as the "Harris Madonna" after its English owners. It appeared on the London market at Colnaghi in 1954–55 and then passed to the Harris collection at Oxnead Hall in Norfolk. In 1965 Luciano Bellosi published it, together with the two small Stories of the Baptist in Bern, as a youthful work of Spinello Aretino. Within a year it was observed that the Virgin was by the same hand as the Annunciation in the Museo Diocesano of Arezzo, whose signature had not yet been discovered. In 1969 the panel was placed instead in an Aretine culture formed in the 1330s and 1340s. In 1971 it was described as the work of an Aretine painter, and it was also given, together with the Annunciation, to the Maestro del Vescovado. Bellosi himself revised his position in 1974, recognising an Aretine master distinct both from Spinello and from the Maestro del Vescovado. In the same year Anna Maria Maetzke published the hidden signature "Andrea f(ecit)" on the diocesan Annunciation, and the Virgin could at last be given to Andrea di Nerio. The attribution has not been questioned since. Between December 2015 and early 2016 the panel returned temporarily to Arezzo for an exhibition at the Casa Museo Ivan Bruschi.

The Virgin is shown at half length against a gold ground, beneath a pointed arch set within a gabled frame with flat lateral cornices. She faces the viewer almost frontally, her head slightly inclined, and her gaze passes beyond the spectator with a grave and absorbed expression. A dark mantle, marked on the shoulder with a gold star, falls over her head and shoulders and opens on an orange-red gown. It is drawn across her body in a broad curve that reveals its pale lining, rendered in small segments. On her left arm she holds the Child, who sits firmly on her open hand and turns towards her with lively attention. He wears a pale rose tunic strewn with small red rosettes and enriched with an ornamental collar and cuffs, his feet are bare, and a white cord hangs loosely from his waist. With his right hand he clasps the fingers of his mother's raised right hand, while his left hand opens in a sudden gesture of the palm, as if the movement had just been arrested.

The composition derives from the central panel of Pietro Lorenzetti's polyptych of 1320 in the Pieve di Santa Maria at Arezzo. Andrea took from it the restless Child and the luxurious treatment of the mantle, but he eliminated every reference to the throne and replaced Lorenzetti's tense lateral torsion with a calm, almost hieratic frontality. The Virgin's hand no longer grips the Child with the anxious urgency of the Sienese model but supports him with untroubled firmness. The hard grey modelling of the faces, the sharp, elongated nose and the clear-cut features point instead to the Aretine works of Buonamico Buffalmacco. The broad sweep of the mantle, whose folds are now flattened by the darkening of the blue, recalls French Gothic sculpture. The foreshortening of the hands reflects attention to Giotto's mature painting. The result is a synthesis neither Sienese nor Florentine, carried by a solemn and slightly melancholy gravity that would remain characteristic of the painter.

The physical structure of the panel bears directly on its date. It is made of two horizontal boards, and the horizontal grain shows that the lower board originally continued into lateral compartments. The work was therefore once the central element of a rectangular dossal with a raised central gable, a type characteristic of the late thirteenth century and largely abandoned in Florence by about 1330. Restoration revealed, beneath later regilding, flat pilasters bearing strips of silver leaf decorated with star patterns. The gold ground is not punched but merely grained with a point, its foliate motifs left in reserve in imitation of a precious textile; traces of these scrolls are still visible around the figures. On these grounds the gallery's catalogue entry dates the panel around 1330 and suggests that the lost lateral compartments may have contained narrative scenes rather than half-length saints. Roberto Bartalini placed the work instead in the fourth or fifth decade of the century, and a more recent study has argued for the 1340s, reading the archaic structure as a sign of Andrea's training in Buffalmacco's orbit rather than of an early execution. The date around 1330 adopted here follows the catalogue entry. A somewhat later date nevertheless remains possible, especially if the Virgin's face is compared with the Bertoldini chapel frescoes in Arezzo cathedral, generally placed soon after 1340.

Two further works have been proposed as evidence of Andrea's earliest activity, neither unanimously accepted. The first is a small panel in the Baltimore Museum of Art showing the Virgin and Child enthroned with four saints, among them the Aretine patronesses Flora and Lucilla. Its Aretine character was recognised in 1970; Bartalini listed it in 2005 among the works by then accepted for Andrea, noting traces of lateral hinges that suggest a small triptych; and the most recent study places it in the 1330s, partly on the evidence of costume. The second is a fragmentary fresco in San Domenico in Arezzo, of which only the lower part of a Saint Dominic<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a>, the head of a donor and a cosmatesque border with small griffins survive, tentatively proposed as one of Andrea's earliest works or as one in which he took part. Both proposals remain under discussion.

  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio (attributed), Virgin and Child Enthroned with Four Saints, early 14th century. Tempera on panel, 41.9 × 32.4 cm. Baltimore, Baltimore Museum of Art, Bequest of Saidie A. May, inv. 1951.392.</figcaption>
</figure>

This small gabled panel, which reached the Baltimore Museum of Art in 1951 with the bequest of Saidie A. May, belongs to the most debated margin of Andrea di Nerio's catalogue, the works proposed as evidence of his earliest activity. Its Aretine character was recognised in 1970, when the presence of Flora and Lucilla, the virgin martyrs venerated as patronesses of Arezzo, was understood to tie it to the devotional world of the city. Roberto Bartalini included it in 2005 among the works by then accepted for Andrea. He observed that traces of hinges along the lateral edges suggest that the panel was once the centre of a small triptych, whose wings, now lost, would have folded over it. The most recent study of the painter places it in the 1330s, partly on the evidence of the costume, which would make it one of the earliest surviving works associated with his name. The attribution has not been accepted by every scholar, however, and the panel should be regarded as a probable early work rather than a secure point of reference.

The Virgin sits frontally on a broad throne whose high back and projecting lateral wings form a shallow architectural enclosure against the gold ground. A white veil frames her face, and a dark mantle, lined with red, covers her shoulders and breast, while a pale cloth falls in heavy folds over her knees and down to the footstool. On her left knee sits the Child, dressed in a mauve tunic and mantle, his body turned towards the viewer and his curly head slightly inclined. With her right hand the Virgin holds a long band of pink fabric that runs towards him, and the Child holds a small white object in his hand. On either side of the throne the four saints are arranged in two tiers. Above stand two female saints in the black habit and white wimple of nuns, their hands joined in prayer. Below, in the foreground, sit two youthful haloed figures with curly hair, dressed in orange and bright red, who may be identified with Flora and Lucilla, the red of their garments alluding to martyrdom.

Despite its small size and worn surface, the panel shows the qualities that link it to Andrea's more secure early works. The Virgin's long, pale face, with its narrow nose and grave expression, recalls the Madonna Sarti, and her frontal pose and firm grip on the Child reflect the same rethinking of Pietro Lorenzetti's Aretine models. The broad, simple volumes of the drapery and the solid geometry of the throne point to a painter attentive to Giottesque spatial construction but still bound to a local, devotional idiom. The hierarchical arrangement, with the Virgin dominating small saints set in tiers beside her, suits the private or conventual use of a portable triptych. The two nuns may point to a female religious community as the original owner, although no document supports this.

## Saints on a Pier: The Pieve Fresco of Saints Francis and Dominic

Among the frescoes still in place, the figures of Saints Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a> and Dominic painted on the last pier on the left side of the Pieve di Santa Maria, near the raised presbytery, are especially significant, since they stand in the church where Andrea received his first documented commission and are almost unanimously given to him. Their date is disputed. One reading places them close to 1331, alongside the Madonna Sarti, among the first surviving works of the painter; another groups them with the works of the 1330s to 1350s in which Andrea's personal quality is most fully realised. Documents cannot decide the question, since the 1331 commission concerned a chapel and an Annunciation, not a pier, and the pier fresco may have been a separate commission. Bartalini's description of Andrea's personages, with their drooping noses and pointed profiles, fits these two saints particularly well. They show Andrea's style at the scale of fresco and in a public setting, under the eyes of the Lorenzetti polyptych that was his most important model, and their pairing of the two founders of the mendicant orders on a pier of the main parish church reflects a devotional logic familiar from other Tuscan churches.

### Saints Francis and Dominic

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-04.jpg" alt="Fresco on a round church pier showing two standing saints against a dark ground: on the left, Saint Francis in a brown hooded habit with a knotted cord, raising a hand marked with the stigmata and holding a red book; on the right, a taller Saint Dominic in a white habit and black hooded cloak, holding a sprig of lily and a red book.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio (attributed), Saints Francis and Dominic, c. 1350. Fresco. Arezzo, Pieve di Santa Maria.</figcaption>
</figure>

The two founders of the mendicant orders are painted on the curved surface of the last pier on the left side of the Pieve di Santa Maria at Arezzo, near the raised presbytery. Andrea di Nerio received his first documented commission in this church, in 1331, and the great polyptych painted by Pietro Lorenzetti for the high altar in 1320 provided one of his formative models. The attribution of the fresco to Andrea is almost unanimous, but its date is disputed. Some scholars place it close to 1331, alongside the Madonna Sarti, among the painter's earliest surviving works. Others group it with the works of the following two decades, in which his personal manner reaches its fullest expression. No document decides the question, since the commission of 1331 concerned a chapel and an Annunciation rather than a pier, and the date around 1350 given here should be read as a convenient midpoint rather than a certainty. A nineteenth-century copy by Johann Anton Ramboux in the Städel Museum at Frankfurt, catalogued as after Giotto and recording a painting on a pier of the Pieve, appears to reproduce these figures. If so, it suggests how long the fresco was read in the light of the great Florentine master.

The two saints stand side by side against a dark ground on an ochre strip of floor, framed above and below by narrow bands of geometric ornament that follow the curve of the column. On the left Saint Francis, smaller and set slightly back, wears the brown habit with the hood drawn over his head and the knotted cord at his waist. He faces the viewer frontally, his gaunt face marked by deep-set eyes and a long, straight nose. He raises his right hand to show the wound of the stigmata, holds a red book against his body with the other hand, which also bears the mark, and stands barefoot on the floor with the wounds visible on his feet. On the right Saint Dominic, taller and more imposing, turns slightly towards his companion. He wears the white habit and scapular of the Order of Preachers beneath the black cappa, whose hood frames his tonsured head and reddish beard. In his right hand he holds a slender sprig of lily, the emblem of his purity, and with his left he clasps a red book against his side.

The figures show with particular clarity the physiognomic type associated with Andrea's name: long, irregular faces of a slightly strange beauty, with drooping noses and pointed profiles, and an expression of grave, almost melancholy concentration. The broad, simple planes of the habits, modelled with restrained chiaroscuro, give the two saints a quiet monumentality well suited to their position on a pier, where they were meant to be seen at close range by the faithful moving through the nave. The pairing of Francis and Dominic in the city's principal parish church reflects a devotional formula widespread in Tuscan churches of the fourteenth century. They are presented here not as rivals but as twin pillars of the Church, an image given literal form by their place on the column itself.

## Narrative on a Small Scale: The Stories of the Baptist

The small panels with Stories of Saint John the Baptist are by common consent among Andrea's finest works and among those that first gave him a recognisable personality. Two are in the Kunstmuseum Bern, which received them through the bequest of Adolf von Stürler in 1902; in the museum's current titles they show the young John the Baptist in the wilderness and the preaching of the Baptist, painted in tempera on poplar, the first measuring 31.5 by 44.5 and the second 31.3 by 44.5 centimetres, both dated around 1350 in the checklist of the museum's 2026 exhibition *Das volle Leben. Von Duccio bis Liotard* (13 February–27 September 2026). A third panel, the Nativity of the Baptist, on poplar and measuring 29 by 39 centimetres, belonged to the Marquis Giampietro Campana in Rome, entered the Musée Napoléon III in Paris in 1862 and the Louvre in 1863, was restored in 1974–76 and has been on deposit at the Musée du Petit Palais in Avignon since 1976. The Louvre's collection database still catalogues it cautiously as "école de Andrea di Nerio", recording the former attribution to the "Maître de la Nativité de saint Jean-Baptiste", while the French national database gives it to Andrea himself around 1350. A further fragment of the series is reported in the Museo di Capodimonte in Naples, but since the French catalogue descriptions of the dispersed scenes do not exactly match the titles used in Bern, the precise distribution of the series has not been verified against the museum catalogues.

The panels are usually described as predella elements of an altarpiece dedicated to the Baptist, though the original complex has not been identified, and their dating varies between the museums' "about 1350" and an earlier placement in the 1330s and 1340s. The technical evidence of the gold ground, in which small punches begin to appear in the haloes and borders, places them after the Madonna Sarti and the dispersed saints and before the signed Annunciation. In the Nativity, the bed of Elizabeth with its striped coverlet, the attendant women with their long pointed noses and the domestic interior within a schematic architecture combine the clarity of Giottesque narrative with a popular directness linked to the local Giottesque generation and an expressive sharpness derived from Buffalmacco. The gold grounds of the Bern panels are entirely worked with incised foliate motifs and cross-hatching, a type of decoration common in Riminese painting but absent in Florence and Siena, which Andrea's interpreters have taken as a sign of the independent character of the Aretine school.

### The Nativity of Saint John the Baptist

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-05.jpg" alt="Small gold-ground panel of the birth of Saint John the Baptist: the aged Elizabeth sits up in a bed with a striped coverlet while two attendants bring her a bowl and a jug; in the foreground the Virgin in a blue mantle holds the haloed newborn John, who reaches towards an old woman seated opposite; on the right a pale Gothic building opens the room.">
  <figcaption>The Nativity of Saint John the Baptist, c. 1350. Tempera and gold on panel, 29 × 39 cm. Paris, Musée du Louvre (MI 385), on deposit at the Musée du Petit Palais, Avignon.</figcaption>
</figure>

This small panel belongs to the series of Stories of Saint John the Baptist that played a central part in the reconstruction of Andrea di Nerio's oeuvre. Two companion scenes are in the Kunstmuseum Bern, and the three probably formed the predella of a lost altarpiece dedicated to the Baptist. The panel belonged to the Marquis Giampietro Campana in Rome, entered the Musée Napoléon III in Paris in 1862 and the Louvre in 1863. It was restored between 1974 and 1976 and has since been on deposit at the Petit Palais in Avignon. Its critical history mirrors that of the painter himself. Inventoried at first as a work of the school of Giotto, it later gave its name to an anonymous "Master of the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist", invented around 1970 to account for a small group of related works. Once the signature of Andrea di Nerio had been discovered on the Annunciation of the Museo Diocesano of Arezzo, the Avignon catalogue of 1976–77 recognised the Nativity as the work closest to that signed panel, and the anonymous master was absorbed into Andrea's catalogue. The French national database now gives the panel to Andrea di Nerio, while the Louvre's collection database still catalogues it as "école de Andrea di Nerio".

The birth of the Baptist is set in a domestic interior of disarming clarity. On the left, beneath a scalloped valance and against a red wall hanging sprinkled with blue motifs, the aged Elizabeth sits up in a broad bed with a striped coverlet. Her head is wrapped in a white veil and her face is lined with age. She extends her hands towards a young attendant who brings her a bowl of broth, while a second woman approaches with a jug and a towel draped over her arm. In the foreground the Virgin, in a pale blue mantle marked with a star on the shoulder, is seated on the floor holding the newborn John, who stretches eagerly towards an old woman seated opposite, wrapped in a cream mantle, who holds out a small object to him. The presence of Mary follows the tradition, recorded in the Golden Legend<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-jacopo-da-varagine" class="footnote"><sup>10</sup></a>, that she remained with her kinswoman until the birth and was the first to take the child in her arms. On the right a pale building of Gothic arches, windows and an inner staircase opens the room onto the outside world and lends the scene a measured depth.

The painting combines the narrative lucidity of the Giottesque tradition with a popular directness and an expressive sharpness of its own. The women's long, pointed noses and narrow faces, the hard, clear modelling and the attentive rendering of domestic objects are characteristic of the works now gathered under Andrea's name. In the haloes, small punch marks begin to enrich the tooling of the gold, a technique that becomes increasingly important in his later panels and culminates in the signed Annunciation. The scene is generally dated around 1350, in line with the Bern panels, although some scholars place the series somewhat earlier, in the 1330s or 1340s.

### The Sermon of Saint John the Baptist

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-06.jpg" alt="Small gold-ground panel: Saint John the Baptist, barefoot in a camel-hair garment and rose mantle, stands at the centre holding a slender cross-staff and points to a white lamb with a red-cross banner on a hill at left, while a group of bearded listeners in red, yellow and pale robes gathers at right.">
  <figcaption>The Sermon of Saint John the Baptist, c. 1350. Tempera and gold on poplar panel, 31.3 × 44.5 cm. Bern, Kunstmuseum, Bequest of Adolf von Stürler, Versailles (inv. G 0882).</figcaption>
</figure>

This small panel and its companion in the same museum, showing the young Baptist in the wilderness, are among the works that first gave Andrea di Nerio a recognisable artistic personality. Both entered the Kunstmuseum Bern in 1902 with the bequest of Adolf von Stürler. They belong to a dispersed series of Stories of Saint John the Baptist, together with the Nativity of the Baptist now on deposit from the Louvre at the Petit Palais in Avignon, and probably formed the predella of a lost altarpiece dedicated to the saint. In 1965 Luciano Bellosi published the two Bern panels as youthful works of Spinello Aretino. Within a few years they were recognised as the work of an older Aretine painter formed in the 1330s and 1340s, and after the discovery of the signature on the Annunciation of the Museo Diocesano of Arezzo they found their place in Andrea di Nerio's catalogue. The museum dates them around 1350, while some scholars prefer a date in the 1330s or 1340s.

Although catalogued as the Sermon of the Baptist, the panel represents a precise moment of his preaching: the scene in which John points out Christ to his listeners as the Lamb of God, according to the Gospel of John. The Baptist stands at the centre on the bare ground of the wilderness, barefoot, wearing his shaggy garment of camel hair beneath a rose mantle thrown over his shoulders. In his left hand he holds a slender cross-staff, and with his right arm stretched out he points towards a hill on the left. On the crest of the hill, beside a single slender tree, stands a white lamb bearing a banner with a red cross, the emblem of Christ's sacrifice and resurrection. On the right a tightly packed group of listeners turns towards the saint: bearded elders in red and yellow mantles and a younger man in a pale robe and red cap, who holds a small red object, follow his gesture with grave attention.

The composition is reduced to a few elements set against the gold ground: the sweeping curve of the hill, the isolated figure of the Baptist and the compact mass of the crowd. The empty space between preacher and listeners gives the scene a sense of expectancy and concentrates attention on John's commanding gesture. The gold is not punched but entirely worked with incised foliate motifs and cross-hatching. This type of decoration is common in Riminese painting but absent in Florence and Siena, and it has been taken as a sign of the independent character of Aretine practice. The elongated faces, sharp profiles and grave expressions of the figures are characteristic of the works now gathered under Andrea's name.

This panel is the companion of the Sermon of Saint John the Baptist in the same museum, and like it entered the Kunstmuseum Bern in 1902 with the bequest of Adolf von Stürler. Together with the Nativity of the Baptist now on deposit at the Petit Palais in Avignon, the two Bern scenes belong to a dispersed series of Stories of Saint John, probably the predella of a lost altarpiece dedicated to the saint. First published as youthful works of Spinello Aretino and later recognised as the work of an older Aretine master, they have been part of Andrea di Nerio's catalogue since the discovery of his signature on the Annunciation of the Museo Diocesano of Arezzo. The museum dates them around 1350.

The scene illustrates an episode from the legendary childhood of the Baptist, who according to tradition withdrew to the desert while still a boy, to prepare himself for his mission in solitude and penance. The story is told with tenderness and economy. On the right a tall angel in a salmon-pink robe patterned with small gold roundels, his blue-grey wings half folded, holds the young John by the hand. The child, haloed and dressed in a dark tunic, looks up at his guide, while the angel bends his head towards him and stretches out his other arm to show the way into the wilderness. On the left, at the edge of a grove of slender trees with rounded crowns, a second angel in a pale robe sprinkled with dots stands waiting. He holds over his arm the shaggy garment of camel hair that John will wear for the rest of his life, the sign of the ascetic existence that awaits him.

As in the Sermon, the composition is built on a few figures set against the gold ground, separated by a broad, empty stretch of grey-green ground that suggests the distance the child must cross. The contrast between the warm, richly patterned robe of the guiding angel, the pale figure of the waiting angel and the small dark silhouette of the boy gives the scene a quiet narrative clarity. The gold ground is worked with fine incised decoration rather than punched, in keeping with the Aretine practice that distinguishes these panels from contemporary Florentine and Sienese painting. The long, delicate faces of the angels and their grave, attentive expressions are characteristic of the works gathered under Andrea's name.

## A Dispersed Altarpiece of Standing Saints

A significant part of Andrea's surviving panel work consists of standing saints cut from one or more altarpieces or tabernacles and now dispersed. A Saint Gregory the Great<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gregory-the-great" class="footnote"><sup>11</sup></a> formerly in the Chalandon collection at Parcieux, near Lyon, had been known since the early 1960s and was discussed in 1971; a Saint Bishop was identified in the Czech Republic and is now in the museum at Olomouc; and four further panels of similar presentation, Saint John the Evangelist, Saint John the Baptist, Saint James the Greater and Saint Michael, once belonged to the Baroni collection in Paris. According to the published reconstruction, the two Saints John are now of unknown location, the Saint James has entered the Alana collection in Newark, Delaware, and the Saint Michael the collection of Lisa De Carlo in Florence; the Saint Gregory, measuring 56.5 by 31.3 centimetres and still with the heirs of the collector Albin Chalandon until then, was sold as lot 1 at the Crait + Müller sale at the Hôtel Drouot in Paris on 13 December 2024, where, against an estimate of 10,000–15,000 euros, it was knocked down for 42,000 euros. Each saint stands before a gold ground beneath a painted trilobed arch with spandrels once decorated with an acanthus motif, and because that motif was sometimes cut in half when the complex was dismantled, the panels could be reassembled in pairs: the two Saints John, Saint James with Saint Michael, and Saint Gregory with the Saint Bishop.

The Saint Gregory, showing the pope in Benedictine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedictine-order" class="footnote"><sup>12</sup></a> habit beneath his tiara, blessing with a gloved hand and holding the book, displays the same combination of monumentality and refinement as the Madonna Sarti and the same grained rather than punched gold ground, which is why the series is usually placed in the 1330s, before the Stories of the Baptist; proposed dates nevertheless range from around 1330 to mid-century. Whether the panels formed a single polyptych or, as the pairing might suggest, two tabernacle wings cannot be decided on present evidence. The ensemble shows in miniature what has happened to Aretine Trecento painting as a whole, whose reconstruction depends on photographs, sale catalogues and private collections as much as on public museums.

## Fresco Painting in the Cathedral: The Bertoldini Chapel and the Vaults of 1341

In the cathedral of Arezzo, on the right wall of the nave, the chapel of the Bertoldini family preserves a fresco of the Virgin and Child flanked by stories of Saints Christopher and James the Greater; some descriptions call it the Spadari chapel, a name whose origin has not been verified in the scholarly literature. Maetzke attributed the fresco to Andrea in 1974, the attribution has been generally accepted, and the work is dated shortly after 1340, the year of the contract for the chapel's sculptural apparatus. The narrative scenes are arranged in compartments on either side of the central image, with two further episodes in the lunette. A study of Tuscan hagiographic frescoes has observed that this layout suited the need to divide the space symmetrically between the stories of two saints, and that Spinello later adopted it in a fresco devoted to Saint Christopher alone, where symmetry was no longer necessary, suggesting a deliberate continuity with his predecessor's model.

Examination of the Bertoldini frescoes from scaffolding has revealed decorative passages in metal leaf, now altered and darkened, a preciousness in fresco regarded as typical of Andrea and his circle and recurring in the frescoes of the Master of Pieve a Sietina. Andrea's taste for precious materials, already visible in the silver strips of the Madonna Sarti, thus extended to his work on the wall, which suggests that the distinction often drawn between his refined panels and supposedly coarser frescoes may owe more to conservation than to quality. The vaults of 1341, painted with Balduccio di Cecco on the model of Buffalmacco's arches, are lost, but the commission shows that by then Andrea was one of the painters to whom the cathedral turned for decorative work of public importance. Around mid-century he was, in the words of the most recent study, "il pittore più famoso e autorevole" in Arezzo.

  <figcaption>Virgin and Child with Stories of Saints Christopher and James the Greater, 1340–60. Fresco. Arezzo, Cathedral of Santi Pietro e Donato, Cappella Bertoldini.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fresco decorates the chapel of the Bertoldini family on the right wall of the nave of Arezzo cathedral, a space sometimes also called the Spadari chapel. Anna Maria Maetzke attributed it to Andrea di Nerio in 1974, and the attribution has been generally accepted. The work is usually dated shortly after 1340, the year of the contract for the chapel's sculptural apparatus, although a broader span reaching into the following decades cannot be excluded. It is the principal surviving fresco securely associated with the painter, and it shows Andrea at work in the cathedral where, in 1341, he and Balduccio di Cecco were commissioned to paint vaults on the model of Buffalmacco's arches in the bishop's chapel. The composition is organised around a central image of the Virgin and Child, flanked by narrative compartments devoted to two saints, with two further episodes in the lunette above. This layout suited the need to divide the space symmetrically between the stories of two protagonists. Spinello Aretino later adopted it for a fresco devoted to Saint Christopher alone, where symmetry was no longer required, a sign of the continuity between the two painters.

At the centre, within a tall Gothic tabernacle with a trilobed arch and winged seraph heads in the spandrels, the Virgin sits enthroned before a red cloth of honour. She wears a blue gown beneath a white mantle strewn with small clusters of dots, and she holds on her lap the Child, dressed in an ochre tunic, who presses close to her and clasps the edge of her gown. Her long, pale face, gently inclined towards him, has the grave composure characteristic of Andrea's Madonnas. The throne rests on a stepped platform decorated with lozenges.

The stories of Saint Christopher occupy the left side. In the lunette the giant carries the Christ Child on his shoulders across the river, leaning on his staff as the water swirls about his legs. Below, he stands bound to a stake while arrows fly towards him and hang harmlessly in the air around his body, as the legend recounts, the hand of an archer just visible at the left edge. In the lowest compartment, before a group of soldiers in armour, his beheaded body lies stretched on the ground in his red tunic. The stories of Saint James the Greater fill the right side. The lunette shows a crowded scene in which a young man in yellow leads away a captive while a figure kneels before a haloed saint in a red mantle; it probably relates to the saint's arrest or to the conversion of his accuser Josias on the way to execution. Below, an executioner raises his sword beside a soldier in armour, while a haloed saint kneels in prayer next to a decapitated body whose haloed head lies on the ground. In the lowest compartment the saint's disciples lay his body in a boat, the episode of the translation of his remains, above which appears a court scene with a ruler enthroned before a hanging patterned with fleurs-de-lis.

The narratives are set in rocky, sparsely wooded landscapes and simple architectural settings, with figures of slender proportions and lively, legible gestures. Examination of the frescoes from scaffolding has revealed decorative passages executed in metal leaf, now altered and darkened. This preciousness, unusual in fresco and typical of Andrea and his circle, recalls the silver strips of the Madonna Sarti. Despite losses, especially in the lunette and along the edges, the chapel preserves the most complete idea of Andrea di Nerio as a painter of narrative cycles on the wall.

## The Maestro del Vescovado and the Question of Identity

The relationship between Andrea di Nerio and the Maestro del Vescovado is one of the most delicate problems of Aretine Trecento painting. The Maestro del Vescovado takes his name from the fresco of the Crucifixion with the Virgin, Saint John, Saint Michael and Saint Francis, with the kneeling Ciuccio Tarlati, in the marble chapel commissioned in 1334 in the cathedral, Vasari's "Vescovado"; a triptych from San Domenico in Arezzo is given to the same hand. His art, like Andrea's, grows from the Tarlati context and from the lessons of Lorenzetti and Buffalmacco, and the two personalities are evidently close.

The literature has taken three positions. The first, advanced in 1971 before the signature was known, gave the Harris Madonna and the Annunciation to the Maestro del Vescovado and so effectively merged the two painters. The second treats the Maestro del Vescovado, the Master of Pieve a Sietina and Andrea as stages of a single career. The third, articulated in Bartalini's 1996 study and since broadly prevalent, treats them as distinct personalities: the Maestro del Vescovado close to Andrea and active in parallel with him in the 1330s and 1340s, and the Master of Pieve a Sietina a secondary artist in Andrea's orbit, named after a small group of frescoes in the Pieve of Santa Maria Maddalena at Sietina that includes a Virgin and Child datable around 1368. Bellosi had already pointed in this direction in 1974. It has also been suggested that the Maestro del Vescovado might be Balduccio di Cecco, Andrea's partner of 1341, an attractive idea that would give a name to an anonymous master and a body of work to a documented one. Yet no document links Balduccio to the Ciuccio Tarlati chapel or to any surviving painting, and the proposal has rightly been called suggestive but undocumentable. The most defensible position is that the two were distinct but closely related painters, both formed in Tarlati Arezzo.

## The Workshop and the Late Years: The Dispersed Altar Frontal

From about mid-century the works associated with Andrea show increasing variation in quality, which scholars generally attribute to a growing workshop; one study states that in the second half of the century the contribution of collaborators "aumenta verticalmente". The most important document of this phase is a group of small panels from a dismantled altar frontal devoted to the Virgin and the Passion of Christ. Reconstructed in 1970, according to the account published in *La Nazione* on 5 December 2021, as a frontal of "nove storie sormontate da tre piccole cuspidi" and dated to the 1380s, it was reconsidered in 1985 in the catalogue of the Martello collection, which altered the narrative sequence, moved the date back to 1365–75 and assigned the complex to Andrea's workshop; the most recent discussion has suggested that the young Spinello may have taken part.

Three of these panels, the Way to Calvary, the Crucifixion and the Deposition, were bought at Düsseldorf in 1931 as works of Taddeo Gaddi by the textile industrialist Carl Neumann, remained with his heirs, and were offered through the Munich auction house Karl & Faber. On the initiative of the regional museums directorate of Tuscany they were acquired by the Italian Ministry of Culture for the Museo Nazionale d'Arte Medievale e Moderna in Arezzo and presented on 24 October 2021 in the museum's Sala delle Muse, with speakers from the Direzione regionale Musei nazionali Toscana, the museum, the auction house and the restorer of the panels, having previously been known only through old photographs in the Kunsthistorisches Institut in Florenz. The official presentation described them as "attribuite dagli studiosi ad Andrea di Nerio", appropriate wording for works whose degree of autograph execution is still debated.

Several frescoes in Arezzo and its territory belong to the same zone of workshop production. The most recent survey discusses Annunciations in street tabernacles and private houses, including one in Via Cavour near Spinello's house, in which the most careful passage, usually the head of the Virgin, contrasts with cursory execution elsewhere. A fresco of the Christ of the Passion detached from a niche in San Bartolomeo, now in the Museo Nazionale, is associated with Andrea's circle, whereas the Stories of Saint Thomas in San Bartolomeo, sometimes cited in general literature as Andrea's narrative frescoes, are now more often given to the young Spinello, a discrepancy between older reference works and specialist literature of which readers should be aware.

## Master and Pupil: Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino

Much of Andrea's modern visibility derives from his relationship with Spinello Aretino, son of the Aretine goldsmith Luca di Spinello, born around mid-century, first documented as a painter in 1373, and author of the earliest firmly dated work in his catalogue, the detached fresco of 1377 with the Virgin and Child between Saints James the Greater and Anthony Abbot from the tomb of Clemente Pucci in the cloister of Sant'Agostino, now in the diocesan museum. The assertion that Andrea was Spinello's teacher comes not from Vasari but from the modern recognition that Spinello's earliest works grow directly out of Andrea's art, together with the reported marriage of Niccolò Spinelli to Rebecca. The entry on Spinello in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (vol. 93, 2018) states that the rediscovery of Andrea, "principale artista cittadino del secondo e terzo quarto del Trecento", furnished the premises for understanding the origins of Spinello's language, and notes that the Madonna of Spinello's Lucchese period painted for Puccio di Landuccio, master of works of Pisa cathedral, now in Stuttgart, is still marked by Andrea's "geometrica volumetria".

The difficulty lies in a group of Aretine frescoes given alternately to master and pupil: the Baptism of Christ and the fragmentary Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine in San Francesco, the Virgin and Child in Santi Michele e Adriano, and the Annunciation of the outer tabernacle of the Santissima Annunziata. For the two frescoes in San Francesco and the Madonna of Santi Michele e Adriano, Bellosi, in his 2005 reconsideration of Spinello's early works, and Bartalini proposed Andrea, whereas others regard them as early works of Spinello shortly before the fresco of 1377, arguing that the elongation and immobile composure of the figures introduce a tension foreign to Andrea. For the Annunziata tabernacle the positions are reversed: Bellosi, Bartalini and several others give it to Andrea, Maetzke and the most recent specialist study to Spinello, while a proposal of 2009 assigns all three frescoes to master and pupil together. One study has argued that the San Francesco frescoes, whoever painted them, cannot be later than about 1373 for reasons connected with the history of the wall. The disagreement cannot be resolved here. What it demonstrates is the closeness of the two languages in Arezzo around 1370, when the elderly Andrea presumably still led a workshop in which his presumed pupil was forming an independent manner. That convergence makes the master–pupil relationship highly plausible, but no apprenticeship contract or record of collaboration has been published, and it remains the strongest available explanation of the evidence rather than a documented fact.

### The Baptism of Christ and the Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-09.jpg" alt="Fresco in two fields on a dark red ground: on the left, Christ stands in the Jordan with arms crossed over his chest while John the Baptist in a rose mantle raises his arm above his head and two angels in white hold his red garment; on the right, the Virgin sits on a gilded Gothic throne and the Christ Child on her lap places a ring on the finger of Saint Catherine, who kneels in a white mantle.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino (attributed), The Baptism of Christ and The Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, c. 1370. Fresco. Arezzo, San Francesco, Cappella Gozzari.</figcaption>
</figure>

The two scenes form the upper register of the decoration of the Cappella Gozzari in San Francesco at Arezzo, directly above the Stigmatisation of Saint Francis, whose red-winged seraph can still be glimpsed below the lower border. Few Aretine frescoes of the fourteenth century have been so persistently contested. Luciano Bellosi, in his 2005 reconsideration of Spinello's early works, gave both scenes to Andrea di Nerio, and Roberto Bartalini shared that view. Other scholars regard them instead as early works of Spinello Aretino, painted shortly before the fresco of 1377 from the tomb of Clemente Pucci, the earliest firmly dated work in his catalogue. They argue that the elongation and immobile composure of the figures introduce a tension foreign to the older master. A proposal of 2009 resolved the dilemma by assigning the frescoes to master and pupil working together, and one study has argued, on grounds connected with the history of the wall, that they cannot be later than about 1373. Whoever held the brush, the paintings document with unusual clarity the moment around 1370 when the languages of Andrea and Spinello converge almost to the point of identity.

The Baptism unfolds against a plain dark red ground. Christ stands frontally in the waters of the Jordan, which rise in a greenish stream between rocky banks to cover his legs. He wears only a white loincloth, and his arms are crossed over his chest in a gesture of humility. From the right John the Baptist advances barefoot, his rose mantle thrown over the shaggy garment of camel hair visible at the hem, and stretches his right arm high above Christ's head. On the left two angels in white robes, one of them with great dark wings, stand ready with Christ's red mantle and a white cloth. The figures are tall and slender, the bodies modelled in pale, translucent tones, and the composition is reduced to a few large shapes set against the flat ground, giving the scene a solemn, almost suspended quality.

In the adjoining field the Virgin sits on a monumental Gothic throne of gilded architecture, crowned by a gable and flanked by pinnacled turrets. She wears a green gown under a dark mantle that falls in broad folds over her knees. On her lap the Child, in a red tunic, turns towards the right and places the ring on the finger of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, who kneels in a white mantle and extends her hand, her head bowed with grave attention. The Virgin inclines her head towards the gesture, and the three figures are bound together by the line of their joined hands. Several lacunae interrupt the lower part of the scene, but the composition remains clear.

The two fields are framed above by a frieze of acanthus scrolls in which diamond-shaped panels enclose small heads, and below by a band of geometric cosmatesque ornament. That band is the same pattern that frames the Stigmatisation and Saint Michael below, confirming that the scenes belong to a single decorative programme.

### The Virgin and Saint Francis Comforting Saint John of Damascus in Prison

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-10.jpg" alt="Damaged fresco: on the left, Saint Francis in a brown habit stands in an arched niche, holding a red book and raising a hand marked with the stigmata; on the right, in a cell with walls of square panels and a narrow window, the Virgin in a dark mantle sits on a gilded Gothic throne and gestures towards Saint John of Damascus, seated in a lilac robe at a desk with an open book, his face lost to damage.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino (attributed), The Virgin and Saint Francis Comforting Saint John of Damascus in Prison, c. 1370. Fresco. Arezzo, San Francesco, Cappella Gozzari.</figcaption>
</figure>

This scene belongs to the upper register of the decoration of the Cappella Gozzari in San Francesco at Arezzo. It is framed above by the same frieze of acanthus scrolls with diamond-shaped panels enclosing small heads that crowns the Baptism and the Mystic Marriage, and below by the familiar band of geometric cosmatesque ornament. Like the other frescoes of the chapel it lies in the zone of Aretine painting disputed between Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino, and it is here given to both painters with the same caution. The subject is rare. It is identified as the Virgin and Saint Francis comforting Saint John of Damascus<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-john-of-damascus" class="footnote"><sup>13</sup></a> in prison, and it probably relates to the legend of the eighth-century theologian and defender of images. According to that tradition he was punished by the caliph on the strength of a forged letter, and his severed right hand was miraculously restored by the Virgin. The presence of Saint Francis, unknown to that legend, must reflect the Franciscan context of the chapel. The precise meaning of the scene, however, deserves further study.

The composition is divided into two unequal fields. On the left, within a narrow arched compartment lined with a patterned wall, stands Saint Francis in his brown habit girded with the cord. He holds a red book against his breast and raises his right hand, displaying the wound of the stigmata, which is also visible on his other hand. His gaunt face and steady gaze give him the character of a witness or intercessor rather than a participant in the action. On the right the scene opens into a closed chamber of striking geometric regularity. The walls are covered with square panels set with pink and green lozenges, the ceiling is painted with a lattice of red diamonds, and a single narrow window, deeply recessed, pierces the back wall. This is the cell in which the saint is confined. The Virgin, in a dark mantle and veil, is seated on a tall gilded throne with a pointed gable and arms carved with foliate scrolls. She turns towards the prisoner and extends her right hand in a gesture of consolation. Opposite her, behind a desk decorated with a band of inlay, sits Saint John of Damascus in a lilac mantle, an open book before him. He raises his hand to his chin in a pose of meditation or grief. His face is now lost in a lacuna, though traces of the beard and halo remain.

Several large losses interrupt the lower part of the scene and the figure of Francis, but the composition remains readable. The careful construction of the interior, with its foreshortened ceiling, receding walls and solid furniture, shows a painter fully at ease with the Giottesque tradition of measurable space. The elongated, quietly solemn figures correspond closely to those of the Baptism and the Mystic Marriage on the adjoining wall. The decorative richness of the throne, the inlaid desk and the patterned walls reflects the taste for ornament characteristic of Aretine painting in the circle of Andrea di Nerio.

### The Stigmatisation of Saint Francis

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-11.jpg" alt="Damaged fresco of a wooded mountain landscape: at left, Saint Francis kneels before a small church with a bell-cote and raised hands, his head lost to damage; a red-winged crucified seraph appears in the sky at upper right, and a friar at a doorway on the right shields his eyes; a woman in black kneels in prayer at lower left, and two circular consecration crosses are painted over the sky.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino (attributed), The Stigmatisation of Saint Francis, second half of the 14th century. Fresco. Arezzo, San Francesco, Cappella Gozzari.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fresco belongs to the fragmentary decoration of the Cappella Gozzari in the church of San Francesco at Arezzo, the chapel that today houses the sarcophagus of the Blessed Benedetto Sinigardi, one of the first companions of Saint Francis. Together with the standing Saint Michael, painted in a separate field beside it and not shown in this image, the scene is usually grouped with the frescoes of the chapel ascribed jointly to Andrea di Nerio and to Spinello Aretino. The attribution should be treated with caution. The frescoes of San Francesco lie in the zone of Aretine painting that scholarship has long disputed between the older master and his presumed pupil, and the image of Saint Michael has also been catalogued under Spinello's name alone. A joint attribution would fit what is known of Aretine workshop practice in the decades around 1370, when the two painters' languages converge so closely that individual hands can be hard to separate. In the present state of research, however, it remains a hypothesis rather than an established result.

The narrative unfolds across a wide landscape, framed above by a band of geometric cosmatesque ornament and below by a painted marble dado. On the left, before a small whitewashed church with a bell-cote and a dark arched doorway, Saint Francis kneels on a ledge of rock in his brown habit girded with the knotted cord. He lifts his open hands towards the vision. His head and upper body are lost in a large lacuna, but the upward thrust of the figure remains legible. In the sky at upper right the seraph appears, the crucified Christ borne on spreading red wings, his body stretched horizontally above the trees. On the far right, at the door of a second building, a friar shades his eyes with his hand as he looks up at the apparition. This is evidently Brother Leo, the companion who in the Franciscan sources witnesses the miracle on La Verna.

The setting is one of the most attractive passages of the cycle. The mountain is evoked as a dense wood of slender trunks and rounded crowns, threaded with paths and opening onto a wooden hut in the distance. Birds rest among the branches, and small wild animals move across the clearings. In the foreground the rocky ground breaks into a deep cleft, recalling the fissured stone of La Verna. Below Francis, at the lower left edge, kneels a small female donor in a black mantle, her hands joined in prayer; her identity is not recorded. Two consecration crosses in circular fields were painted over the sky at a later date.

Saint Michael, not shown here, occupies a separate field to the left of the scene (see the next figure). He stands within a Gothic niche crowned by pinnacles and framed by twisted columns. He wears armour beneath a rose mantle lined with green, his wings golden, and he holds a drawn sword across his body. His calm, frontal pose and broad, softly modelled drapery give the figure a monumentality that contrasts with the narrative animation of the landscape beside it. At his feet a second small donor kneels in prayer, so that the two painted fields together record the devotion of the chapel's patrons.

### Saint Michael the Archangel

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-12.jpg" alt="Fresco of Saint Michael the Archangel standing frontally in a painted Gothic niche with pinnacles and twisted columns, wearing pale blue armour under a rose mantle lined with green, with large golden wings, holding a long sword diagonally across his body; a small donor in red with a white headdress kneels in prayer at his feet.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino (attributed), Saint Michael the Archangel, c. 1370. Fresco. 254 x 103 cm. Arezzo, San Francesco, Cappella Gozzari.</figcaption>
</figure>

The figure of Saint Michael occupies a tall narrow field at the left of the lower register of the Cappella Gozzari, immediately beside the Stigmatisation of Saint Francis, with which it shares the cosmatesque border above and the painted marble dado below. Like the rest of the chapel's decoration it lies in the zone of Aretine painting disputed between Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino. The photographic archive of the Fondazione Zeri catalogues a Saint Michael in San Francesco under Spinello's name, while the joint attribution to both painters reflects the view that the chapel was decorated by master and pupil working side by side around 1370. Neither position has been settled. The figure is nevertheless among the best preserved and most accomplished passages of the ensemble.

The archangel stands within a painted Gothic tabernacle, an illusionistic niche whose trilobed arch is crowned by a gable with foliate crockets and flanked by slender pinnacles. Twisted columns and mosaic-patterned pilasters frame it at the sides, and the painted vault recedes into depth behind it. Against the green ground of the niche Michael appears frontally, standing on a red floor. His face is oval and serene, framed by curling golden hair and a broad halo, and his gaze is direct and calm. He wears a pale blue cuirass with a gilded collar over sleeves of the same steely blue. His legs, sheathed in plate armour, emerge beneath a voluminous rose mantle lined with green, which falls from his left shoulder and is gathered across his body in broad, heavy folds. Great golden wings, shading into green and violet at the tips, open behind his shoulders. He holds a long sword diagonally across his body, gripping the hilt low with his right hand and steadying the blade near its point with his left.

The archangel is shown without the dragon, as a calm guardian rather than a combatant. His protective role is made explicit by the small kneeling donor at his feet, a man in a red garment and white headdress who joins his hands in prayer and looks up at the saint. His counterpart is the woman in black kneeling in the adjacent Stigmatisation, and together the two figures record the devotion of the chapel's patrons, whose identity is not documented. The ample, softly modelled drapery, the slightly elongated proportions and the quiet monumentality of the pose correspond closely to the figures of the Baptism and the Mystic Marriage in the register above. They also explain why the boundary between Andrea's late manner and Spinello's early one has proved so difficult to draw in this chapel.

### The Annunciation of the Cappella Gozzari

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Andrea-di-Nerio/andrea-di-nerio-13.jpg" alt="Fresco of the Annunciation within painted Gothic architecture: at left, the angel Gabriel in a lilac mantle with green and gold wings arrives with his hand on his breast; at centre, a vase with a flowering plant stands on a red tiled floor; at right, the Virgin in a blue mantle stands with crossed hands beneath a Gothic canopy beside a lectern with an open book; above, a divine figure in a glory of red seraphim sends a kneeling angel.">
  <figcaption>Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino (attributed), The Annunciation, c. 1370. Fresco. Arezzo, San Francesco, Cappella Gozzari.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Annunciation is one of the best preserved and most ambitious scenes of the Cappella Gozzari in San Francesco at Arezzo. It is framed above by the band of geometric cosmatesque ornament that runs through the chapel and below by a painted cornice. Like the rest of the decoration it lies in the zone of Aretine painting disputed between Andrea di Nerio and Spinello Aretino, and it is given here to both painters with the same caution. Its interest for the question of authorship is considerable, because the composition takes up, on the monumental scale of fresco, the scheme of the signed Annunciation by Andrea di Nerio now in the Museo Diocesano of Arezzo. Above the main scene a divine figure, seated in a glory of red seraphim, entrusts a small kneeling angel with his mission. Below, the two protagonists are set in adjoining architectural spaces divided by a slender twisted column. Whether the fresco is a late work of Andrea or an early work of his pupil, it shows how closely the formulas of the master's workshop were carried forward in the generation that followed.

The scene unfolds within an elaborate painted architecture of Gothic structures with gables, pinnacles and crenellated walls. On the left Gabriel alights in the forecourt of the Virgin's house, still borne forward by the impetus of his flight: his lilac mantle billows behind him, and his great wings, golden at the shoulders and shading into green and violet, are still half spread. He wears a pale blue tunic with a gilded collar and armbands. He lays one hand on his breast in a gesture of reverence and stretches the other towards Mary. Behind him a loggia with two-light openings gives onto a garden of dark trees. At the centre, on a floor patterned with red tiles, stands a slender vase holding a leafy plant with small flowers, the traditional emblem of the Virgin's purity. Beside it a small painted shield, now illegible, probably once bore the arms of the chapel's patrons.

On the right the Virgin stands beneath a richly decorated Gothic baldachin whose arch is fringed with cusps and whose gable encloses a roundel with a bearded half-length figure. Behind her rises the gabled back of her throne, and before her a lectern holds an open book. She wears a rose gown under a deep blue mantle edged with gold and lined with yellow, and she crosses her hands on her breast with her head bowed in humble acceptance of the message. The tall, slender proportions of the two figures, the calm gravity of their faces and the rhythmic fall of the drapery give the scene a solemn, almost ceremonial quality. The decorative richness of the architecture, the tiles and the furnishings reflects the taste for ornament that runs through Aretine painting in the circle of Andrea di Nerio.

## Style, Technique and Iconography

Considered together, the works most securely given to Andrea, namely the Madonna Sarti, the dispersed saints, the Stories of the Baptist, the Pieve saints, the Bertoldini frescoes and the signed Annunciation, reveal a coherent artistic physiognomy. The most recent attempt to define what distinguishes his personal participation from his workshop's names quality, harmony, preciousness of materials and of drawing, decorative care and narrative eloquence. To these one may add a particular emotional key, a solemn and melancholy gravity expressed above all in the gaze of his Virgins, which in the Madonna Sarti seems lost in space, as if veiled by foreknowledge of the Child's fate.

The formal sources of this style have been identified with reasonable consistency. From Lorenzetti's Pieve polyptych Andrea took the types of the Virgin and Child, the restless movement of the Child, the luxurious material details and, directly or through the local tradition it founded, the Annunciation with the angel's wing still outside the room. From Buffalmacco he took the hard chiaroscuro, the sharp features and certain graphic habits, such as the eyebrow drawn "a piccoli tratti obliqui, simili a cuciture", which Bellosi had identified as characteristic of Buffalmacco in the Camposanto and which survives in Andrea in attenuated form, sometimes on only the more brightly lit brow. From Florentine painting of the 1330s, from Giotto's late works and the circle of Maso di Banco and Bernardo Daddi, he drew a sense of measurable volume and calm narrative clarity, although recent analysis regards the influence of Maso as overestimated in older literature and comparatively late.

Andrea's technique shows a distinctive local character. The gold grounds of his early panels are grained and decorated with foliate motifs in reserve or incised with cross-hatching rather than punched, closer to Riminese than to Florentine or Sienese practice, while punch tooling appears progressively from the Stories of the Baptist and becomes important in the Annunciation. Silver leaf in the frame of the Madonna Sarti and metal leaf in the Bertoldini frescoes reveal a taste for precious materials that persisted in his circle, and cosmatesque geometric decoration recurs in his painted architecture and borders; its disappearance from the interior architecture of Spinello's later Annunciation in San Domenico has been read as one sign of the pupil's departure from the master's model.

Iconographically, Andrea's surviving works belong almost entirely to the devotional repertoire of an episcopal city with a strong mendicant and confraternal life: the Virgin and Child; the Annunciation, a subject of special importance in Arezzo; the founders of the mendicant orders; the Baptist; the saints of the city and of the Benedictine tradition, among them Flora and Lucilla and Gregory the Great in Benedictine habit; and the Passion of Christ. His known patrons were the clergy of the Pieve, the cathedral operai, a patrician family, the Bertoldini, and probably, if the hypothesis about the Annunciation is accepted, a lay confraternity, a range that corresponds closely to the social structure of post-Tarlati Arezzo. There is no evidence of patronage by the Tarlati themselves, although Andrea's art was formed in the climate they had created.

## Historiographical Fortune and the Present State of Research

Andrea di Nerio's historiographical fortune falls into three phases. In the first, from Vasari to the early twentieth century, he was a name without works, mentioned in documents published by Aretine archival scholarship and otherwise absorbed into the figure of Spinello, a situation reinforced by Vasari's denial of any Aretine painting between Margarito and Spinello and by the tendency of nineteenth-century connoisseurship to read provincial Trecento painting as a reflection of Florence and Siena. In the second, from 1965 to 1974, works now given to him were gathered under other names, first Spinello and then a series of anonymous masters, as a new philological interest in "pre-Spinellesque" Aretine painting took shape. In the third, from the publication of the signature to the present, name and works have been joined, the catalogue extended, debated and refined, and Andrea recognised as the central figure of Aretine painting in the middle of the century. Recent years have increased his visibility through the showing of the Bern panels in the Kunstmuseum's 2026 exhibition *Das volle Leben. Von Duccio bis Liotard*, the showing of the Madonna Sarti in Arezzo in 2015–16, the acquisition of the three panels of the altar frontal in 2021, and the 2016 proceedings on Spinello and his time, whose substantial contribution on Andrea proposed new attributions and argued for a model of workshop collaboration and generational "communicating vessels" rather than an exclusive concern with individual authorship.

Several problems remain open: the documentary basis for the date 1369 and for death before 1387; the explicit documentation of Rebecca's marriage to Niccolò di Luca Spinelli; the original destination of the signed Annunciation; the original structures of the Baptist altarpiece and of the dispersed saints; the dating of the Madonna Sarti between about 1330 and the 1340s; the attribution of the disputed frescoes in San Francesco, Santi Michele e Adriano and the Santissima Annunziata; and the unprovable identity of the Maestro del Vescovado with Balduccio di Cecco. These uncertainties are not weaknesses of the reconstruction but the conditions of any honest account of a fourteenth-century painter known from a few documents and a single hidden signature.

What can be said with confidence is nonetheless substantial. A painter named Andrea, son of Nerio and connected by family with Donato di Rigo, held commissions of standing in Arezzo from 1331, worked in 1341 on the cathedral in the wake of Buffalmacco, and signed an Annunciation around mid-century. Around that panel a coherent body of work of high quality can be assembled, an original synthesis of Sienese, Florentine and local traditions whose forms and sentiments evidently shaped the young Spinello. Bartalini, in particular, showed that Arezzo between the Tarlati and Florentine rule was not a passive province receiving forms from Siena and Florence but the setting of an autonomous pictorial culture, of which Andrea di Nerio is the principal representative. His recovery has altered our picture of Tuscan painting in the middle decades of the fourteenth century, and it reminds us how much of that picture still depends on what chance, restoration and patient scholarship allow us to see.

## A Master in His Son's Shadow

Few personalities of Italian Gothic art are known with such precision in their legal and economic contours, and with such vagueness in their artistic substance, as *Camaino di Crescentino*. For almost four decades, between the last years of the thirteenth century and the middle of the 1330s, his name recurs in the archives of Siena: in council deliberations and commissions of experts, in pay lists of the cathedral workshop, in arbitrations over land, in notarial acts and in a tax declaration that tells us where he lived and what he owned. The city still preserves buildings with which his name is connected, from the brick loggia of the Fonte Nuova in the Pian d'Ovile to the upper façade of the cathedral and the vaulted baptistery of San Giovanni that lies beneath the eastern extension of the church. And yet no inscription, no contract and no contemporary chronicle names him as the author of a single statue, a single relief or a single architectural design in the sense in which we speak of authorship today. He is, in the most literal sense, a master known by his functions rather than by his works.

It is no paradox, therefore, that Camaino's posthumous renown should rest largely upon the career of another man. His son, Tino di Camaino, became the most internationally successful Sienese sculptor of the early fourteenth century, working in Pisa, Siena, Florence and finally at the Angevin court of Naples, where he shaped a whole tradition of royal funerary monuments. Tino twice carved his father's name into his own signed works, and on one of these occasions he went so far as to declare, in stone, that he would not wish to be called master while his father was alive. That gesture of deference, almost without parallel among the boastful signatures of Italian Gothic sculptors, has done more to keep Camaino's memory alive than anything his own hands can securely be shown to have produced. Between the eloquence of the documents and the silence of the works lies what recent scholarship has aptly called the enigma of Camaino di Crescentino.

The present essay attempts to reconstruct this figure as far as the primary sources and the critical literature allow, and deliberately no further. It begins with a consideration of the evidence itself, since the nature of the archival record determines what can and cannot be said about a master of this kind. It then follows Camaino through the documented stages of his life: his origins and family, his first appearance as an expert in the service of the Comune in 1298, his long years in the Opera di Santa Maria after the departure of Giovanni Pisano, his involvement in the completion of the cathedral façade and in the bold eastward extension of the church over the slope of Vallepiatta, and the civic commissions of his final decade. Only then does it turn to the questions that have generated the liveliest debate, namely whether Camaino was a sculptor at all, which works have been proposed as his, and in what sense he can be regarded as the teacher of his famous son.

Throughout, an effort is made to keep documented fact distinct from scholarly hypothesis, and both distinct from the confident simplifications that tend to accumulate around a figure of this sort in general literature. Several statements about Camaino that circulate widely, such as his supposed tenure of the formal office of capomaestro of the cathedral, his alleged activity as a mosaicist, or his unqualified authorship of the upper façade, turn out on inspection to rest on misreadings or on inferences that the sources do not support. Where the documents fall silent, this account falls silent with them. The resulting portrait is perhaps less heroic than some older reconstructions, but it is also, in its own way, more interesting: the portrait of a man who embodied, in a single career, the transformation of the Sienese cathedral workshop from a yard dominated by a charismatic genius into a collective enterprise governed by the commune and sustained by the competence and authority of its senior masters.

## The Nature of the Evidence

Any study of Camaino must begin with an admission: almost everything we know about him derives from documents produced for administrative and fiscal purposes, not for the commemoration of artists. The Opera di Santa Maria, the institution charged with building and maintaining the cathedral of Siena, generated over the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries a dense archive of account books, contracts, deliberations and inventories, which survives today in the Archivio dell'Opera della Metropolitana and, in part, in the Archivio di Stato di Siena. Beside it stand the records of the Comune: the deliberations of the Consiglio Generale and of the ruling magistracy of the Nine, the registers of the Biccherna<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-biccherna" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, which served as the city's treasury, the fiscal surveys of the Lira and the Estimo, and the notarial instruments preserved among the papers of the Gabella dei contratti. Camaino's documented life is assembled from these heterogeneous materials. They tell us what he was paid, what he owned, which tasks he was entrusted with and in whose company he worked; they almost never tell us what he made with his own hands.

The history of how these documents came to light has itself shaped the image of the man. Already in the early nineteenth century a Sienese antiquarian, compiling a manuscript dictionary of the city's artists, included Camaino among its entries, although later scholarship would show that some of his statements rested on misreadings of the sources. The decisive moment came in 1854, when the first volume of the Documenti per la storia dell'arte senese placed in print the key cathedral documents of 1310 and 1318. Among them were the payments that named Tino together with his father, documents which contributed substantially to the rediscovery of a sculptor whom Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> had transmitted to posterity only in a garbled form. In 1911 a canon of the cathedral published a documentary history of the building which added further notices, including Camaino's presence in the Opera's records of 1305 and the chronology of the eastward extension. In 1974 the entry devoted to Camaino in the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani condensed this material into a sober biographical sketch that remains the natural point of departure, notable for its scepticism towards any sculptural activity on his part.

A new phase opened in the first decade of the present century. In 2007 a systematic repertory of the documentary sources on Camaino di Crescentino and Tino di Camaino, covering the years from 1298 to 1348, was appended to a monograph on Tino published in Switzerland. Almost at the same time the long-running German research project on the churches of Siena produced both a monumental archival reconstruction of the Opera di Santa Maria from the twelfth to the fourteenth century, published in 2005, and a detailed chronology of the cathedral's construction based on the documents. For the first time it became possible to read Camaino's career not as a sequence of isolated notices but against the full institutional history of the workshop in which he spent most of his working life. The most focused modern discussion of his artistic identity, [Silvia Colucci](/scholars.html#colucci-silvia)'s essay on the sculptures of the upper part of the cathedral façade and the enigma of Camaino di Crescentino, appeared in 2011 within a collective volume on Sienese Gothic sculpture between 1260 and 1350; it is from that essay that the expression enigma, adopted in the introduction, derives.

The surviving evidence therefore has a characteristic profile. It is rich in dates, sums of money and functions, and poor in artistic attributions. The image built upon it varies according to how one reads the institutional vocabulary of the documents, and in particular such terms as magister lapidum, caputmagister and operarius, which carried precise meanings in the language of the Opera and the Comune. Much of the modern debate about Camaino, as will become clear, turns upon whether a given word in a given document implies a rank, an office or merely a profession. It should also be said at the outset that a few chronological details reported in the secondary literature disagree with one another, sometimes by several years, and that in such cases the present essay follows the scholarly reference works and signals the discrepancy rather than resolving it by fiat.

## Origins, Family and Property

Camaino was born in Siena, probably around 1260. This date is an inference rather than a documented fact: it is derived from the chronology of his career, which begins in the sources in 1298, and from the presumed birth of his son Tino around 1280. No baptismal record, no declaration of age and no reference to his youth has come down to us. His father was Crescentino di Dietisalvi, a name also spelled Diotisalvi, and the patronymic places the family among the many Sienese artisan households identified by Christian names rather than by stable surnames. The documents render Camaino's own patronymic in several forms, as di Crescentino, di Crescenzino or di Crescenzio, a variability entirely typical of the scribal habits of the period and of no particular significance. Of his mother, of his wife and of any children other than Tino nothing certain is known; the name of Tino's mother, too, remains unrecorded.

The family's later economic standing is far better attested than its beginnings. In 1318 Camaino and Tino submitted a joint property declaration, from which we learn that father and son held several plots of cultivated land together with a house in the contrada of Vallepiatta. The location is eloquent. Vallepiatta was the quarter clinging to the steep slope immediately below the apse of the cathedral, the very ground on which, from 1316 onwards, the eastward extension of the church and the new baptistery were to be built. The most recent biographical entry on Tino, published in 2019, reads the declaration as a sign that the family had reached a condition of comfortable prosperity, and the interpretation appears entirely reasonable. A master who owned land in the countryside and a house within sight of his principal workplace was no itinerant journeyman wandering from yard to yard, but a settled and propertied citizen, embedded in the parish and neighbourhood networks that structured Sienese urban life. One cannot help noticing, moreover, a certain poignancy in the fact that Camaino would spend his mature years presiding over works that transformed the very hillside on which he lived.

Where and how he received his training cannot be documented. The 1974 biography proposed that his formation as an architect must have taken place in the school, or at least in the orbit, of Giovanni Pisano, who between about 1284 and 1296 was raising the lower part of the cathedral façade. This is a plausible hypothesis, and it has the merit of explaining why the Opera should later have entrusted the continuation of Giovanni's work to Camaino; but it remains a hypothesis. Camaino's generation of Sienese stoneworkers came of age while two exceptional workshops were active on and around the cathedral: that of Nicola Pisano, whose great pulpit was completed in 1268, and that of his son Giovanni, whose statues of prophets, sibyls and philosophers began to populate the façade in the following decades. It would indeed be surprising if a master later regarded as the most trustworthy in the yard had never worked within it during Giovanni's tenure. Yet no surviving document places Camaino in the cathedral workshop before 1299, and the stylistic and technical culture of that site, which permeated the whole of Sienese stonework at the end of the thirteenth century, could well have been absorbed without any formal apprenticeship to its chief master.

The silence of the sources on his early years is not, in itself, unusual. Medieval building accounts recorded masters when they were paid or when they acted in a legal capacity; a young assistant working under another man's contract might leave no trace whatever. What is significant is that when Camaino does finally appear, he appears not as a beginner but as an established professional, already trusted by the commune with tasks that required experience, judgement and personal standing. Whatever his formation had been, by the late 1290s it was complete.

The earliest secure document of Camaino's activity is dated 10 August 1298. On that day a commission of five master stonemasons, appointed by the Comune of Siena for the purpose, determined the site, the dimensions and the architectural characteristics of a new fountain, the fonte nuova, to be built in the Pian d'Ovile, in the contrada of Vallerozzi. The members named in the published record are Camaino di Crescenzo, as the document calls him, Grazia di Martino, Vanni di Giovanni and Sozzo Rustichini. In the presence of witnesses they unanimously confirmed that the fountain should rise where the conduit discharged its water, and that the void of the building should measure seventeen braccia and that of the basin eight, so as to leave a covered passage around the water for the citizens. The commission is revealing in more than one respect. It shows that by 1298 Camaino enjoyed sufficient professional standing to be named to a municipal panel of experts; it presents him at work not on sculpture but on the planning of a building at once utilitarian and monumental; and it places him within one of the most characteristic fields of Sienese civic patronage, the city's supply of water.

Siena stands on a system of ridges and possesses no river. From the high Middle Ages onwards it depended on an elaborate network of underground conduits, the famous bottini, which gathered water from the surrounding hills and led it to a series of public fountains. Several of these fountains received architectural form as vaulted loggias protecting the basins from dirt and from the sun, and they became, in the course of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a source of considerable civic pride. The Fonte Nuova belongs to this family. The surviving structure is built entirely in brick, with sparing stone ornament; two pointed arches open on the principal front and one on the side, giving access to basins covered by cross vaults. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale singles it out for its great formal elegance and observes that its pointed arches recall Cistercian models, a lineage that is far from accidental, since Cistercian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-cistercian-order" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> building culture had entered the Sienese cathedral workshop through the lay brothers of the abbey of San Galgano, who administered the Opera for much of the thirteenth century.

The verdict of 1298 closed a long and uncertain prehistory. In March 1295 a first commission, composed of the masters Giovanni, Casolese di Martino, Sozzo Rustichini and Insegna, master of the Opera, and assisted by master Vanni di Camporegio and by the painter Duccio di Buoninsegna, had proposed to build the fountain in the square facing the outlet of the conduit. The proposal evidently did not satisfy the commune, which in the following year entrusted the same question to a second commission of citizens, before a third, made up entirely of stonemasons and including Camaino, settled the matter. Once it had done so the commune acted swiftly: on 31 December 1298 the Biccherna made a first payment of 1,622 lire<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lira-senese" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> and 6 soldi to Giacomo di Giovanni and Checcuccio Rustichelli, operai of the Comune elected to direct the masonry. The building itself dates the start of the works to August 1298, in the foundation inscription discussed below. By 1303 the fountain was certainly in service, for in that year the Biccherna paid for its cleaning under the new name of fonte di Borgofranco, taken from the suburb in which it stood.

Local tradition and a good deal of popular literature name Camaino and Sozzo di Rustichino as the designers of the fountain. The documents confirm that both sat on the commission of 1298, and Sozzo on the earlier one of 1295 as well, but they do not distribute responsibility for the executed building among its members, and it would be imprudent to call the Fonte Nuova simply Camaino's work. What can be said with confidence is that the fountain marks his debut in the sources as a planner and expert in municipal service, a role that would recur throughout the rest of his life.

Within a few years the commune called upon him again, and on two occasions in 1305 the nature of the tasks entrusted to him is particularly instructive. In the first he acted as arbiter in the allocation of lands at Macereto, Foiano and Tocchi, which were to be divided between the Opera del Duomo and the Cistercian monks of San Galgano, the very community whose lay brothers had long governed the cathedral workshop. In the second he was one of six experts charged with examining and valuing houses and open spaces lying between the church of the abbey of San Donato and the road to Camollia, which were to be demolished to make way for a new street, almost certainly the present Via della Stufa Secca. Neither task required the carving of stone. Both demanded instead the ability to measure land, to assess the value of property, to understand legal procedure and, not least, to command enough personal authority to be accepted by parties with conflicting interests.

Such commissions should not be regarded as marginal to Camaino's profession. In the Siena of the Nine the commune increasingly drew upon the masters of the Opera as a reservoir of technical competence for public works of every kind, and the boundaries between the cathedral yard and the civic administration were porous. A senior magister lapidum was expected to be a builder, a surveyor, an assessor and, when necessary, an arbitrator. Camaino's earliest documented activities thus already sketch the profile that the rest of his career would fill out: that of a master whose value to the city lay as much in his judgement as in his hands.

The Fonte Nuova carries its own birth certificate. It is a square slab of white Sienese marble set into the right-hand pier of the main front, as one faces the building. Already at the beginning of the twentieth century it was in very poor condition. [Bargagli Petrucci](/scholars.html#bargagli-petrucci-fabio) published its photograph in 1906, together with the transcription that is still the basis for any reading of the text. His plate shows a tablet framed by a raised moulding, and a deep crack crosses it diagonally from the upper left towards the lower centre. Erosion has worn away the left-hand portion of the lower lines, and the surviving letters stand in shallow relief against a pitted ground. The letters are in a Gothic majuscule with rounded forms. Lines that end short are completed by small incised horizontal strokes, which fill the remaining space. A cross at the start of the first line opens the text, in keeping with epigraphic custom.

The first six lines form three Leonine hexameters, each rhyming its middle with its end: *ducentenos* with *denos*, *Christe* with *iste*, *mense* with *Corrigiense*. They read:

> Mille ducentenos annos novies tibi denos / octo dedi, Christe, cum ceptus fons fuit iste, / Augusti mense, sub milite Corrigiense.

In English: "A thousand and two hundred years, nine times ten and eight, I have given to you, O Christ, when this fountain was begun, in the month of August, under the knight of Correggio." The year is given as an arithmetical periphrasis: one thousand, two hundred, nine tens and eight make 1298. The distributive numerals *ducentenos* and *denos* are chosen to fit the metre. The text dates the beginning of the works, not their completion. *Miles* was the usual title of the foreign knight who held the office of podestà, so the "knight of Correggio" is most probably the podestà of Siena in that year. This identification has not been established against the lists of Sienese podestà, and the name is left open here.

The dating agrees closely with the documents examined above. Bargagli Petrucci calls the inscription the "last and most solemn confirmation" that building began in the same month as the commission's verdict of 10 August, and the first payment for the masonry followed on 31 December. The inscription thus records the moment when Camaino's earliest documented commission turned into building work.

The second half of the text is far less legible, and every reading of it must stay provisional. Lines seven and eight seem to name further persons. One of them is a Theodelmus, followed by a place-name that Bargagli Petrucci prints as *Perganiensi*. The preceding *cive (?)*, which he marks with a question mark, may introduce a citizen. The closing lines turn to the fountain itself in the second person.

A plausible reading is:

> aque france daris / Francho burgo, qui locaris / in Camollia; quem servet / Virgo Maria,

that is, "you are given as free water to Borgo Franco, you who are placed in Camollia; may the Virgin Mary preserve it." If this reading is right, the verses play on the adjective *franca*, "free" or "exempt", and the name of the suburb, Borgo Franco. The rhymes *daris*/*locaris* continue the verse structure of the opening. The place-name is independently attested by the Biccherna payment of 1303 cited above, and could still be read on a nearby alley in 1906. The final invocation places the fountain under the protection of the Virgin, to whom Siena was dedicated.

An epigraphic puzzle remains. Bargagli Petrucci, again on the testimony of the eighteenth-century antiquarian Pecci, reports the same six opening lines word for word as the inscription of a lost fountain at Rosia in the Val di Merse. He does not comment on the coincidence with the text he had published thirty pages earlier. Pecci may have attached the Sienese inscription to the wrong site, or the formula may have been reused, but the surviving evidence does not allow a decision. Only the Fonte Nuova slab survives in situ, and only there does the full twelve-line text tie the verses to a specific suburb and a specific gate district of the city.

## The Cathedral Workshop After Giovanni Pisano

To understand the position Camaino came to occupy in the Opera di Santa Maria, one must recall the crisis through which the cathedral workshop passed at the end of the thirteenth century. From about 1284 the façade of the cathedral had been entrusted to Giovanni Pisano, who is recorded as capomaestro of the yard in a document of 3 April 1287 and who received Sienese citizenship and exemption from taxation as a reward for his labour. Under his direction the lower half of the façade rose with its three portals and an unprecedented cycle of monumental statues of prophets, sibyls and pagan philosophers, a programme which the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale describes as the first example in Italy of a statuary cycle conceived on a monumental scale. Giovanni, however, did not bring it to completion. He left Siena between 1296 and 1297, after disputes and an inquiry into delays and disorder on the site, at a moment when the structure had reached roughly the height of the portal gables; some of his statues had to wait until the masonry was finished before they could be set in place. The same encyclopaedia records that he nevertheless regarded the façade as his masterpiece, so much so that he wished to be buried at its foot.

The institutional situation of the Opera at this moment is no less important than the artistic one. Since the middle of the thirteenth century the commune had steadily extended its control over the cathedral workshop, which it largely financed. The constitution of 1262 committed the Comune to support ten masters who were to work throughout the year on the cathedral and could accept other employment only with the permission of the podestà; every January the podestà and the consuls were to decide how many masters should be employed and at what wages, while successive councils supervised the work through committees of citizens. The documents of the 1290s reveal a commune impatient with the administration of the workshop. In May 1296 a council demanded an *operaio*, that is, an administrator of the Opera, who could read and write, complaining that the incumbent could do neither; in 1299 the Consiglio Generale objected that an operaio burdened with wife and children was less suitable than one who lived all year round at the workshop. In April 1308 a reform confirmed by the Consiglio Generale forbade the operaio to undertake any new work without the approval of his counsellors, of his *capudmagistri* and of at least six of the stonemasons. The clause is of great interest, for it shows that by this date the masters of the yard were formally recognised as a body whose consent mattered in technical decisions.

This was the workshop Camaino entered: administratively reorganised under communal supervision and artistically orphaned of its great master. Elsewhere the departure of a Giovanni Pisano might have meant a prolonged interruption, and the history of medieval building sites offers many examples of projects that stalled for decades after the loss of their leading mind. In Siena, by contrast, the work continued. The façade was completed, the nave was raised, and within twenty years the Opera embarked on the most ambitious structural undertaking in the cathedral's history. Someone, or more precisely some group of people, held the yard together.

The reference literature has generally identified that someone with Camaino. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale states that after Giovanni's exit the sculptures of the cathedral were carried forward by the capomaestro Camaino di Crescentino, who directed the works for decades, until beyond the first third of the fourteenth century, together with a long series of sculptors, partly of Pisan training, whose individual contributions are extremely difficult to isolate. In its survey of Tuscan art the same work calls Camaino Giovanni's direct successor in the direction of the cathedral yard and credits him with having already tempered the violent pathos of his predecessor. These formulations capture something real about Camaino's prominence. Whether, however, capomaestro is the right word for his position, and whether his role can be described as direction in the sense in which that term applies to Giovanni Pisano, is precisely what more recent archival research has called into question, and it is to this problem that we must now turn.

## Rank and Title: The Problem of the Capomaestro

The documents allow Camaino's career within the Opera to be traced with some precision, even if they leave room for divergent readings of his rank. According to the summaries of Colucci's study, he first appears in connection with the Opera in 1299, as a witness to a financial transaction, and in 1302 he is repeatedly named in notarial acts as *sindaco e procuratore*, that is, as the legal representative of the institution. These two notices remain to be verified against the original documents, but they accord well with what was already known. The 1974 biography had observed that he was probably in the Opera's service by 1300 and certainly by 1305, when a cathedral document published in 1911 names him together with a master Ciolo and a master Orlando. The function of legal representative is worth underlining: it implies literacy, or at least familiarity with legal forms, and a degree of trust on the part of the administration that went well beyond what was required of an ordinary stonemason.

The best-known document of this phase bears the date of 28 November 1310. On that day it was decided that only ten masters should be retained in the cathedral workshop and that all the others should be dismissed, and in the list of those retained Camaino's name stands first. A recent French chronology of the cathedral, which draws on Colucci's essay, describes the same reduction to ten masters chosen from among the better and more useful, again with Camaino at their head, but places it in 1319. The 1974 biography gives 1310 and cites the corresponding page of the 1854 edition of the documents; the later date may well be an error of transmission, but the discrepancy cannot be settled without recourse to the originals, and it is signalled here rather than silently resolved. In either case the meaning of the document is clear enough. At a moment of retrenchment, when the commune reduced the workforce to the core of ten masters foreseen in its constitution, Camaino was the first master to be kept. The list does not say whether his position reflected seniority, salary, skill or merely the order in which a scribe happened to write the names; considered together with the rest of the evidence, however, it is difficult to regard it as a coincidence.

Here the central problem of Camaino's documented career comes into focus. Since the nineteenth century, and still in the 1974 biography, it has been repeated that he was almost certainly capomaestro of the cathedral from 1310 to 1318 and again from 1321 until his death, with an interval in 1319 and 1320 during which his son Tino held the office. That Tino bore the title is beyond doubt: a document of January 1320 calls him chapomastro. Whether the title was ever conferred upon Camaino himself is another matter. Recent research, founded on a complete re-examination of the sources, has observed that although Camaino heads the list of masters he is never described in the documents as master of the works. Colucci nonetheless judges that he probably played an important role and had largely won the confidence of the Opera's administration in the first decade of the century. A parallel reading in the recent literature, based on the archival chronology compiled within the German research project, describes him as consistently the best-paid master of the yard, documented from 1299 to 1335, and as a first among equals rather than a caputmagister in the formal sense; it states explicitly that, contrary to what is often repeated, he is never declared capomaestro of the cathedral.

The correction is not a pedantic matter of nomenclature. It alters the historical picture in a substantial way. If Camaino never formally held the office, then the image of a single directing intelligence succeeding Giovanni Pisano gives way to a more collegial model, in which the workshop after 1297 was conducted by a group of senior masters, among whom Camaino enjoyed the highest pay and the greatest trust, while the formal title of chief master remained vacant until it was briefly bestowed upon his son. Such a model accords well with the reform of 1308 described above. It accords equally well with the stylistic evidence of the façade sculpture, in which most specialists recognise several tendencies and several hands rather than the imprint of one dominant personality.

In what follows, therefore, Camaino's responsibility for the great works carried out in these decades should be understood as leadership within a collective, documented by his rank and his remuneration, and not as authorship in the sense in which one speaks of Giovanni Pisano's façade. This distinction may seem to diminish him. In reality it situates him more accurately within the institutional culture of the Sienese Opera, which had learned, through the troubled experience of the 1290s, to prefer reliability and continuity to genius.

The photograph isolates the zone of the façade that rises above Giovanni Pisano's portals: a screen of white marble veined with pink and darker stone, articulated by three gables and four tall pier-turrets bristling with crockets and pinnacles. At its centre a great circular oculus, set within a square frame, is surrounded by a dense lattice of small niches holding half-length figures. Seen from outside, it shows only its protective glazing, which mirrors the clouds; the stained-glass *Last Supper* by Pastorino de' Pastorini, of the mid-sixteenth century, is visible only from within. A frieze of small seated figures under trefoil arches runs above the square, while along the lower edge of the photograph a row of standing statues and the winged beasts of the Evangelists mark the cornice that separates the upper zone from the portals below. The tall central gable encloses, on a gold ground, a mosaic of the *Coronation of the Virgin* amid a choir of angels and is crowned by a winged figure brandishing a lance; the smaller side gables hold the *Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple*, on the left, and the *Nativity*, on the right. These mosaics are not medieval: they were executed in Venice in 1878 on cartoons by Sienese painters of the purist school, and the figurative programme they present belongs to the nineteenth-century restoration of the front. Behind the right-hand turrets rises the campanile with its alternating bands of white and dark marble, a reminder of the striped colouring that dominates the cathedral's flanks and interior.

The case of this upper façade offers a striking illustration of how a change in dating can transform a minor figure into a protagonist. For a long time the upper zone of the front just described was attributed to Giovanni di Cecco and assigned to the second half of the fourteenth century. The attribution rested on a document recording work on a *facciata* in the cathedral square, which was later recognised as referring not to the cathedral at all but to the loggia of the bishop's palace standing on the same piazza. As long as the old chronology prevailed, there was simply no place for Camaino di Crescentino in the history of the façade.

The decisive revision came from [Antje Middeldorf Kosegarten](/scholars.html#middeldorf-kosegarten-antje), whose study of the Sienese sculptors at work on the old cathedral was published in Munich in 1984 and in Italian translation in 1988. Drawing on a document of 1310 concerning the provision of a mosaic for the central gable, and on a careful rereading of three fourteenth-century Sienese chronicles that had previously been overlooked, she argued that the upper façade belonged to the same campaign as the lower and was essentially complete by 1317, together with the raising of the central nave. According to the introduction to the Italian edition, the chronicles show the façade approaching completion by May 1317, at least up to and including the level of the rose window. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* has accepted the conclusion in two of its entries. The one devoted to Giovanni Pisano states that the documents attest the execution of the façade in a single campaign from 1284 to 1317. The entry on Siena reports the proposal to date the upper part before 1317 and records, alongside it, a more cautious opinion placing the statues around the rose window in the two decades after 1300, when the lower sector was certainly finished and the nave was being heightened with new vaults and windows.

If the upper façade belongs to the years between about 1299 and 1317, it falls squarely within the period in which Camaino was the senior master of the Opera di Santa Maria. Hence the now common assertion in general literature that the upper façade is the work of Camaino di Crescentino, who laboured on it between about 1299 and 1317 and gave it its present three-gabled appearance. As a description of responsibility within the workshop the statement is defensible. As a claim of design authorship it goes beyond the documents, which nowhere name Camaino as the designer of the façade. On the evidence available it would be equally possible to imagine the upper zone as the product of collective decisions, taken within the framework established by the communal reform of 1308.

The irregularities of the façade bear directly on this question. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* notes that, having been built in two phases, the front displays a marked anomaly, with the sculptural decoration clearly prevailing over the architectural framework. Observers have long remarked that the upper zone does not align perfectly with the lower, and that the piers flanking the rose window fail to continue the axes established below. The sources do not permit us to decide whether such adjustments reflect the deliberate choices of a single designer, the compromises inherent in a collective workshop continuing someone else's project, or later interventions. What they do permit is a recognition that the upper façade, whoever conceived it, was conceived as a completion rather than as a fresh beginning, and that its builders were negotiating with a structure whose logic they had inherited.

The redating also has consequences beyond Siena. At Orvieto the Sienese Lorenzo Maitani directed, in the early fourteenth century, the construction of a cathedral front of comparable type, with three gables, abundant sculpture and mosaics. Under the old chronology the Sienese upper façade appeared to follow the Orvietan model. Under the new one the Sienese solution comes first, or at least develops in parallel with Maitani's project, and several authors have therefore suggested, with due caution, that Siena may have served as a model for Orvieto rather than the reverse. Middeldorf Kosegarten herself returned to the Orvietan façade in *Die Domfassade in Orvieto* (1996), a study that belongs to the same line of research. Whatever the precise direction of the exchange, the workshop in which Camaino held the first place has moved from the margins to the centre of a key chapter in the history of Italian Gothic façade design. Nothing in the evidence, however, authorises us to credit him personally with its formal invention.

## The Eastern Extension and the Baptistery of San Giovanni

No undertaking of Camaino's years in the Opera was technically more ambitious than the eastward extension of the cathedral over the slope of Vallepiatta. The 1974 biography describes it as an enlargement, or more precisely a lengthening, of the church towards the apse, and stresses that the work was of no small difficulty in two respects: structurally, because the great mass of the new east end had to rise on the steep flank of the hill; and aesthetically, because the architectural balance of the interior, and above all its relation to the dome, was not to be disturbed. According to the documentary history of the cathedral published in 1911, the works began on 21 April 1316. A recent popular chronology gives 21 April 1313 while citing the same page of that history, which suggests a slip; the scholarly biography's date of 1316 is followed here.

The event did not pass unnoticed by contemporaries. The Sienese chronicler Agnolo di Tura del Grasso, writing of the year 1317, describes a city living in great peace and tranquillity, each man attending to his own profit and all bound together in fraternal concord, and records that in that year the Sienese enlarged the cathedral towards Vallepiatta and began the façade of San Giovanni, which he praises as a work both beautiful and great. The chronicler's tone, which links the enlargement of the cathedral to the prosperity and harmony of the commune, reminds us that the building was understood as a civic as much as an ecclesiastical enterprise.

The architectural logic of the project is well understood. The intention was to add two bays to the choir and to enlarge the transepts on ground that fell away sharply towards the valley. To achieve this the builders created a substructure beneath the new east end, whose vaults would carry the floor of the extended choir above, and the lower space thus obtained was used to house a new baptistery dedicated to Saint John. The decision tied the extension to a long-standing civic aspiration: as early as 1296 the government of the Nine had resolved to build a new baptistery near the old one and had entrusted the task to Giovanni Pisano, but nothing had come of it before his departure. The baptistery as built is a broad rectangular hall covered by pointed cross vaults and divided by two massive piers into three aisles of two bays each, with a large semi-hexagonal niche opening in the end wall to contain the altar. According to the documentary history its vaults were closed in 1326. Its façade, which the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale counts among the formally most accomplished examples of Sienese Gothic architecture, belongs to a later phase generally associated with Giovanni d'Agostino and was left unfinished; a parchment drawing in the Museo dell'Opera records its intended appearance.

The 1974 biography credits Camaino with superintending the extension, while the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale more prudently describes the works as traditionally attributed to him, which is the appropriate degree of confidence. What the documents securely establish is that Camaino and Tino were both in the service of the Opera during the decisive phase of the project. On 8 June 1318 the Opera acknowledged a debt of three hundred lire for that year's work to Camaino, to his son Tino and to twenty-three other masters; on the same day Camaino, Tino and a certain Giovanni di Nerio were credited with twenty-five gold florins<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gold-florin" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> which they had advanced to purchase red marble from Gerfalco, in the Sienese Maremma, where the Opera had rented a quarry since 1293. Other documents of the first half of 1318 and of early 1320 attest the work of both father and son. As the 2019 biography of Tino observes, it is impossible to distinguish the respective shares of the two masters within so vast a programme of construction and decoration.

The two documents of June 1318 are informative in themselves. The first shows Camaino working alongside his son and at least twenty-three other masters, a workforce far exceeding the statutory ten and an eloquent measure of the scale of the eastern project. The second shows him, with Tino and a third master, advancing personal funds for the purchase of materials. Such advances were common in medieval building finance, where institutions with irregular revenues relied on the liquidity of their senior craftsmen and suppliers; they indicate that Camaino possessed capital and that the Opera trusted him with it, which accords perfectly with the property declaration of the same year.

The extension soon ran into difficulty. On 17 February 1322 the operaio and the government of the Nine asked a commission of experts to judge the recently begun works. It comprised Lorenzo Maitani, then capomaestro of the cathedral of Orvieto, the Sienese Niccolò di Nuto, and the Florentines Cino di Francesco, Tone di Giovanni and Vanni di Cione. Their verdict was severe: they raised serious structural concerns and concluded that it would be better to build a new church, beautiful, great and magnificent. That judgement prepared the ground conceptually for the grandiose project of the Duomo Nuovo launched in 1339. Camaino is not named in the commission's report, and it would be speculative to read its criticism as a personal judgement on his competence. Yet the works examined had been carried out while he was the senior master of the Opera, and it is fair to note that outside experts found the most ambitious engineering project of his career structurally problematic. The extension was completed only in the second half of the century, after the collapse of the Duomo Nuovo; as the 1974 biography remarks, Camaino did not live to see the work finished.

## Civic Expertise, Last Years and Death

Alongside his work for the Opera, Camaino continued to serve the commune as an expert in urban questions, and in his last documented decade he was involved in two of the defining enterprises of Siena under the Nine. In 1329 he was one of the masters appointed to establish the boundary of the Piazza del Campo on the side towards the Porta Salaria. The Campo had been created in the second half of the thirteenth century through a long process of expropriation and demolition, and by 1297 a statute already prescribed uniform window types for the houses that lined it, so that its fan-shaped form was by then largely fixed. The precise demarcation of its edges nevertheless retained both legal and aesthetic importance, since the curving perimeter and the regulated façades were central to the image of civic order that the square was meant to express.

At about the same time Camaino was asked, together with others, to indicate the course of the new city walls. The 1974 biography dates the commission to 1330, while a chronology based on the archival research of the German project on the churches of Siena records that on 3 April 1331 master Camaino and five other stonemasons were paid for planning a new town wall. The two dates may well refer to the commission and to the subsequent payment, or to different stages of the same task, and need not be regarded as contradictory. The final circuit of Siena's walls, planned from the middle of the 1320s, was geometrically ambitious and included new gates such as the Porta Romana, which Agnolo di Tura praised as greater and more beautiful than any other gate in Italy. Camaino's part in its planning places him among the technicians who gave concrete form to the urban programme of the Nine.

These commissions illuminate the social profile of a senior master in early fourteenth-century Siena. Such a man was not merely a craftsman paid by the day, but an expert whose judgement the commune could summon for the valuation of property, the division of land, the alignment of streets, the supply of water and the design of fortifications. By this period the Opera di Santa Maria functioned as something close to the city's own building agency, controlled and in large part financed by the commune, and it provided the institutional setting in which such expertise was cultivated and deployed. The Opera was regularly called upon to act on the commune's behalf, from the inspection of wells in the 1290s to the construction of the churches of San Basilio and San Luca, which the commune imposed upon it in 1328. Camaino's career reveals on an individual scale the same interpenetration of cathedral workshop and civic administration that the institutional history reveals collectively.

His material standing completes the picture. He owned a house in Vallepiatta and several plots of farmland, he could advance gold florins to the Opera, he was consistently among the best-paid masters of the yard, and he was repeatedly chosen for public commissions. All this places him in the upper ranks of the Sienese artisan class, which under the regime of the Nine enjoyed a measure of civic recognition while remaining distinct from the merchant and banking families who governed the city. There is no evidence that Camaino ever held political office; the designations he receives in the documents are professional rather than honorific, those of magister, magister lapidum and legal representative of the Opera.

The last phase of his life is documented only sporadically. According to the repertory of 2007, as reported in the 2019 biography of Tino, Camaino was still alive in January 1336, and the first notice of his death dates from 1338; older literature hesitated between 1337 and 1338. The safest formulation, in the light of the documents, is that he died between 1336 and 1338. He presumably died in Siena, though the place of his burial is unknown. One modern reconstruction of the record extends his presence among the cathedral masters to 1335, which, if the conventional birth date of about 1260 is correct, means that he remained active into his middle seventies.

The chronology of his death intersects with that of his son in a manner that has not escaped the notice of scholars. Tino died in Naples, where he had worked for the Angevin court since about 1323. Documents of 8 and 11 July 1336 record his replacement in the works at the Certosa di San Martino and at Castel Sant'Elmo, and in the second his name is accompanied by the word quondam, implying that he had recently died; another document, however, would record him alive on 2 June 1337, so that some scholars place his death in that year, with the first certain notice dating from January 1338. Whichever date is correct, father and son died within about two years of one another: the father in Siena at an advanced age, the son in Naples at the summit of a brilliant career. The man who had refused the title of master while his father lived did not, in the event, long survive him, if he survived him at all.

## Camaino as Sculptor? The Problem of Attributions

From the documented career we come to the most contested question of all: was Camaino also a sculptor, and if so, what did he carve? The 1974 biography answered with exemplary bluntness that there are no traces or reliable testimonies of any sculptural activity on his part. It added that the designation magister lapidum applied to him in the documents of 1298 and 1318 can denote an architect as readily as a sculptor; that the habit of some earlier writers, including the nineteenth-century Sienese antiquarian and the compilers of the great German lexicon of artists, of calling him a sculptor lacked any foundation; and that the notion of his having worked as a mosaicist should be rejected outright, since it arose from a misreading of the 1310 document concerning the mosaic of the central gable, the very document that would later contribute to the redating of the upper façade.

Against this scepticism stands a series of hypotheses advanced since the 1940s. The first came from [Enzo Carli](/scholars.html#carli-enzo), director of the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Siena and author of the first modern monograph on Tino di Camaino, published in 1934. In his guide to the museum of 1946 Carli suggested, with great caution as the 1974 biography emphasises, that some of the statues carved for the exterior of the cathedral might be Camaino's. The proposal acquired new relevance once the upper façade had been redated into Camaino's working life. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale distinguishes two stylistic tendencies among the sculptors who continued Giovanni's work.

The first, more faithful to Giovanni, is dynamically expressive but lacks the master's grandiose drama, and it is seen above all in statues of apostles and saints, now in the Museo dell'Opera, probably intended for the upper levels of the façade; with this group, the encyclopaedia suggests, Camaino's name may perhaps be associated. The second tendency is increasingly pictorial, with smooth surfaces and very soft features, and the encyclopaedia links it with the lintel of the central portal, which it regards as probably an early work of Tino. This remains, in essence, the state of the question. Colucci's essay of 2011 re-examined the sculptures of the upper façade in the light of the documentary repertory; as far as its published summaries allow one to judge, it keeps the problem of Camaino's sculptural identity open while acknowledging the gap between his documented eminence and the absence of any securely attributable work.

It may be added that the documents consistently call him magister lapidum rather than something more specific, and assign him legal, surveying and planning duties. This profile is compatible with a predominantly architectural and organisational role. It is equally compatible, however, with a master who also carved, for in the Tuscan yards of the period the distinction between the stonemason who squared blocks, the carver of ornament and the figure sculptor was a matter of degree and of individual talent rather than of separate professions. The documents simply do not decide the matter. Scholarship has proposed several plausible stylistic groupings within the façade sculpture, but none has been anchored to Camaino by external evidence, and attributions in this field must therefore be treated as hypotheses of varying probability rather than as facts.

Two further groups of works outside Siena have been drawn into the discussion. The first consists of eleven marble statuettes of saints, prophets and apostles from the cathedral of San Cerbone at Massa Marittima, three of which the local museum now assigns to Camaino; the group, its scholarly history and the three figures in question are examined in the pages that follow.

Six marble statuettes stand in a row on a low steel plinth, one beside the other, as they might once have stood along the top of a liturgical screen. They belong to a group of eleven figures of prophets, apostles and saints, now in the Museo di Arte Sacra of Massa Marittima, the former Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> convent of San Pietro all'Orto. They are generally believed to come from the iconostasis, or choir screen, of the cathedral of San Cerbone, though no document records their original setting. Their modest size, their frontal emphasis and their measured spacing all suit a row of figures set above eye level on a partition between nave and choir. There they would have been read as a single company of witnesses rather than as isolated images.

The scholarly history of the group is short but significant. In 1984 an article in *Prospettiva* assigned all eleven statuettes to Gano di Fazio, the Sienese sculptor documented from 1302 until his death in 1316 or 1317. Gano is the author of the signed tomb of Bishop Tommaso d'Andrea<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-tommaso-d-andrea" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> at Casole d'Elsa. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* accepted this view and placed the statuettes in Gano's late, intensely Gothic phase. The present display divides the group. Eight figures in white marble remain with Gano. Three figures in a grey-white marble with painted details are assigned to Camaino di Crescentino and dated about 1300 to 1320. The published literature consulted does not establish who first proposed this separation, or on what grounds beyond the difference of material and finish. The attribution to Camaino should therefore be treated as a working hypothesis of the museum, not as an established fact. Its interest lies in the fact that no sculpture can be securely assigned to Camaino on documentary grounds.

The three figures on the left of the photograph are mature, bearded men, and they display the most vigorous carving of the series. Their heads are large in relation to their bodies, and the hair and beards are cut into deep, separate locks that catch the light. This treatment recalls the expressive heads of Giovanni Pisano's façade statues at Siena, which every Sienese stonemason of the generation would have known. The first figure gathers his mantle in a great sling of folds across the body and holds a rolled scroll. The second lets a long, blank unrolled scroll fall straight down from his right hand, and his head turns slightly to one side with an air of grave attention. The third, in the centre of the row, is the most eloquent of the group. He holds a long unrolled scroll diagonally across his body, while the raised fingers of his right hand make the gesture of speech. Traces of blue survive in the hollows of his drapery, a reminder that these figures were originally coloured and that their present whiteness is largely the effect of loss.

The three figures on the right are beardless and youthful, and the carving changes with the type. Faces become smooth and rounded, hair falls in soft curls around the cheeks, and draperies descend in longer, quieter folds. The fourth figure displays an unrolled scroll that falls vertically in front of his body; incised lines on it imitate writing. The fifth lays his left hand on his breast in a gesture of devotion or witness, and a broad ornamented hem runs diagonally across his mantle. The sixth, wrapped in a cloak fastened at the neck, lowers his eyes towards a book that he holds open in both hands. In several figures the mantle borders are worked with incised ornament imitating embroidered textile hems. Their darker tone suggests that remains of colour or gilding may survive there, though only technical examination could confirm it.

Of the six figures shown here, the museum's attribution to Camaino concerns the second and the fifth from the left, the bearded prophet with the blank scroll and the young apostle in grey-veined marble; the third figure assigned to him, a polychrome deacon saint, is displayed separately. All three are examined individually below. The contrast between deeply drilled bearded heads and smoother young faces runs through the whole group and may reflect the conventions that distinguish old prophets from young apostles rather than two hands. A judgement on the attribution would require an examination of the figures from all sides, of their tool marks and of their surviving polychromy, none of which is yet available in print.

Whatever their distribution between Gano and Camaino, the statuettes are precious evidence for the diffusion of Sienese sculpture into the territory of the commune in the first two decades of the fourteenth century. Massa Marittima was then one of the richest centres of the Sienese Maremma. Its cathedral was enlarged and furnished in the same years, and Goro di Gregorio's Arca di San Cerbone was completed for it in 1324. The screen figures show how the language formed on the Sienese cathedral yard, Giovannesque in its heads and increasingly calm and decorative in its draperies, reached a provincial centre through masters trained in that yard. If the museum's hypothesis is correct, they would also give a face to Camaino di Crescentino, otherwise known only from documents, as a carver of figures as well as a builder, surveyor and arbiter.

The prophet stands frontally, his weight settled evenly on both feet, wrapped in a heavy mantle that falls from his shoulders almost to the ground. His head is the dominant feature of the figure: large in relation to the body, with a deeply furrowed brow, heavy lids and a long beard. The beard is carved in separate, twisting locks, and the drill has opened small round cavities between them. That technique, which breaks the surface into patches of light and shadow, belongs to the tradition of Giovanni Pisano’s prophets on the Siena façade. It fits the view, put forward in the museum’s attribution, of a sculptor trained in the post-Giovanni cathedral yard. The expression is grave and inward rather than dramatic: the violent pathos of Giovanni’s heads has been tempered into an attitude of concentrated attention.

The prophet’s attribute is the long scroll that falls vertically from his right hand, unrolled down the front of his body. Its smooth, unworked surface was almost certainly meant to carry a painted text, a verse of prophecy now entirely lost. Without it the figure cannot be identified, and the scroll itself, flat and rigid, reads at first glance as a fold of the mantle. The left hand rests lightly on the falling drapery at the hip, completing a quiet, balanced pose.

The carving of the garments is careful and decorative. A rosette-shaped brooch fastens the mantle on the left shoulder. The neckline of the tunic, the cuffs and the borders of the mantle are worked with incised bands of chevron ornament, imitating the embroidered hems of luxury textiles. Faint warm tones in these borders may be remains of colour or gilding. The heavy, angular fold that cuts diagonally across the chest, and the long vertical channels of the lower drapery, give the figure a columnar solidity suited to a statue seen from below and from in front.

The figure’s surface is worn, particularly at the right hand and around the feet, and some of the finer detail of the beard has been lost. The statuette’s flat back and frontal design are consistent with its proposed origin on a choir screen, where it would have stood in a row with the other prophets and apostles.

The young apostle is the most elegant figure of the group. He stands with his weight on one leg, and his head turns sharply towards his right shoulder, giving the slender body a gentle twist. His face is a smooth oval, with a straight nose, a small closed mouth and wide almond-shaped eyes under clearly drawn brows. His hair is gathered in soft rolls around the forehead and ears. Nothing in the face recalls the furrowed intensity of the bearded prophet: the sculptor, or the convention, has shifted to an ideal of calm youthful beauty.

The drapery carries the composition. A long mantle passes over the right shoulder, falls in a broad curtain along the right side and is pulled in a great diagonal sweep across the body to the left hip. The folds are deep and regularly spaced, and the curving hem, edged with a carved fringe, describes a long rhythmic line across the lower half of the figure. The decoration is finely worked: a band of punched dots outlines the neckline of the tunic, and the mantle border on the right shoulder carries an incised pattern of interlocking circles and quatrefoils. The left hand is laid flat on the breast in a gesture of devotion or of personal witness, a familiar gesture for apostles in the sculpture and painting of the period.

The bare feet that emerge from beneath the robe identify him as an apostle rather than a prophet or a saint, since in medieval convention bare feet are reserved for Christ, the angels and the apostles. No attribute survives that would identify him more closely. The distinctive grey veining of the marble runs across the whole figure and clearly distinguishes this statuette from Gano’s figures in purer white stone. That difference of material is the most visible basis for the museum’s division of the group.

Stylistically the figure stands at some distance from Giovanni Pisano. The long, flowing mantle, the attention to ornamental detail and the softness of the face belong rather to the increasingly pictorial direction that Sienese sculpture took in the first decades of the fourteenth century. Whether that softness is the hand of Camaino, of another master of the same workshop, or simply the treatment the period reserved for young saints, cannot be decided from the statuette alone.

The cleric is the only figure of the group that still conveys how these statuettes were meant to be seen. Where his companions now appear almost white, he wears a full coat of colour. His face is pale, with rosy cheeks and dark painted eyes. His tightly curled hair, his shoes and the borders of his vestments are gilded, and a flat gilded disc set horizontally above the head serves as a halo. The polychromy is so extensive and so well preserved that its relation to the original surface needs technical examination. Parts of it may have been renewed, and the halo in particular looks like a later addition.

He wears the dalmatic, the vestment of a deacon, and this places him among the deacon saints, such as Stephen or Lawrence. Without his attribute he cannot be named, and the museum’s title calls him simply a “beardless cleric with an open book”. The garment is painted to imitate a precious silk. A diaper of red lozenges with gilded centres and branching sprigs covers the white ground, scattered with gilded rosettes. Broad gilded apparels on the chest and above the hem, the square ornamental panels of the dalmatic, are decorated with painted motifs. Narrow gilded bands outline the opening of the sleeves and the sides of the vestment.

The pose is frontal and stiff, with the arms held away from the body. The right hand, raised in a loose fist, is pierced to hold an attribute, perhaps a palm or a book, now lost. The left hand is extended with the palm turned down and the fingers bent as if it once held something. The open book mentioned in the museum’s title is therefore no longer visible. The hands are large in proportion and slightly different in finish from the rest of the figure, and their originality has not been established, which limits any stylistic conclusion drawn from them.

The value of the figure lies above all in its colour. It shows that the sculpture of the choir screen was conceived as a coloured ensemble, whose effect in the dim interior of the cathedral depended as much on gold and pigment as on carving. It also helps to explain why the museum describes Camaino’s three figures as “painted”: the traces of colour on the prophet and the apostle are the remains of a surface like this one.

The second case concerns the tomb of Pope Gregory X<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gregory-x" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a> in the cathedral of Arezzo. Gregory died at Arezzo on 10 January 1276, leaving funds for the construction of a new cathedral, and his monument, with a sarcophagus bearing roundels of the Lamb of God and the four Evangelists, a recumbent effigy and a canopy enriched with crockets, is among the earliest Gothic figurative tombs of central Italy. Its authorship and date have been much debated. A conference paper of 1985, published in 1990, assigned it to a Sienese workshop of the later thirteenth century and connected it with the early career of Gano di Fazio; a study of 2003 accepted the attribution to Gano but dated the tomb to 1303 and 1304, while another reading places it in the 1280s. In 2015 the author of the 2003 study published an article whose very title announces a passage from Gano di Fazio to Camaino di Crescentino, and whose declared subject is the intervention of both sculptors in the monument. The contents of that article are not documented here, so which parts of the tomb it assigns to Camaino, and on what arguments, remains to be verified. Reference works now describe the attribution of the monument as oscillating between Gano or his workshop and Camaino. This is the only proposal known to the present writer that gives Camaino a role in a work outside the Sienese cathedral yard and its immediate dependencies, and it deserves careful assessment once its arguments can be weighed.

Taken together, these proposals sketch the figure of a sculptor working in a Giovannesque idiom tempered by Sienese restraint, who would have produced statues for the upper façade of the cathedral, contributed to the liturgical furnishings of Massa Marittima and perhaps intervened in a papal tomb at Arezzo. The picture is coherent, but its coherence is partly a product of the desire to give substance to a documented name, and each of its components rests on stylistic judgement without documentary anchorage. A prudent historian must keep two propositions apart. That Camaino was a highly esteemed and well-paid master of the Sienese cathedral workshop during decades in which much sculpture was produced there is documented and certain. That specific surviving sculptures are by his hand remains possible, in some cases even probable, but it is not proven.

## Father and Son: Camaino and Tino di Camaino

If Camaino's own works elude us, his relationship with his son is unusually well attested, and from both sides. The payments of 1318 and 1320 and the joint tax declaration of 1318, discussed above, show father and son working, investing and owning property together. Tino's own inscriptions supply the rest. On a Madonna and Child now in the Museo Civico of Turin, probably carved during his Pisan years, he signs himself as the son of Camaino, to whom he gives the title of magister. On the tomb of Bishop Antonio d'Orso in Florence Cathedral, completed before the solemn translation of the bishop's body on 18 July 1321, he carved into the masonry beside the monument an inscription declaring that he, born in Siena of master Camaino, had sculpted the whole work in this Florentine setting, and adding that it befitted him to bow before his own father, so that he would not wish to be called master while his father lived.

This declaration of filial deference is highly unusual among the inscriptions of Italian Gothic sculpture, and its meaning has been much discussed. The 1974 biography read it simply as an expression of filial reverence. The 2019 entry on Tino in the Dizionario Biografico offers a sharper interpretation. Giovanni Pisano's inscriptions had famously proclaimed his superiority over all earlier sculptors, implicitly including his own father Nicola; Tino's twofold praise of his father, the entry suggests, may be read as a deliberate contrast with that attitude, and points rather to an artistic education received alongside Camaino in the cathedral yard at Siena.

The reading carries weight in a long-standing debate on Tino's formation. For much of the twentieth century it was taken for granted that Tino had been a pupil of Giovanni Pisano, perhaps collaborating on the pulpits of Pistoia and Pisa. The 2019 entry raises two objections to this view: Tino's name is absent from the abundant documentation concerning Giovanni's works, and in the early years of the century he appears as an alternative to Giovanni in the most important Pisan commissions rather than as his assistant. Both considerations argue against a long apprenticeship under the older master. On this reading, the Giovannesque features undeniably present in Tino's early works derive from the culture shaped by Giovanni that pervaded Siena, where both Nicola and Giovanni had worked for decades, and they were transmitted to the young sculptor within the post-Giovanni workshop that his father helped to lead.

If this interpretation is accepted, Camaino becomes the decisive figure in Tino's training. Two cautions are nonetheless required. Since no sculpture can securely be assigned to Camaino, it is impossible to say what Tino learned from him in specifically stylistic terms; one can speak only of a shared workshop culture. And the presumed early fruit of Tino's Sienese apprenticeship is itself uncertain: Carli in 1941 attributed the damaged lintel of the central portal of the cathedral to the young Tino, but the 2019 entry finds the attribution not entirely convincing.

Tino's career then carried him away from his father for long periods. His first securely accepted work is the arca-altar of Saint Rainerius in Pisa Cathedral, from the first decade of the century. He is documented in Pisa in 1312, when he signed the cathedral's baptismal font, and as capomaestro of the Pisan Opera he executed the tomb of the Emperor Henry VII, contracted on 12 February 1315 and installed by the July of that year. After fighting with the Guelf<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guelphs" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a> league against Pisa at the battle of Montecatini on 29 August 1315 he returned to Siena, where he carved the tomb of Cardinal Riccardo Petroni in the cathedral, now dated by many to about 1315 or 1316 rather than 1317, and worked beside his father in the Opera until 1320. In July 1322 the Pisan government formally declared him removed from office because, in the words of the decree, he was a Guelf and had fought against the Pisans at Montecatini; by then he had moved to Florence, and from late 1323 or early 1324 he worked in Naples for Robert of Anjou<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-robert-anjou" class="footnote"><sup>10</sup></a>.

Two features of this trajectory bear upon Camaino. Nothing in the documents suggests that Camaino himself ever worked in Pisa; the family's connection with Pisan building sites runs entirely through Tino, and apart from the hypothetical attributions at Arezzo and Massa Marittima the sources offer no support for activity on Camaino's part outside Sienese territory. Moreover, the years 1318 to 1320, in which father and son are documented together in the cathedral yard, coincide with the most demanding phase of the eastern extension. The temporary conferral of the title of capomaestro upon Tino in those years shows how highly the Opera valued him, and it may also suggest that the Opera, or Camaino himself, regarded the son as the natural heir to the family's leading position in the workshop. Tino's departure for Florence and Naples left that expectation unfulfilled.

In Naples Tino worked as an architect as well as a sculptor, directing works at the Certosa di San Martino and at Castel Sant'Elmo and taking part in operations at Castel Nuovo and in the port. This double competence corresponds closely to the profile of his father's career in Siena. Whether it reflects specific instruction received from Camaino or simply the normal range of a Sienese magister lapidum of the time cannot be decided, but the parallel is striking, and it lends support to the view that Camaino's most enduring artistic legacy was a son who united sculptural and architectural mastery in the manner that the father's documented career had anticipated.

## Historiography and Conclusion: The Significance of Camaino di Crescentino

The critical fortune of Camaino can be followed through four broad phases, each shaped by the documents available and by the questions asked of them. In the antiquarian culture of the early nineteenth century he appeared as one name among many recovered from the archives, and was assigned, sometimes fancifully, the roles of sculptor and mosaicist. Vasari had not mentioned him at all, and even Tino survived in the Lives only in distorted form, as a Sienese sculptor and architect called Lino, to whom the chapel of Saint Rainerius and the baptismal font of Pisa Cathedral were credited. Tino's rediscovery in the nineteenth century, assisted by the publication of the documents of 1318 in 1854, inevitably drew attention to his father, but it also fixed Camaino in the role of a father rather than of an artist in his own right. In the second phase, extending from the early twentieth century to the 1970s, Camaino was treated essentially as an architect and administrator. The documentary history of the cathedral of 1911 inserted him into the chronology of the building, the monographs on Tino, beginning with Carli's of 1934, discussed him as the father and presumed teacher of their subject, and Carli's cautious suggestion of 1946 concerning the exterior statues remained isolated. The 1974 biography represents the culmination of this phase: a careful synthesis of the documents, sceptical of any sculptural attribution, yet still repeating the belief that Camaino formally held the office of capomaestro for most of his career.

The third phase opened in 1984 with Middeldorf Kosegarten's redating of the upper façade to the years before 1317. At a stroke Camaino was transformed from a marginal figure into the senior master of the workshop during the construction of one of the most important Gothic façades in Italy, and the question of who had carved the sculptures of that façade was reopened. Reference works began cautiously to associate him with the Giovannesque statues of the upper zone, while popular literature began, less cautiously, to speak of the upper façade simply as his. The fourth phase, inaugurated by the publication of the complete documentary repertory in 2007 and by the archival reconstruction of the Opera, has produced a paradoxical result. It has removed from Camaino the title of capomaestro that earlier literature took for granted, while confirming his pre-eminence among the masters of the Opera. Colucci's essay belongs to this phase, as does the line of research that seeks Camaino's hand outside Siena, at Massa Marittima and at Arezzo. The figure that emerges is in one respect more modest than the older literature supposed, since he was not a formally appointed chief master on the model of Giovanni Pisano; in another respect he is more substantial, since he appears as the leading master of a collective workshop throughout its most productive and technically daring decades.

What, then, was the significance of Camaino di Crescentino? The answer depends on the measure one adopts. Measured by securely attributed works, he barely registers, for no statue, relief or design can be assigned to him on documentary grounds and the attributions proposed so far remain hypotheses. Measured by fame, his name would scarcely be remembered outside specialist circles were it not for his son's career and inscriptions. Measured by his contribution to the collective enterprises that shaped Siena in the decades of its greatest prosperity, however, his importance is considerable and well documented. For almost forty years Camaino was one of the masters upon whom the commune and the Opera relied. He helped to define the Fonte Nuova, valued property for new streets, divided lands between the Opera and San Galgano, fixed the edges of the Campo and planned the course of the new walls. Above all, he was among the senior masters of the cathedral yard, and apparently the best paid of them, in the years in which the façade begun by Giovanni Pisano was completed, the nave was raised, the choir was extended over the slope of Vallepiatta and the baptistery of San Giovanni was vaulted beneath it. That outside experts criticised some of these works on structural grounds in 1322, and that the extension was finished only decades after his death, diminishes neither their ambition nor his share in them; it reminds us rather that medieval building was a collective, cumulative and frequently contested process, in which the role of the individual master is better described as responsibility than as authorship.

Camaino's career also illustrates a change in the organisation of Italian building sites. Giovanni Pisano had embodied the model of the charismatic capomaestro, a master of exceptional genius who defined the artistic vision of a project and whose departure could bring it to a halt. The Siena that came after him appears to have preferred a different model, entrusting its cathedral to a body of senior masters bound by communal regulation and by the consent of their peers, among whom one man held a recognised but untitled primacy. Whether this model tempered Giovanni's pathos, as one reference work suggests, is a stylistic judgement that depends on attributions still in question. That it allowed the work to proceed without interruption for two decades is a historical fact.

Finally, Camaino belongs to the history of art through the formation of Tino di Camaino. The most recent scholarship reads Tino's inscriptions as evidence that he learned his craft not principally in Giovanni Pisano's workshop but at his father's side in the cathedral yard of Siena. If that reading is correct, the line of Sienese Gothic sculpture that runs through Tino to Florence and Naples, and from Naples into much of the funerary art of fourteenth-century southern Italy, passes through Camaino's workshop. This remains a hypothesis grounded in Tino's own words rather than in any document of apprenticeship, but it is the most persuasive account currently available, and it gives Camaino an indirect place in the history of Italian sculpture that his own uncertain oeuvre cannot secure. The inscription in Florence Cathedral, in which a celebrated sculptor declines the title of master out of respect for his living father, remains the most eloquent testimony to that place, and the only contemporary text that tells us what Camaino di Crescentino was worth to someone who knew both him and his work.

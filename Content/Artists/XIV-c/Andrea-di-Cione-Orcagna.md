## Introduction: The Problem of Orcagna

Few artists of the Italian fourteenth century have been asked to carry as much interpretative weight as Andrea di Cione, called Orcagna. He is at once one of the best-documented Florentine masters of his generation and one of the most persistently misunderstood. The documents place him at the centre of nearly every major artistic enterprise undertaken in Florence between the fall of the Duke of Athens in 1343 and the outbreak of plague and political crisis at the end of the 1360s: the Dominican basilica of Santa Maria Novella, the Franciscan basilica of Santa Croce, the guild oratory of Orsanmichele, the cathedral workshops of Florence and Orvieto. He signed his name on two surviving works of the first importance, one painted and one carved. He was, uniquely among his Florentine contemporaries, active as painter, sculptor and architect, and the sources credit him with verse as well. He directed a family workshop whose products supplied altarpieces to Florentine churches for half a century.

And yet the picture that emerges from the modern literature is curiously unstable. For four centuries Orcagna was known chiefly as the author of works he did not make. He was credited with the frescoes of the Camposanto at Pisa, with the Loggia della Signoria, with a body of panel painting that has since been redistributed among his brothers and a shifting population of anonymous masters. In the twentieth century he acquired a second, quite different celebrity: he became the exemplary artist of the post-plague reaction, the painter in whose work a supposedly confident, humane, spatially coherent Giottesque tradition was seen to falter and turn back towards hieratic abstraction. That reading, advanced with great learning and rhetorical force in 1951 by [Millard Meiss](/scholars.html#meiss-millard), dominated Anglophone accounts of Tuscan painting for a generation, and it still shapes the way Orcagna is introduced in general surveys and museum labels. It has also, in its essentials, been substantially revised — and one of the two works on which it was originally built is no longer thought to be by the artist to whom Meiss attributed it, nor even to postdate the plague.

The result is that Orcagna is habitually described in terms of what he supposedly represents rather than what he demonstrably made. The purpose of this essay is to reverse that emphasis: to set out, as precisely as the evidence allows, what is documented about Andrea di Cione's life and career; what survives of his work and in what condition; what the early sources actually say, and where they can be shown to be wrong; and how the modern historiography has arrived at its present, considerably more cautious position. Where the evidence is contested — and it very often is — the disagreement is reported rather than resolved.

A methodological note is necessary at the outset. This essay observes a deliberate economy in its citation of modern scholarship, naming only three specialists: [Richard Offner](/scholars.html#offner-richard), whose connoisseurial work provided the foundation for every subsequent discussion of the Cione workshop; Millard Meiss, whose 1951 book framed the interpretative debate; and [Gert Kreytenberg](/scholars.html#kreytenberg-gert), the author of the standard modern monograph on the artist and of the principal encyclopaedia treatments. Other scholarly contributions are described by date, venue and argument rather than by author's name. The early sources — Franco Sacchetti, Lorenzo Ghiberti, the Libro di Antonio Billi, the Anonimo Magliabechiano, Giorgio Vasari — are named throughout, since they are the objects of analysis rather than instruments of it. Claims that rest on a single source, or that are disputed, are flagged as such.

II. Name, Family and Origins

Andrea was the son of a Florentine named Cione, from whom he and his brothers took their patronymic. Three brothers are securely attested as artists: Nardo di Cione, a painter, who died around 1365 or 1366; Jacopo di Cione, also a painter, documented from the mid-1360s and active until the closing years of the century; and Matteo di Cione, a sculptor, who worked alongside Andrea at Orsanmichele and accompanied him to Orvieto. The precise birth order is unrecorded, though Vasari's assertion that Nardo was the elder has been widely repeated, and Jacopo was certainly the youngest of the four.

It is worth stating plainly that Benci di Cione, the architect associated with the Loggia della Signoria and with much of the fabric of Orsanmichele, was not a member of this family. His full name was Benci di Cione Dami; his father Cione was an architect of Comasque origin who had settled in Florence and was dead by April 1348. The coincidence of patronymic — "Cione" being a common Florentine hypocoristic, possibly abbreviated from Belfincione — has generated a persistent error, still repeated on museum websites and in guidebooks, that makes Benci a brother of Orcagna and thereby draws the Loggia into the family orbit. The two men were unrelated, though they did work in proximity: both appear among the artists consulted on the Florentine cathedral in the mid-1360s.

The nickname is more interesting than it looks. "Orcagna" is a Florentine vernacular corruption of Arcagnolo or Arcagnuolo, itself derived from Arcangelo — archangel. Kreytenberg reports that contemporaries applied the epithet to Andrea admiringly, and Vasari, who devoted a long and enthusiastic Life to him, treats the name as already fixed by tradition. The form is unstable in the early sources: some documents and manuscripts give the family name as "di Cione Arcagnuolo," which has occasionally been read as evidence that the grandfather was called Arcangelo, and which lies behind the form "Andrea di Cione di Arcangelo" found in modern reference works. The philological question remains genuinely open. What matters is that by the 1390s, when Sacchetti was writing, "l'Orcagna" was a name a Florentine reader would recognise without explanation.

Of Andrea's birth date nothing is known directly. Vasari states that he died at sixty, which — taken with the documented death in 1368 — yields a birth around 1308, and this is the figure most frequently repeated. Kreytenberg regards it as too early and proposes a birth between about 1315 and 1320, on the grounds that a man born in 1308 would be expected to appear in the guild records well before 1343. The disagreement is not trivial: a birth in 1308 makes Orcagna a contemporary of the last Giotto workshop and a mature painter at the time of the Black Death; a birth in 1318 makes him a member of the generation that came of age in the plague's immediate aftermath. Neither hypothesis can be proved, and both remain in circulation.

Nor is anything known of his training. Vasari names Andrea Pisano as his master in sculpture and, in a passage that has been much quoted and little tested, implies an apprenticeship in the Giottesque orbit. The visual evidence points elsewhere, or at least more widely. The earliest works plausibly given to Andrea show a close engagement with Maso di Banco — the breadth of form, the massive and simplified drapery, the intensity of unmodulated colour — and with Taddeo Gaddi, whose linear rhythms and doctrinal cast of mind seem to have suited him. Kreytenberg regards Maso and Taddeo as the two decisive presences for the young Andrea while acknowledging that we simply do not know in whose shop he served.

Of his domestic circumstances the documents preserve a few facts. In 1349 he was living in the quarter of San Giovanni, near the Porta di Balla, in the parish of San Michele Visdomini; from 1354 he lived in the parish of San Lorenzo. A document of 1377, drawn up nine years after his death, records that he had been married to a woman named Francesca and that he left two daughters, Tessa and Romola. There is no evidence that any of his children continued the workshop; that fell to Jacopo.

III. The Documentary Spine, 1343–1368

Orcagna's career can be reconstructed from archival records with a precision unusual for a Trecento artist, and it is worth setting the sequence out plainly before turning to the works, since almost every attribution argument depends on it.

1343. Andrea is recorded working as a painter at Santa Maria Novella for the Compagnia di Gesù Pellegrino, a lay confraternity attached to the Dominican convent. This is his first documentary appearance.

1343–46. He is inscribed in the Arte dei Medici e degli Speziali, the guild of physicians and apothecaries, within which Florentine painters were organised as a subgroup. The registers are not precisely dated; 1343–44 is the usual reading.

1346. An altarpiece of the Annunciation painted for the Florentine church of San Remigio carries a donor inscription naming the wool merchant Bellozzo di Bartolo and the signature formula Andreas Cionis de Florentia me pinxit, with the date 1346. The panel later entered the Conti Gerli collection in Milan. Its evidential value is now almost nil: it was in very poor condition and was so extensively repainted in a restoration after 1947 that Kreytenberg judged it could scarcely any longer be regarded as Andrea's work in any material sense. What survives is essentially a modern surface over a fourteenth-century compositional idea.

c. 1348–49. In connection with a polyptych planned for San Giovanni Fuorcivitas in Pistoia — a commission that ultimately went to Taddeo Gaddi — Andrea's name appears on a list of the best painters in Florence. The document is preserved in the account books of the Pistoiese Opera and was published in the nineteenth century among the annotations to Vasari. Its significance is that it establishes Andrea's high standing among his peers well before any of his surviving major works.

1349. Recorded as resident in the parish of San Michele Visdomini.

1352. Two crucial entries. Andrea paints a panel of the Madonna, now lost, for the Compagnia di Orsanmichele. In the same year he matriculates in the Arte dei Maestri di Pietra e Legname — the guild of stone- and woodworkers — shortly after receiving the commission for the Orsanmichele tabernacle. The sequence matters: he joined the builders' guild because he had taken on a work in marble, not the reverse. He never ceased to describe himself as a painter.

1354. Tommaso di Rossello Strozzi commissions the polyptych for the family chapel in the left transept of Santa Maria Novella.

1355. Payments for the Orsanmichele tabernacle are documented from this year onward.

June–August 1357. At the cathedral works of Santa Maria del Fiore, a dispute arises over the form to be given to the nave piers. Francesco Talenti, the capomaestro, and Andrea each present a model. Andrea's is approved; nevertheless a second design by Talenti is ultimately adopted. This episode — a painter-sculptor competing on equal terms with the cathedral's chief architect, winning the vote, and then losing the commission — has been the subject of a specialised literature since a much-cited article in The Burlington Magazine in 1971 reopened the question of what Orcagna was actually doing in that year.

1357. The Strozzi altarpiece is signed and dated.

June 1358. Andrea contracts with the Opera del Duomo of Orvieto. The terms are precise and revealing: he will complete the Orsanmichele tabernacle within about fourteen months, then take up the post of capomaestro at Orvieto for a salary of three hundred florins a year, for a maximum of five years, with four months' notice of termination on either side. The salary is very substantial, and the fourteen-month clause tells us the Orvietan authorities were prepared to wait for him.

1359. The Orsanmichele tabernacle is signed and dated. In October Andrea takes up the Orvieto post, bringing his brother Matteo as assistant.

1359–60. At Orvieto his principal charge is the mosaic decoration of the cathedral façade. He remains only until March 1360. In 1360 he collaborated with a local master, Giovanni di Buccio di Leonardello, on the cartoon for a mosaic of the Baptism of Christ for the gable over the left portal.

September 1360 – 1362. He returns to Orvieto in September 1360 to rescind his contract and to finish the mosaic within three months. In early 1361 the mosaic is measured; in September 1362 it is valued, and — after doubts are raised about its structural soundness — priced at sixty florins. None of the original fourteenth-century façade mosaics survives; the present decoration is the result of later campaigns and replacements.

October 1364. Back in Florence, Andrea takes part in a consultation at the cathedral concerning the structure of the nave walls, the possible installation of a gallery, and the form of the windows.

July 1366 – October 1367. The definitive structure of the cathedral choir is determined — a centrally planned volume to be annexed to the longitudinal body. The resulting model was the product of collective deliberation by a body of masons and painters, among whom Andrea held a prominent position. A rival competition entry had been submitted by Simone Talenti and Giovanni di Lapo Ghini; the winning model, agreed in August 1366, became binding for decades.

13 September 1367. The Arte del Cambio, the guild of money-changers, commissions Andrea, for forty florins, an altarpiece with a full-length St Matthew and lateral compartments, to clothe one of the piers of Orsanmichele.

November 1367 – June 1368. The captains of Orsanmichele press him vigorously to complete a fresco of the Madonna in their Audience Chamber. The work drags on; in March 1368 his goods are distrained; in June, following completion, they are returned. The fresco is lost.

25 August 1368. Jacopo di Cione promises the consuls of the Arte del Cambio that he will execute the panels of the St Matthew altarpiece. The document states that Andrea is ill.

End of 1368. Andrea is presumed to have died. There is no surviving death record, and the frequently cited date of 25 August 1368 — which appears in a number of reference works, including widely consulted online sources — is almost certainly a misreading: it is the date of Jacopo's undertaking, not of Andrea's death. Since the artist was alive in June 1368 when his goods were restored, and since Jacopo's promise of August cites illness rather than death, the terminus is best given as late 1368.

This is a career of extraordinary institutional density. Between 1352 and 1368 Andrea was simultaneously running a large painting workshop, directing a monumental sculptural project, holding a cathedral appointment in another city, and sitting on the advisory bodies that determined the shape of Florence's principal building site. The distraint of his goods in 1368 is not a sign of failure but of overcommitment; Kreytenberg observes that in 1367–68 Andrea could not meet all his obligations because he had too many of them and was seriously ill.

IV. The Sources: Sacchetti, Ghiberti, Vasari and the Making of a Reputation

Orcagna is one of the very few Trecento artists who appears as a character in fourteenth-century literature. In the hundred and thirty-sixth story of Franco Sacchetti's Trecentonovelle, written in the last decade of the century, a group of painters and other masters dining at San Miniato al Monte fall into an after-dinner argument. It is "l'Orcagna" — identified by Sacchetti as chief master of the noble oratory of Our Lady of Orsanmichele — who poses the question that starts it: who was the greatest master of painting, apart from Giotto? The company proposes Cimabue, Stefano, Bernardo, Buffalmacco, and others; Taddeo Gaddi replies that many able painters have worked in ways beyond human capacity, but that the art is declining daily. The story then veers into a comic set-piece, but the frame is precious. It shows that within a generation of his death Orcagna was remembered not merely as a maker but as the kind of artist who convened debates about the history and standing of his profession — and it registers, in Taddeo's answer, an anxiety about decline that Florentine painters themselves apparently articulated.

Sacchetti also identifies him by his office at Orsanmichele rather than by his paintings. Two decades after the tabernacle was finished, that was the achievement by which a literate Florentine would place him.

The most important art-historical source is Lorenzo Ghiberti, writing in the Commentarii in the mid-fifteenth century. Ghiberti attributes to Andrea, at Santa Maria Novella, the Strozzi altarpiece, the frescoes of the main chapel of the choir, and "a great many other things"; at Santa Croce, "three magnificent istorie," which other sources allow us to identify as the Triumph of Death, the Last Judgement and the Hell, together with the decoration, now lost, of a chapel; two chapels at Santissima Annunziata, both destroyed; and the refectory of Santo Spirito. Crucially, Ghiberti assigns the frescoes of the Strozzi Chapel itself — as distinct from the altarpiece — to Nardo. This single sentence is the foundation of the modern separation of the two brothers' hands, and it has held up remarkably well.

The fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Florentine compilations — the Libro di Antonio Billi, the Codice Magliabechiano, [Giovanni Battista Gelli](/scholars.html#gelli-giovan-battista)'s lives — add further attributions of varying reliability. The tradition culminates in Vasari, whose Life of Orcagna appears in both the 1550 and 1568 editions of the Vite and whose account is by far the fullest, the most influential, and the least trustworthy.

Vasari's contribution is threefold. First, he transmits genuine information: the derivation of the name, the age at death, the identification of the tabernacle as Andrea's masterpiece in architecture and sculpture, the note that Andrea also wrote verse. Second, he supplies a critical judgement of real acuity: he observes that the Orsanmichele tabernacle, though di maniera tedesca — that is, Gothic — nonetheless achieves "grace and proportion." Coming from a writer temperamentally hostile to Gothic form, this is a considered concession, and it points, as later critics have recognised, to the tabernacle's unusual structural coherence, gathered upward and held by the dome, beneath its dense encrustation of ornament.

Third, and most consequentially, Vasari attributes to Orcagna works that are not his. Two errors have proved especially tenacious.

The first is the Camposanto at Pisa. Vasari assigns to Andrea, as early works, the great mural cycle of the Triumph of Death, the Last Judgement and the Hell in the Pisan burial ground, and explicitly states that Andrea afterwards repeated the same subjects in Santa Croce. The attribution held for roughly four centuries and shaped every account of the artist written before the twentieth century. It has since been comprehensively dismantled. The Camposanto frescoes were successively reassigned — to Bernardo Daddi, to Pietro Lorenzetti, to Vitale da Bologna, and, in Meiss's confident but contested proposal, to the Pisan painter Francesco Traini. In 1974 a monograph devoted to the question argued for Buonamico Buffalmacco and, decisively, for a date before the plague of 1348. That attribution and that dating are now the majority position, though not unanimous.

The second is the Loggia della Signoria. Vasari states that Orcagna designed it, and later writers repeated him; in the nineteenth century the building came to be called the Loggia dell'Orcagna, a name still current. The documents are unambiguous. Construction began in 1376 — more than eight years after Andrea's death, and years after the purchase and demolition of the houses that cleared the site — under the direction of Benci di Cione Dami and Simone di Francesco Talenti, and continued to 1382. Kreytenberg rules out Andrea's authorship on chronological grounds alone. Some accounts allow that he may have contributed to a project or model at an earlier stage, since a resolution to build a loggia had been taken as early as 1356, but no document connects him to the design, and a nineteenth-century commentator made the sensible objection that a design produced before the site was cleared could hardly have survived the process of execution intact.

A third Vasarian claim deserves separate treatment because it belongs to literary rather than art history. Vasari reports that Andrea took pleasure in writing verse and that, in old age, he addressed sonnets to the young Burchiello. This is chronologically impossible: Domenico di Giovanni, called il Burchiello, was born in 1404, thirty-six years after Andrea's death. The underlying tradition, however, is not simply an invention. Florentine literary history recognises a comic-nonsense manner known as rimare alla burchia, of which Burchiello was the great populariser rather than the inventor, and the precursors named in that tradition are Franco Sacchetti and "an Orcagna, painter." Whether that Orcagna is Andrea di Cione or a later namesake has been questioned since the nineteenth century, and at least one authoritative reference work registers doubt that the poet was the famous artist. The safest statement is that Andrea had a reputation as a versifier, that a strand of Florentine comic poetry was associated with his name, and that Vasari's specific anecdote about Burchiello is untenable.

The cumulative effect of these errors is worth emphasising. For most of its history, the Orcagna known to European art lovers was largely a construction: a painter of terrifying Pisan frescoes and a designer of one of the most admired secular structures in Florence. Strip away the Camposanto and the Loggia, and what remains is a smaller, stranger, and considerably more interesting artist.

V. Beginnings: Santa Maria Novella and the Maso–Taddeo Horizon

The earliest works given to Orcagna with any confidence are frescoes at Santa Maria Novella, and they are fragments.

Beneath the fifteenth-century ornamental bands on two ribs of the vault of the main chapel — the cappella maggiore, entirely refrescoed by Domenico Ghirlandaio at the end of the fifteenth century — thirty-five painted quatrefoils containing busts of saints and prophets were recovered in 1940. They are the only surviving trace of a monumental decorative campaign. Documents relating to the chapel between 1340 and 1348 have been used to date them around 1345. The decoration was begun in 1348, when it was agreed that the Tornaquinci family, who intended to bear the cost, would not thereby acquire burial rights in the chapel — the Ricci held the patronage — but might display their arms there. A campaign on this scale must have occupied several years and required assistants; Kreytenberg distinguishes Andrea's hand from those of two other painters in the surviving vault fragments.

Closely related in style, and perhaps slightly earlier, are a Nativity and a Crucifixion frescoed in the Chiostrino dei Morti at Santa Maria Novella, in the Tornaquinci-Trinciavelli burial chapel behind the chapter house. In these the debt to Maso di Banco and to Taddeo Gaddi in their maturity around 1340 is at its clearest — broad, weighty forms achieved by intense passages of colour, combined with a searching linear rhythm. Some critics have detected a temperamental affinity with Giottino: a certain dryness of drawing, a restrained dramatic intensity, an isolation of each figure within the composition. The two Chiostrino scenes were at one point attributed to Giottino outright, and Kreytenberg assigns them to one of the assistants distinguishable in the choir vault rather than to Andrea himself. The attributions remain unsettled.

To the same early orbit belongs one of the most historically remarkable images of Trecento Florence: the Expulsion of the Duke of Athens, a large circular fresco, roughly three metres in diameter, painted for a wall of the Stinche, the city's prison for political detainees. On 26 July 1343 — the feast of St Anne — the Florentines rose against Gualtieri VI of Brienne, Duke of Athens, whose brief tyranny had followed the banking collapses of the preceding years; the prison was stormed and the political prisoners freed. The fresco commemorates the event allegorically, showing the duke driven from a Palazzo Vecchio still in its medieval state, under the protective gaze of St Anne, to whom the emblems of the city are offered. St Anne was thereafter honoured as a protectress of Florentine liberty.

Two things make the work exceptional: it is one of the earliest surviving depictions of a contemporary political event in Florentine art, and it preserves one of the oldest views of the Palazzo Vecchio. The attribution to Orcagna is longstanding but has always been qualified. The fresco has at various times been assigned to the workshop or the circle, and is now most often referred to the young master himself; Kreytenberg regards the imprint of Maso di Banco as even more evident here than in the Santa Maria Novella fragments, and dates it not long after the events of 1343. The proposed range is generally 1343–49. The painting was detached from the wall in 1964, restored, and given to the city; it is displayed in the Mezzanino of Palazzo Vecchio, an area only intermittently open to the public and returned to the museum circuit in recent years.

Two panel paintings complete the early group, both of them problematic. The signed Annunciation of 1346 has already been discussed: its repainted state removes it from serious stylistic argument, though Kreytenberg's reconstruction of its original design — the oblique loggia, the V-shaped disposition of the figures, the symbolic vase set on the ground between them on the axis of a now-lost pinnacle — describes an unusually inventive treatment of a standard subject, in which architecture is made to both separate and join the participants. A triptych of the Madonna between the Magdalen and St Ansanus, now in the Rijksmuseum, is dated 1350 and connected with frescoes of the life of St Thomas painted for Tommaso Baronci in the chapel of Sant'Ansano at Santa Maria Maggiore in Florence. Stylistically close to it is the polyptych from Santissima Annunziata, now in the Galleria dell'Accademia, whose squat figural proportions suggest a considerable interval before the Strozzi altarpiece and possibly a date around 1347–48.

What this early material shows, in aggregate, is a painter formed in the most solid and least decorative current of post-Giottesque Florence, preoccupied with mass, weight and the legibility of a figure in space. Whatever happened in the 1350s, it did not happen to a painter who had never understood how to construct a body or a room.

VI. Santa Croce: The Triumph of Death, the Last Judgement and the Hell

The most ambitious pictorial undertaking of Orcagna's career survives only in ruins. On the right-hand wall of the nave of Santa Croce — the great Franciscan church on the other side of the city from his Dominican patrons — Andrea painted an immense mural comprising three linked subjects: a Triumph of Death, a Last Judgement, and a Hell. Ghiberti calls them "three magnificent istorie"; Vasari describes them and, in the same breath, wrongly claims that they repeat what Andrea had already done at Pisa.

The wall did not survive the sixteenth century. Vasari's own reordering of Santa Croce, with its rank of stone altars set against the nave walls, together with plastering and later the damp and the 1966 flood, destroyed the greater part of the cycle. Fragments were recovered in two campaigns, in 1911 and — notably, in the case of the Hell fragment found behind an altar — in 1942. Six fragments from the ensemble are now displayed in the Museo dell'Opera di Santa Croce, installed in the old refectory alongside Cimabue's Crucifix and Taddeo Gaddi's Tree of Life and Last Supper, and arranged so as to make the original narrative as legible as the losses allow.

What can still be read is remarkable. Along the outer band of the composition ran the fifteen signs that, according to a widespread medieval tradition, would precede the Last Judgement. In the first surviving scene, men observe extraordinary portents in the sky, above all the darkening of the sun. In the second, an earthquake brings down the buildings of a city — a passage of vehement spatial and architectural description. In the third, people watch the sea rise. The Last Judgement itself is lost; by analogy with the Pisan Judgement it is presumed to have had Christ as judge at the centre, with the blessed and the damned at the lower extremities. In the surviving fragment at the lower right, beside a twisted column, one sees a damned soul wearing a headdress of oriental fashion, a black demon acting as a doorkeeper, and the gate of Hell surmounted by a vulture-shaped demon pronouncing the Dantean verse commanding the abandonment of hope. Other fragments show the punishment of the lustful; the punishment of the avaricious and the wrathful, with Satan devouring Judas, Brutus and Cassius; and Lucifer clawing two giants and Antenor.

The Dantean armature is unmistakable and has been recognised as such since the fragment's rediscovery. This is a monumental painting organised, at least in part, by the Inferno — a fact of some importance for the history of Dante illustration, since the Santa Croce fragment stands between Giotto's Arena Chapel Judgement of 1304 and the Pisan Camposanto Hell, and belongs to the same Florentine milieu that produced Nardo's Dantean Hell in the Strozzi Chapel.

The dating is the crux, and it remains genuinely unresolved. Proposals cluster around two poles. One school places the cycle in the artist's late maturity, around 1360–65, on the grounds of its affinity with parts of the Orsanmichele tabernacle and the theatrical, declamatory character of its tragic effect. The other, argued by Kreytenberg, places it early, around 1345, citing the pronounced interest in spatial representation — the earthquake scene above all — and the plastic corporeality and harmonious proportions of the figures, which also account for the closeness to Maso di Banco. Some catalogue entries compromise at "around 1350." The difference matters enormously for the Meiss thesis: an early date makes the Santa Croce cycle a pre-plague meditation on death and judgement rather than a response to one.

There is a further, quieter point. Whichever date is preferred, the Santa Croce murals show Orcagna working at maximum scale in a narrative and dramatic mode, and doing so with evident relish. The nineteenth- and twentieth-century characterisation of him as an artist temperamentally averse to narrative, who reduced istoria to icon, is difficult to sustain against these fragments — which is precisely why their date has been so heavily contested.

VII. The Strozzi Altarpiece, 1354–1357

The polyptych on the altar of the Strozzi Chapel in the left transept of Santa Maria Novella is the only painting entirely by Orcagna that has come down to us intact, and it is signed and dated: Anno Domini MCCCLVII Andreas Cionis de Florentia me pinxit. It was commissioned in 1354 by Tommaso di Rossello Strozzi.

The setting

The chapel itself had been completed around 1335 and stands slightly off-axis at the head of the transept because the campanile occupies the space behind the left portion of the end wall. It was dedicated from its foundation to St Thomas Aquinas — a dedication that determines everything about the altarpiece's iconography and that also, conveniently, echoed the patron's own name. Between about 1350 and 1357 the walls were frescoed by Nardo di Cione, with marginal participation by other hands, in a programme devoted almost entirely to the Last Judgement, distributed across the three walls with Paradise on one side and a Dante-derived Hell on the other. It has often been suggested in the literature that Tommaso Strozzi undertook the decoration in expiation of the sin of usury; the proposal is plausible in the context of Florentine banking piety but is an inference rather than a documented fact.

The chapel is therefore a coordinated ensemble by two brothers, and one of the very few places where the distinct temperaments of Andrea and Nardo can be compared directly on site. Ghiberti already understood the division of labour, and it has never been seriously challenged.

Structure and format

The panel is a five-compartment polyptych with a painted predella of three scenes. Its most consequential formal decision is negative: Orcagna suppressed the twisted colonnettes that ordinarily divide the compartments of a Florentine polyptych. In doing so he abolished the additive logic of the type — a row of discrete saints, each in his own gilded box — and produced a single continuous painted field within a frame that has become part of the representation rather than a container for it.

Kreytenberg's analysis of this device is the most searching in the literature. The pointed arches of the frame, ornamented with a small arcaded frieze, are detached from the plane of the panel so that a zone of shadow of indefinite depth opens between the two. The result is that the frame reads simultaneously as architecture and as a threshold into pictorial space. The ornamental motifs of the floor, which are not scaled perspectivally and therefore assert the flatness of the surface, run continuously only beneath the central figure of Christ; at the sides they are interrupted by figures and thus become legible in a quasi-spatial sense. The outer pairs of saints stand obliquely, one behind the other, generating diagonals that are continued in the projecting angles of the frame.

This is not a painter abolishing space. It is a painter constructing a very precise equilibrium between two-dimensional assertion and three-dimensional description — an equilibrium in which the flattest passage is reserved for the theophany at the centre.

The programme

At the centre, within a mandorla of seraphim, Christ appears frontally, as in a vision. He does not sit in a narrative or a devotional setting; he manifests. With one hand he hands a book — the symbol of ecclesiastical doctrine — to St Thomas Aquinas, who kneels at his right in a position of exceptional privilege and is presented by the Virgin. With the other he gives the keys, the sign of the Church's authority, to St Peter, presented by John the Baptist. Behind the two kneeling saints, the Virgin and the Baptist flank Christ to form a Deesis. The outer pairs are the Archangel Michael and St Catherine of Alexandria on one side, St Paul and St Lawrence on the other. (Michael is sometimes misidentified in guidebooks as St George; the confusion arises from the dragon.)

Two things are unusual here, and both were noticed early. First, the depiction of an adult, full-length Christ as the principal figure of a Florentine or Sienese altarpiece is close to unprecedented in the fourteenth century; the norm is the Virgin and Child. Second, the subject — the simultaneous transmission of doctrine and of jurisdiction — has no ready parallel in Trecento panel painting. Its ancestry lies in the Traditio legis of early Christian and Roman art, and behind that in the imagery of papal authority. The choice of a hieratic, frontal, visionary Christ is thus not merely a matter of style; it is a return to a specific and highly charged iconographic tradition, made in a Dominican chapel dedicated to the order's greatest theologian, at a moment when the Dominicans of Santa Maria Novella were vigorously asserting the doctrinal authority of the Church.

The predella extends the argument. Its three scenes are the Mass of St Thomas Aquinas; St Peter saved from the waters, an adaptation of the Navicella; and the death of the Emperor Henry II, whose soul is saved when the scales tip in his favour on account of his donation of a golden chalice. The unifying theme is not the lives of the saints but the sacramental and doctrinal machinery of the Church itself: the Mass, the primacy of Peter, and the efficacy of pious donation. In a chapel paid for by a banker, the last of these is not an idle choice.

Notably, the predella is also the most spatially developed part of the ensemble. Where the main register presses towards the plane, the small scenes below open into depth. This distribution — hieratic above, narrative and spatial below — is a deliberate rhetorical arrangement, not an inconsistency.

Style

The formal character of the altarpiece is easy to describe and hard to evaluate fairly. The figures detach themselves from the gold ground with abrupt, statuesque clarity; the modelling is compact; the drapery folds are handled with an angular, faceted incisiveness; the colour is intense, saturated and, in places, almost violent in its contrasts. There is no atmospheric softening. Every form is closed.

Read one way — and this is essentially how it was read for most of the twentieth century — the picture registers a retreat from Giottesque naturalism into an archaising, anti-illusionistic hieraticism, a "tense, cramped abstract space" belonging to the previous century. Read another way, it is a work of considerable formal ambition in which the artist has unified a traditionally additive format, integrated frame and image, and calibrated the relation of flatness to depth scene by scene, register by register.

Kreytenberg's judgement is that a diminution of spatial illusionism relative to the 1346 Annunciation is undeniable, but that the picture is neither developed two-dimensionally nor purged of pictorial space, as an "anti-illusionist" reading would require. Something similar had been said much earlier from a less sympathetic angle: the 1961 entry in the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani describes the result as a calculated accord between archaic ideality and modern Gothic manner that amounts to compromise and academic pedantry — a harsh verdict, but one that at least identifies the same two poles rather than reducing the picture to one of them.

VIII. The Tabernacle of Orsanmichele, 1352–1359

If the Strozzi altarpiece is the work by which Orcagna is judged, the Orsanmichele tabernacle is the work by which his contemporaries knew him. Sacchetti identifies him by it. A Florentine chronicler writing in 1377 is reported to have mentioned it as the one work of art in the city worth naming — in preference to Giotto's paintings, Andrea Pisano's reliefs and Arnolfo's architecture. It is the only work of sculpture that can be given to Andrea with certainty, and the only surviving evidence of his activity as an architect.

Institution and occasion

Orsanmichele was, and in its lower storey still visibly is, a communal grain loggia; it was also the seat of a lay confraternity devoted to a miracle-working image of the Virgin, and it became the ceremonial church of the Florentine guilds. The panel the tabernacle was built to house is Bernardo Daddi's Madonna and Child with Angels, painted in 1347 to replace an earlier miraculous image damaged by fire.

The commission has a specific and rather uncomfortable institutional background. The plague of 1348 brought the confraternity an extraordinary windfall as the wills of thousands of Florentine victims were opened at once and their bequests distributed. The chronicler [Matteo Villani](/scholars.html#villani-matteo) condemned the captains of Orsanmichele in the strongest terms for their handling of these funds, accusing them of embezzlement and selfishness. Andrea received the commission for a new tabernacle shortly afterwards, in 1352. It has therefore been argued — most fully in a 1981 study in The Art Bulletin devoted to the tabernacle's context, and in a series of specialist articles of the later 1980s and early 1990s — that the project functioned in part as a public and lavishly visible justification of the confraternity's stewardship. The building of an ostentatiously costly shrine is a familiar answer to accusations of misappropriation.

Architecture

The tabernacle is square in plan and stands on a base of three steps. Four corner piers, formed of clustered twisted colonnettes, are joined by round-headed arches and carry a ribbed cross vault. Above rises a crowning stage with four pinnacles over the piers, linked by triangular gables; these enclose a dome of eight segments set on a drum. The arches are closed at the bottom by low balustrades that read as a socle; the interior is accessible only through a very small door at the back.

The rear arch is closed entirely, not merely by the balustrade but by a solid wall. This wall contains a narrow staircase leading to the upper part of the structure. On its inner face — that is, facing into the tabernacle — hangs Daddi's panel. On its outer face is the monumental relief of the Death and Assumption of the Virgin, with which the cycle of reliefs on the socle concludes.

The functional logic of this arrangement is worth spelling out, because it explains features that otherwise look arbitrary. The staircase and the concealed interior served the ritual unveiling of the image: custodians seated inside the structure operated ropes that drew back an actual curtain in front of the Madonna. Angels carved around the opening pull back a marble curtain, permanently rehearsing in stone the ceremony performed intermittently in cloth. As the fourteenth century advanced, the open arcades of the surrounding building were progressively walled up and the image itself increasingly veiled; access to it became a privilege, and the tabernacle is the machinery of that privilege.

The dome on a drum, of eight segments, is architecturally the tabernacle's most consequential idea. Kreytenberg argues that the conception formulated here allowed Andrea to exercise a decisive influence on the design of the choir of Florence Cathedral in 1366–67 — the moment at which the octagonal crossing that Brunelleschi would eventually have to vault was fixed as binding for the future. Whether or not one accepts the full weight of that claim, it is at least the case that the man who built an eight-part dome over a drum in miniature in the 1350s sat on the committee that determined the shape of the cathedral's east end a few years later.

Sculpture

The tabernacle carries something in the region of 120 pieces of figural sculpture — reliefs, statuettes, angels, evangelists, prophets — worked in marble and inlaid with gold, coloured glass and semi-precious stone, producing a dense, jewelled polychromy that is at its most concentrated in the celestial zones and that has the effect of flattening them further. The elaborate surface finds its counterpart in the brocaded textiles of contemporary painting.

The socle carries an alternating sequence of polygonal reliefs: scenes from the Life of the Virgin, and personifications of the Virtues. (Descriptions in the literature differ as to which subjects occupy the hexagonal and which the octagonal fields, and readers consulting different handbooks will find the two assignments reversed; the alternation itself is not in doubt.) Among the narrative reliefs are the Birth of the Virgin, her Education, the Annunciation, the Presentation, and the Annunciation of her death. The sequence culminates in the great rear relief of the Dormition and Assumption. Around the image itself, angels draw back the marble curtain; evangelists with books and further angels stand on the piers and colonnettes, asserting the textual and doctrinal authority behind the Marian devotion.

Andrea cannot have carved all of this himself, and no one has ever supposed he did. A team of sculptors and masons worked to his design, and his brother Matteo was probably among them. What is striking, and what Kreytenberg emphasises, is the severity with which the master imposed a unified conception on his executants. Figures that are demonstrably by different hands — the paired angels bearing the scrolls Ave Maria and Gratia plena are the classic example — correspond so exactly that they must derive from a common model. Differences of quality are visible, and the passages usually singled out as Andrea's own are the Dormition and Assumption, the Nativity, the Annunciation, the Annunciation of the Virgin's death, and the Virtues.

The relief and the signature

The rear relief is the tabernacle's climax and the most-discussed piece of Trecento sculpture in Florence after Andrea Pisano's Baptistery doors. In the lower zone, the Death of the Virgin takes place in a shallow, crowded space in which the figures press close together and almost no foreground is allowed; the sense of too many persons in too little room is deliberate and oppressive. In the upper zone, the Virgin ascends, and here the ground is worked with gilded and coloured marble inlay rather than described as space. St Thomas receives the girdle — the relic venerated at Prato and a subject of local devotional weight, later treated by Nanni di Banco at the Porta della Mandorla. A small realistic touch has often been noted: two figures at the far right of the lower group wear contemporary dress rather than timeless drapery.

The whole is signed and dated: Andreas Cionis pictor Florentinus oratorii archimagister extitit huius MCCCLIX. The formulation deserves attention. On his one certain work of sculpture, Andrea describes himself as pictor — a painter — and as archimagister of this oratory. He did not call himself a sculptor. The wrought grille that now encloses the structure is later, of 1366.

Kreytenberg draws the conclusion that follows from this. The reliefs are conceived as painted images carved in stone: their compositional structure is related to an ideal surface, and this surface and the space of the relief interpenetrate in a controlled synthesis. Andrea thus develops relief beyond the manner of Andrea Pisano and in the direction that Ghiberti would take. At the same time the tabernacle sculpture and the Strozzi altarpiece, exactly contemporary, exhibit a fundamental agreement of conception realised in different materials — a point one can test directly by comparing the Virgin and St Thomas in the Assumption relief with the Christ and St Peter of the panel, or the Ave Maria angel with the painted St Michael.

The older critical tradition reached a related judgement from the opposite direction. Writing in 1961, the Dizionario Biografico entry observed that Orcagna, preoccupied with giving plastic robustness to Andrea Pisano's Gothic forms, showed remarkably little interest in space — and that this apparent contradiction had a real significance, since his essentially volumetric ambition answered a need, unfelt in Florentine sculpture since Nicola Pisano, to give the human form an architectural value complete in itself, as Giotto had already given it in painting. On this reading Orcagna's importance in the history of Florentine sculpture is genuine, though intellectual rather than poetic, and it finds an echo two generations later in Nanni di Banco.

IX. Orvieto, the Cathedral of Florence, and Orcagna as Architect

The three years between 1359 and 1362 are the least visible and, in some ways, the most revealing part of Orcagna's career.

The Orvieto contract of June 1358 is the clearest measure of his standing. The Opera del Duomo of a wealthy papal city was prepared to wait fourteen months for him, to pay him three hundred florins a year, and to appoint him capomaestro of a cathedral whose façade was among the most ambitious sculptural and decorative programmes in Italy — a post previously held by Lorenzo Maitani and then by Andrea Pisano. He took up the appointment in October 1359 with Matteo as his assistant.

His principal charge was the mosaic decoration of the façade. He also appears to have been responsible, or largely responsible, for the great rose window and the tabernacles flanking it. Marble for the window had been purchased in Rome as early as 1354 — broken up, according to the Orvietan accounts, at the temple of Jupiter and carried in the largest possible pieces to the Tiber — and payments for its transport to the river port at Attigliano run through 1356, 1358 and 1359. The window was therefore in preparation before Andrea's arrival and executed under his direction; the attribution to him rests on that circumstance and on early twentieth-century stylistic argument rather than on a document naming him as its designer. The apostles in the niches above the rose window were a fifteenth-century addition intended to correct its proportions, and the prophets in the flanking niches were completed by later hands over the following two centuries.

The mosaics themselves are lost. In 1360 Andrea collaborated with a local master, Giovanni di Buccio di Leonardello, on the cartoon for a Baptism of Christ for the gable over the left portal. He left Orvieto in March 1360 and returned only in September to terminate the contract and complete the work within three months. In early 1361 the mosaic was measured; in September 1362 it was valued, and, because doubts had arisen about its stability, it was priced at sixty florins — a low figure against the salary he had been promised. No fourteenth-century mosaic survives on the Orvieto façade; the present decoration is the accumulated product of later campaigns and wholesale replacements. Andrea's most conspicuous public commission outside Florence is, in material terms, entirely gone.

In Florence his architectural role is documented but always collective and always consultative. In the summer of 1357 he submitted a model for the nave piers of Santa Maria del Fiore in competition with Francesco Talenti; his model was approved and Talenti's second scheme nonetheless adopted. In October 1364 he participated in a consultation on the nave walls, a possible gallery and the form of the windows. Between July 1366 and October 1367 he was a prominent member of the body of masons and painters that produced the definitive model for the choir — a centrally planned volume annexed to the nave — after a competition entered by Simone Talenti and Giovanni di Lapo Ghini.

The pattern is consistent: Orcagna made designs and directed, and no building can be securely assigned to his hand. This is not unusual for a Trecento capomaestro, and it is exactly why the Loggia della Signoria attached itself so easily to his name. The tabernacle remains the sole architectural work that is unambiguously his, and it is a work of architecture in miniature.

X. The Late Works and the Workshop

Three surviving works, all collaborative to some degree, represent the last phase.

The frescoes of the refectory of Santo Spirito — a large Crucifixion above a Last Supper, the standard Tuscan refectory arrangement — are given by Ghiberti to Andrea, and Kreytenberg proposes that he worked on them with Nardo, dating them 1361–63 on the closeness of the figures to those of the Strozzi altarpiece. Others have resisted the attribution: the 1961 Dizionario Biografico entry judged the Crucifixion too late in character to be his. Very little survives. The eastern part of the room was opened onto the piazza in the late nineteenth century when the wing served as a tram depot, destroying the greater part of the decoration; a few fragments were recovered during architectural restoration between 1938 and 1944 — a fragment at the left of the Last Supper, two haloed apostles at the far right, and an Augustinian saint in an aedicule outside the scene. Whoever painted it, the work is historically significant: with Taddeo Gaddi's refectory at Santa Croce it inaugurates the long Florentine series of monumental cenacoli.

The Pentecost triptych now in the Galleria dell'Accademia, from the high altar of Santi Apostoli, is the third surviving altarpiece associated with Andrea. Three panels, the central one wider, terminate in round-headed arches; the frame, though incomplete, is emphatically architectural and evokes a triumphal arch. The Virgin kneels at the centre, seeming almost to hover, surrounded by six kneeling apostles, with three more on each lateral panel converging diagonally towards the centre; the dove descends between two flying angels. Kreytenberg finds here the same rare harmony of planar and spatial ordering, of bodily presence and spiritualisation, achieved in the Strozzi altarpiece, and detects the influence of Giovanni da Milano in the figure style and colour, proposing a date of 1365–67; other accounts give 1362–65 and identify Jacopo's softer, more graduated modelling in some of the apostles. The attributional history is instructive: Vasari thought the triptych was by Spinello Aretino, nineteenth-century guides called it Giottesque school, and it was [Crowe](/scholars.html#crowe-joseph-archer) and [Cavalcaselle](/scholars.html#cavalcaselle-giovanni-battista) who first proposed Orcagna in 1864 and then Orcagna with Jacopo in 1883.

The St Matthew triptych in the Uffizi is the best-documented and most contested work of the group. Commissioned from Andrea by the Arte del Cambio on 13 September 1367 for forty florins, it was designed to wrap around a pier of Orsanmichele — which accounts for its unusual shape. St Matthew, patron of the money-changers, stands full length at the centre; four scenes from his life fill the wings: the Calling and the taming of the dragons on one side, the Assassination and a miracle of resuscitation on the other. Roundels representing gold coins, the guild's device, appear at the tops of the lateral panels. On 25 August 1368 Jacopo undertook to make the panels, Andrea being ill.

The critical question is how much of the work is Andrea's. Kreytenberg's position is uncompromising: given the burden of commissions and the gravity of his illness in 1367–68, it is very unlikely that Andrea produced even the design. He points to the triptych's close stylistic affinity with the predella panels of Jacopo's San Pier Maggiore polyptych, now divided among several museums, and with Jacopo's Crucifixion in the National Gallery in London; on this reading the triptych represents not the terminus of Andrea's development but the beginning of Jacopo's independent career. Another line of argument, advanced in the 1970s and subsequently accepted by some Italian specialists, excludes Jacopo from the actual execution — holding that he intervened only to discharge the contractual obligation — and assigns the painting to an artist of his workshop known as the Master of the Ashmolean Predella. Older criticism, from [Milanesi](/scholars.html#milanesi-gaetano)'s revised opinion of 1885 onward, treated the work as conceived by Andrea and executed by Jacopo, and this remains the compromise formula found on most labels.

The importance of the dispute lies in what the triptych looks like. Its layout — a large hieratic saint flanked by scenes from his life — is a conscious return to thirteenth-century models, and the gold ground and surface dominate to a degree unmatched in Andrea's securely documented painting, with a marked reduction of both spatial and volumetric illusionism. Meiss took precisely this kind of retrospection as characteristic of art after the plague. If the triptych is Jacopo's invention rather than Andrea's, then the most archaising work in the traditional Orcagna corpus is evidence about the next generation, not about him — and, as Kreytenberg observes, the works of the 1360s that are his show a painter moving towards a freer, not a more rigid, formal language.

The triptych's subsequent history illustrates how such objects travelled. When the painted panels were removed from the piers of Orsanmichele in 1402, it went first to the old hospital of San Matteo, then to the hospital church of Santa Maria Nuova, where it remained until 1860; it later hung in the hospital's picture gallery, which the Uffizi acquired at the turn of the twentieth century.

XI. What "Orcagnesque" Means

Orcagna's importance for Florentine painting after 1350 was institutional as much as artistic. He ran the largest and best-connected workshop in the city, employing numerous assistants and collaborators, and the products of that shop and its satellites — conventionally grouped as orcagneschi or cioneschi — dominate the Florentine altarpiece for the rest of the century.

Yet the family resemblance is deceptive. Two observations recur in the literature. The first is that the painters of the later Trecento generally absorbed the manner of Nardo, or of Jacopo, more readily than Andrea's own: Nardo's lyrical vein, poetic sensibility and soft, colouristically differentiated modelling proved easier to imitate than Andrea's angular severity. The second is that the diffusion of an "Orcagnesque" idiom has repeatedly caused works to be assigned to Andrea that belong to his brothers or to anonymous shop hands.

The disentangling of this material is the achievement of connoisseurship, and above all of Richard Offner, whose Critical and Historical Corpus of Florentine Painting devoted a full volume of its fourth section to Andrea di Cione as painter. Offner's method — the patient comparison of morphological detail, of the drawing of hands, ears and eyelids, of the handling of drapery folds and of punched ornament — produced a series of reassignments that materially reduced Andrea's corpus. Among the most consequential was his transfer, in the 1920s, of two panels of St John the Baptist and St Paul at Yale from Orcagna to Nardo. That judgement stood unchallenged for eighty years until a specialist re-examination in 2004 restored the Baptist to Andrea while leaving the Paul with Nardo, a division maintained in the 2023 catalogue of the Yale collection. The episode is characteristic of the field: attributions in the Cione orbit are provisional, they oscillate, and they oscillate slowly.

The same instability affects works in other collections. A Madonna of Humility in Washington, long catalogued as Orcagna and later as a joint work of Andrea and Jacopo, is now given to Jacopo alone; Kreytenberg himself published a paper on its attribution and iconography in 1990, and had earlier proposed that another Madonna of Humility might be Andrea's latest surviving work. Panels from the same dispersed altarpieces turn up in London, New York, Oxford and elsewhere, their attributions shifting with each new catalogue.

The practical consequence for anyone approaching Orcagna is that the secure corpus is very small: the Strozzi altarpiece; the Orsanmichele tabernacle, with the qualification that much of its carving is by assistants; the Santa Croce fragments, on the strength of Ghiberti's testimony; and a group of frescoes and panels held with varying degrees of confidence. Almost everything else is negotiable.

XII. Historiography: Meiss and After

No account of Orcagna can avoid the interpretative framework erected around him in the twentieth century, and none should adopt it uncritically.

In Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death, published in 1951, Millard Meiss argued that Tuscan society understood the calamities of the mid-Trecento — plague, banking collapse, famine, political upheaval — as divine punishment for worldliness, and that this understanding produced a rejection of the human-centred, naturalistic pictorial style of the early fourteenth century in favour of a revival of the spiritually centred, abstract manner of the Dugento. His book opens with Orcagna's Strozzi altarpiece; his second principal exhibit was the Triumph of Death in the Camposanto at Pisa, which he attributed to Francesco Traini. The chapter headings of the first section — "From narrative to ritual," "Exaltation of God, the Church and the priest," "The supernatural," "Recovery of the Dugento" — state the thesis in miniature.

The book's influence was enormous and, in one respect, deserved: it treated style as historically caused rather than merely described, and it integrated connoisseurship, iconography, social history and religious literature in a way that few art-historical monographs had attempted. For a generation it was the paradigm within which Trecento specialists worked, and it remains the reason most educated readers encounter Orcagna's name at all.

The criticisms began early — a sceptical review appeared in The Art Bulletin the following year — and accumulated in three waves.

The first, gathering in the 1970s, questioned whether the stylistic change had occurred at all in the terms Meiss described. A 1975 study of Florentine painting on the eve of the Renaissance and a 1979 study of Sienese painting of 1330–70 both argued that the supposed mid-century rupture dissolved on close inspection of the actual sequence of works.

The second accepted that something changed but rejected Meiss's explanation. A 1979 contribution to a survey of Italian art-historical method proposed that the change reflected a younger generation's reverence for the "golden age" of earlier masters. A 1981 article in Art History attributed it to changing demographics and patterns of patronage rather than to a spiritual crisis. A 1997 reassessment of painting in the age of Giotto offered the deflating explanation that artistically weaker painters were repeating aspects of earlier models. A 2007 monograph on Sienese painting after the plague argued for a "pluralistic" artistic culture in which continuity and change were held in deliberate balance, and located the cause in altered workshop organisation and a new art market rather than in penitential religiosity.

The third and most damaging line of attack was not interpretative but factual. Meiss's Pisan exhibit has been reassigned. The Camposanto Triumph of Death is now generally given to Buonamico Buffalmacco and dated before 1348, on the arguments of the 1974 monograph mentioned above. If that is right, then one of the two pillars of the thesis is neither by the painter Meiss named nor a response to the event he invoked. Meanwhile the dating of Orcagna's own Santa Croce Triumph of Death remains unresolved between roughly 1345 and roughly 1365, which means it cannot be used to settle the question either way.

A fourth observation, less often made, concerns the internal evidence of Orcagna's own career. The Strozzi altarpiece dates from 1354–57, six to nine years after the plague, and its most conspicuous "archaisms" — the frontal visionary Christ, the Traditio legis type, the emphasis on doctrine and priesthood — are demanded by a chapel dedicated to Thomas Aquinas in the mother house of Florentine Dominican theology. An iconographic programme dictated by a specific patron and a specific dedication is weak evidence for a general shift in collective sensibility. And the works of the 1360s, on Kreytenberg's reading, show Andrea moving towards a freer formal language, not a more rigid one; the rigid, gold-dominated, thirteenth-century-inflected St Matthew triptych, so often cited as the endpoint of his development, may well belong to Jacopo.

Kreytenberg proposes a different interpretative frame, drawn from a 1986 study of the meaning of central perspective. On this account Orcagna's significance lies in his having given form to two opposed spiritual currents — a Franciscan narrative painting saturated with realism and spatiality, and a Dominican devotional and conceptual art that reaches back with some deliberation to the Dugento tradition. Whether or not one accepts the confessional neatness of the scheme, it has the merit of explaining why an artist could paint the earthquake at Santa Croce and the Christ of the Strozzi Chapel without inconsistency: they answer to different orders, different patrons and different purposes.

The prudent conclusion is that Meiss's book should be read as the great statement of a problem rather than its solution. Something does change in Tuscan painting around the middle of the century. Where the agency lies — in the workshops, in the patrons, in the culture at large — remains genuinely undecided, and given the scarcity of first-person documentation it may remain so.

XIII. Afterlife and Modern Recovery

Orcagna's posthumous reputation followed the curve of his misattributions. Through the sixteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth centuries he was, on Vasari's authority, the painter of the Camposanto and the architect of the Loggia — a figure of the first rank, discussed by Bocchi, Del Migliore, Baldinucci, Richa and Lanzi. The nineteenth century began the correction and, characteristically, also the celebration: a formal Elogio d'Andrea Orcagna was published in Florence in 1816, and by the 1820s German scholarship had begun the critical reassessment of the corpus. The decisive work was archival. Documents relating to his activity at Orvieto were published in 1859; Crowe and Cavalcaselle reorganised the corpus in 1864; the great Orvietan documentary volume appeared in 1891; further Florentine documents were published in 1901. By the early twentieth century the documentary spine set out in section III above was substantially in place, and the Camposanto and the Loggia had begun to detach themselves from his name.

The twentieth century added recovery of a different kind. The Santa Croce fragments were retrieved in 1911 and 1942; the thirty-five quatrefoils in the vault of the cappella maggiore of Santa Maria Novella emerged in 1940; fragments of the Santo Spirito refectory were saved during restorations between 1938 and 1944; the Expulsion of the Duke of Athens was detached and conserved in 1964. The 1966 flood then damaged much of what had been recovered at Santa Croce and Santo Spirito. The Orcagna available to a visitor today is therefore, to an unusual degree, a product of twentieth-century salvage, and the physical condition of nearly everything except the Strozzi altarpiece and the tabernacle is poor.

XIV. Conclusion

Andrea di Cione was not the pivotal figure of a spiritual crisis, and he was not the archaising reactionary of the standard account. He was something less dramatic and, on the evidence, more interesting: the most institutionally central artist in Florence in the third quarter of the fourteenth century, a painter who took on the direction of a major sculptural workshop and a cathedral fabric, and who carried into stone a conception of relief formed by painting — signing his one great work of sculpture, pointedly, as pictor.

His formal project, as far as it can be reconstructed from the two signed works, was the pursuit of an equilibrium: between the assertion of the picture plane and the description of space, between the abstraction proper to a theophany and the plastic weight proper to a body, between frame and image, between the flat gold of heaven and the receding floor of the predella. That equilibrium was struck differently in each work, and the results range from the visionary frontality of the Strozzi Christ to the crowded, almost claustrophobic realism of the Dormition relief.

What has obscured this is partly the accident of survival — the loss of the Orvieto mosaics, the Orsanmichele Madonna, the Santissima Annunziata chapels, the greater part of Santa Croce and Santo Spirito — and partly the long shadow of two powerful writers. Vasari gave him works he did not make; Meiss gave him a meaning he may not have carried. Removing both leaves a body of work small enough to be examined closely and coherent enough to reward the examination.

It also leaves a set of questions that are still open, and which any serious study of the artist must confront rather than conceal: when the Santa Croce murals were painted; how much of the St Matthew triptych, if any, is his; whether the Santo Spirito refectory frescoes are his at all; how far he was responsible for the Orvieto rose window; and where, in the long series of Florentine altarpieces of the later Trecento, his hand ends and his brothers' begin. The answers have changed more than once in the last century, and there is no reason to assume they have finished changing.

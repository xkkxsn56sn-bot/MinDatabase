## Introduction: A Name, a Signature and a Problem of Method

Few sculptors of the Sienese Trecento illustrate as sharply as *Gano di Fazio* how far the document, the inscription and the stylistic attribution can drift apart, and how carefully they must be held together if an artistic personality is to be reconstructed at all. Gano belongs to that small company of Sienese sculptors active before Tino di Camaino and Goro di Gregorio whose name is joined to a surviving work by an autograph inscription: his tomb for Tommaso d'Andrea<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-tommaso-d-andrea" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, bishop of Pistoia, in the Collegiata of Santa Maria Assunta at Casole d'Elsa, closes with a proud elegiac couplet naming the sculptor and his city. Yet the archival record of his life, reconstructed in the State Archives of Siena, never once mentions a work of art. It shows us instead a property owner and taxpayer, described only in the generic language of the stone-working trade, who lived in the Vallepiatta district below the cathedral and who was dead by 1318, when his holdings had passed to his daughters. Between these two kinds of certainty — the voice of the signature and the silence of the tax rolls — lies a large and unstable body of attributions that has grown and shrunk over more than a century of scholarship, and some of the most celebrated works once published under Gano's name now stand, with good reason, under other names entirely.

The present essay therefore keeps four layers of evidence distinct throughout. There is, first, what is documented in archival sources, which for Gano amounts to a run of fiscal and property records spanning roughly fifteen years. There is, second, what is established by inscription, which amounts to one monument and its remarkable epigraphic afterlife. There is, third, what has been attributed to him on stylistic grounds, a corpus whose individual members enjoy very different degrees of acceptance and which must be presented with those gradations intact. And there is, finally, what remains frankly hypothetical or actively disputed, including the chronology of his formation, the shape of his late career and the boundary between his hand and those of his contemporaries. Treating all four layers as equivalent, as much of the older literature and most popular accounts still do, produces a Gano who never existed; treating only the first two as admissible produces a sculptor reduced to a single tomb. The historiographically honest Gano lies between these extremes, and the task of this essay is to map him.

Following the editorial convention of this publication, only a few modern scholars are named in what follows, chosen because the modern understanding of Gano is unthinkable without them: three at once, and a fourth, Michael Semff, where the Querceto head is discussed. [Peleo Bacci](/scholars.html#bacci-peleo) reconstructed the documentary biography in 1944, restored to the sculptor his patronymic "di Fazio" and thereby cleared away a tangle of earlier confusions that had merged him with other, unrelated figures. [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni), in a famous article of 1983 in the *Bollettino d'arte*, removed from Gano's corpus its supposed masterpiece, the standing effigy of "messer Porrina" at Casole, and reassigned it, together with its Cremonese companions, to the itinerant master Marco Romano, overturning the image of Gano that had prevailed for half a century. [Gianna Bardotti Biasion](/scholars.html#bardotti-biasion-gianna), in a dense article of 1984 in *Prospettiva* and in the entry devoted to the sculptor in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* of 1995, assembled the modern corpus around the signed tomb, including the controversial attribution of the tomb-altar of Saint Margaret at Cortona. Every other contribution, and there are many of real importance, is cited here anonymously, as the work of earlier antiquarians, of twentieth-century connoisseurship, or of recent criticism.

## Siena and the Sculptural Context Around 1300

Gano's formation belongs to the Sienese cathedral workshop of the last two decades of the thirteenth century, and that workshop was dominated, transformed and ultimately unsettled by one man: Giovanni Pisano, son of Nicola. Giovanni directed the works on the cathedral façade from the mid-1280s and left Siena abruptly in 1296 or 1297, under circumstances that remain partly obscure. In those years he filled the lower zone of the façade with prophets, sibyls and philosophers of unprecedented expressive violence, figures that twist on their axes, crane their necks and lean out of their niches as if arguing with the piazza below. When he departed, the yard was held together by a group of senior masters, among whom Camaino di Crescentino, father of the far more famous Tino di Camaino, enjoyed the highest pay and the greatest trust, and around these masters there gathered a generation of local stone-carvers who are known to us chiefly from fiscal and contractual records rather than from signed works. Bacci's archival reconstruction showed that Gano lived in the Vallepiatta district immediately beside the cathedral, the same quarter inhabited by Camaino di Crescentino and by other sculptors of the workshop, so that whatever we may conclude about his artistic allegiances, his social world was unquestionably the world of the cathedral yard.

It is precisely against this background that the most interesting critical construction concerning Gano has been built. Recent criticism has read one characteristic Sienese response to Giovanni Pisano not as imitation but as a kind of resistance, and has placed Gano, together with Marco Romano, at the centre of what the Italian literature calls an *antigiovanneo* current — an "anti-Giovannesque" alternative. On this reading, a part of the Sienese workshop preferred the compact, legible volumes of Nicola Pisano and of Arnolfo di Cambio, and the measured grace of contemporary Sienese painting, to the dramatic Gothic pathos of the façade figures. Two cautions are necessary. The term is a modern construction that summarises a pattern of stylistic choices; it describes no documented programme, no faction, no recorded quarrel, and it should not be taken to imply a conscious school or rivalry within the yard. And yet, as a description of what the eye actually encounters, it captures something real: the Casole tomb, carved in the first years of the fourteenth century by a man who had grown up in the shadow of Giovanni's façade, seems almost untouched by Giovanni's formal innovations, as if the sculptor had deliberately reached back past him to an older, calmer plastic tradition.

Two further developments of these decades frame Gano's achievement and give it its historical weight. The first is the diffusion of the wall tomb raised on corbels, the *tomba pensile*, which lifts the sarcophagus off the floor, presents the effigy of the deceased on a bier, and frequently stages the moment of revelation itself, with angels or acolytes drawing back a curtain from the body. In its Italian form this type was elaborated in the circle of Arnolfo di Cambio during the last quarter of the thirteenth century, above all in the great cardinalitial and papal tombs of Rome and its orbit, and after 1300 it was propagated and enriched above all by Tino di Camaino, whose monuments carried it from Pisa to Florence and Naples. Gano's tomb at Casole stands near the head of this development: it is among the earliest surviving suspended wall tombs with a life-size effigy, and it is generally considered the earliest complete funerary monument of the Sienese area.

The second development is the growing ambition of narrative relief conceived as a sculpted predella, with a few figures disposed against broad, smooth grounds, legible at a glance and emotionally concentrated. In this field the exchanges between Sienese sculpture and the painting of Duccio and his followers were especially close, and more than one work attributed to Gano has been explained by scholars precisely through comparison with the back panels of Duccio's *Maestà*. A sculptor of Gano's generation, in other words, worked between two gravitational fields — the monumental funerary type coming out of Arnolfo's circle, and the pictorial narrative culture of Duccio's Siena — and the fascination of his small corpus lies in watching those forces meet in marble.

## The Documentary Evidence and the Biography

Everything that can responsibly be said about Gano's life rests on the archival campaign conducted by [Bacci](/scholars.html#bacci-peleo) and published in 1944, a study that remains the foundation of the biography and that later reference works have essentially summarised rather than superseded. In the State Archives of Siena, among the registers of the Gabella dei Contratti for the years 1301–1302, Bacci found the record that gives the sculptor his patronymic, "di Fazio"; the reference usually cited is Gabella Contratti no. 35, at carta 163. The notice concerns not a commission but a purchase of land, and the modern biographical tradition dates it with some precision: the first documentary reference to the sculptor falls on 23 May 1302, when he bought land in Siena, followed on 18 June of the same year by the payment of the tax on that purchase. From this point a series of Lira<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lira-senese" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> and Estimo registers tracks him as a Sienese taxpayer across a decade and a half. The literature cites the Lira register 7 C for 1311–1312, Lira no. 10 for 1312, Lira nos. 385 and 386 for 1313, and the Estimo no. 145 for 1316, in which he appears as the owner of land valued at 283 Sienese lire. Not one of these entries mentions a work of art, a commission, a payment for carving or a workshop. Where a trade is given at all, it is the generic language of the stone-working profession — "Gano de la pietra", or a *magister* in the *ars lapidum* — the ordinary designation of a master stone-worker, which by itself would not distinguish a figural sculptor from a skilled mason.

The end of the life is fixed with equal dryness. The Estimo of 1318, at Estimo no. 98, carta 3, records his real property as having passed to his heirs, and later accounts name those heirs as his daughters Agnese and Ganuccia, citing the formula *heredes Gani magistri lapidum*. From this the literature infers a death falling between September 1316, the last notice of him alive, and the end of 1317 or the very beginning of 1318. The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*, in its entry of 1999, places the death "probably in 1317, or at the beginning of 1318"; the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* prefers the span between September 1316 and the course of 1317. The same biographical tradition adds a further inference: since the daughters were evidently of age to act as heirs in 1318, Gano can hardly have been born later than about 1270. This is a reasonable deduction, and it matters for the chronology of the attributed early works, but it should be recognised for what it is — a deduction resting on an assumption about the daughters' legal majority, not a documented date of birth.

The documentary picture also teaches an important negative lesson, and it is one of the places where the older literature must be actively corrected. Before Bacci, the standard German artists' lexicon had confused the sculptor of the Casole tomb with a certain Galgano di Giovanni, described as an architect and Carthusian monk, and other speculative constructions around a loosely defined "Gano da Siena" circulated in reference works of the 1920s and 1930s. Once the documented chronology of Gano di Fazio was established, these suppositions became untenable, and Bacci dismissed them. Yet the confusion has proved durable in the age of linked data: "Galgano di Giovanni" still appears among Gano's aliases in widely used authority records that aggregate older reference works, and the unwary reader may take the alias for evidence of identity. It is nothing of the kind; it is a historiographical fossil, the trace of an error corrected eighty years ago.

A second correction concerns the activity dates that circulate online. The range most often given, "active between 1290 and 1318", is doubly misleading. The year 1318 is the date of a posthumous fiscal record, not of activity; by then the sculptor was dead. The year 1290 corresponds to no document whatsoever; it is the shadow cast backwards by a stylistic hypothesis, namely that certain works — above all the Ubertini tomb in Arezzo and some carvings for Siena Cathedral — predate the Casole monument. The documented span of the life is 1302 to 1316 or 1317, and any activity before 1302 is inferred, not attested. A similar caution applies to the formula, repeated verbatim across popular sources and even in recent institutional communication, that Gano was "one of the founders of the Sienese school of sculpture" — in the Italian of the Massa Marittima museums in 2024, "considerato uno dei fondatori della scuola senese di scultura". As a critical judgement this is defensible, but it is a judgement and not a historical fact, and it holds only in a precise sense: Gano's signed tomb is the earliest firmly anchored point in a local sculptural tradition that would lead, within a generation, to Goro di Gregorio, Agostino di Giovanni, Agnolo di Ventura and Giovanni d'Agostino. Founding, in that sentence, means standing first in the surviving record; it does not mean teaching, organising or inaugurating anything we can document.

## The Patron: Tommaso d'Andrea, Bishop of Pistoia

The one commission that can be tied to Gano with certainty takes us away from Siena itself, to the hill town of Casole d'Elsa. Casole lay on the old frontier between the territories of Volterra and Siena, a strategic border castle long contested and finally absorbed into the Sienese state after Montaperti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-montaperti" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>. The patron's family ranked among the leading Ghibelline<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ghibellines" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> houses of the town, and its most distinguished member, Tommaso d'Andrea, whose surname also appears as Andrei, made a notable career in the Church as bishop of Pistoia from the mid-1280s until his death in 1303. The literature variously gives 1284, 1285 or 1286 as the start of his episcopate; the Victoria and Albert Museum, which holds the object discussed below, gives his tenure as 1285 to 1303. Even the day of his death is unstable: the monument is reported to give 30 July 1303, episcopal archival sources 29 July, and the 1991 study of the Querceto head 13 July. The discrepancy is small, but it shows how early the written record and the carved one began to diverge, even about so well-documented a man.

Tommaso was an energetic builder. In Pistoia he completed the church of Santa Maria Maddalena by 1290 and rebuilt the church of San Marco. In the years around 1298 to 1301 he also served as apostolic collector of tithes for Tuscany, an office that placed him within the fiscal machinery of the papal curia. Popular accounts sometimes credit him with commissioning Giovanni Pisano's pulpit in Sant'Andrea at Pistoia, carved between 1298 and 1301. The sources support something more modest. In 1298 the bishop consecrated the new high altar of Sant'Andrea, which shows a direct interest in the renewal of the church that the pulpit would crown and makes some involvement in the wider campaign plausible. Whether he commissioned the pulpit itself the accessible evidence does not establish, and the claim should be treated as plausible participation, not documented patronage.

His building activity was not confined to Pistoia, and the best witness to it in his native territory survives, by an accident of the antiquarian market, in London. The Victoria and Albert Museum holds a small inscribed slab of marble, accession number 5802-1859, recording the foundation in 1296 of an oratory dedicated to the Virgin and to Saint Thomas the Apostle, the bishop's own name-saint. The slab measures 38 by 21.6 cm and is 6 cm deep; it is a plain, portable stone weighing about eleven kilograms. Its incised text runs in five heavily abbreviated lines of Gothic majuscule, opening with a small cross. In expanded form it reads

> Venerabilis pater dominus Thomas Dei gratia Pistoriensis episcopus fecit fieri hoc oratorium ad honorem Dei et gloriose Virginis Marie et beati Thome apostoli, followed by the year MCCLXXXXVI,

written in the old additive manner with four Xs for ninety.

In English: "The venerable father, lord Thomas, by the grace of God bishop of Pistoia, had this oratory made in honour of God and of the glorious Virgin Mary and of blessed Thomas the Apostle, 1296." In the lower corners, flanking the date, two small shields carry identical arms, a cinquefoil between three roundels, presumably those of the founder's family. The museum's catalogue of Italian sculpture records the tablet in 1766 as still preserved in an oratory of the Virgin and Saint Thomas at Casole. The building no longer existed by 1833, and the stone reached London in 1859.

The carver wrote the text in the heavily abbreviated style usual in epigraphy of the period. Superscript strokes and contractions turn venerabilis into VENER, gratia into GRA, Pistoriensis into PIST, episcopus into EPS, gloriose into GLOSE, beati into BTI and apostoli into APLI. The script is Gothic majuscule, freely mixing capital and uncial forms. The M is rounded and uncial in type, the E is closed, and the H in THOMAS and HOREM takes a lowercase-style form with a looped bowl. The letters are broad, swelling and generously spaced, and the line breaks fall mid-word without hesitation: ORA/TORIUM, GLOSE/VIRGINIS.

The two shields are identical and almost symmetrical, each with its cinquefoil set between three roundels, two above and one below, within a plain pointed outline typical of the late thirteenth century. Neither the museum record nor any source consulted here names the family whose arms they are, and the blazon has not been compared with Sienese heraldic repertories, so its assignment to the Andrea remains to be verified.

The tablet matters to the history of Gano’s monument for two further reasons. It shows what kind of building the bishop founded at home: an oratory, a place of prayer approved by the church for the celebration of Mass but distinct from a parish church, dedicated to his own name-saint and raised only seven years before his family commissioned his tomb. It must also be kept distinct from the church of San Tommaso at Querceto, which the bishop's brothers founded in his memory in 1304 and which is discussed below; whether that later church rose on the site of the earlier oratory is not known.

Read beside the tomb, the tablet is also an epigraphic lesson. In 1296 the bishop speaks in the third person through the impersonal formula of patronage, fecit fieri, "had made", and no craftsman is named. On the tomb that his brothers raised seven years later, the maker steps forward and signs in his own voice, in Latin verse, claiming praise for his hand. Between the two inscriptions, within the commissions of a single family, the carver moves from anonymity into authorship, and that move is what turns Gano di Fazio from an attribution into a historical person.

The tomb of Tommaso d'Andrea, on the right wall of the Collegiata of Santa Maria Assunta at Casole, is the keystone of everything we know and everything we conjecture about Gano, and it deserves slow description. It carries two inscriptions, which together make it one of the best-documented Sienese monuments of its generation. The first is the sculptor's signature. It runs in a single line along the moulding at the top of the sarcophagus front, directly beneath the effigy, where anyone looking at the bishop must also read the name of the man who carved him. The stone reads

> +Celavit Ganus opus hoc insigne Senensis: / laudibus immensis est sua digna manus

"Ganus of Siena carved this distinguished work; his hand is worthy of immense praises." The printed tradition is less stable than the stone. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale prints Senensis, which is what the inscription shows. The Dizionario Biografico prints Senesis, and an early nineteenth-century Sienese antiquarian transcription reads Senenis and gives et for est. These variants are slips of transcription, not alternative readings, and the moral is the usual one: the text should be quoted from the stone, not from any single printed authority.

The second inscription is the dedicatory epitaph, cut on a broad tablet at the foot of the monument, below the heraldic register described further on. It is laid out in two columns of Gothic majuscule, the letters filled with a dark tint that makes them legible from the nave. Its full text does not circulate in the accessible literature, and the reading given here is taken from close photographs of the stone; it is secure except where the surface is worn.

The epitaph begins with eight leonine hexameters, four to each column, every line rhyming at the caesura and at the end, in the manner of most Italian funerary verse of the time. The left column opens with a cross and addresses the dead man's office:

> Pistorii flamen, cleri populique solamen, / culmen honestatis, Thomas, heros pietatis, / fons decretorum, flos cleri gemmaque morum, / annis exemptis tribus cum mille trecentis.

"Priest of Pistoia, solace of clergy and people, summit of integrity, Thomas, hero of piety, fount of the decretals, flower of the clergy and jewel of conduct, when three years had passed beyond the thousand and three hundred." The phrase *fons decretorum* praises the bishop as a canon lawyer, a learned prelate versed in the papal decretals, and the last verse dates the death to 1303 by circumlocution.

The right column turns from office to lineage and prayer:

> Gloria maiorum, patrie decus atque suorum, / formosus, mitis, virtutum florida vitis, / hic iacet; alme, velis, pater, hunc tibi iungere celis; / Christo dato requievit fide beato.

"Glory of his ancestors, honour of his homeland and of his kin, handsome and gentle, a flowering vine of virtues, here he lies; kind Father, may it please you to join him to yourself in the heavens. Having given himself to Christ, he rested in blessed faith."

The final verse is the least certain, its third word worn. Beneath the verses each column closes in prose. The left column reads Thomas olim Pistoriensis episcopus hic iacet, "here lies Thomas, formerly bishop of Pistoia", preceded and followed by words too worn to read with confidence. The right column records the commission:

> ob cuius memoriam domini Iacobus et Sozus … germani ipsius fecerunt fieri hoc,

"in whose memory the lords Iacopo and Sozzo, his brothers, had this made." The stone thus confirms directly what the published summaries reported: the year 1303 and the brothers Iacopo and Sozzo as the patrons. The formula *fecerunt fieri* repeats in the plural the *fecit fieri* with which the bishop had recorded his oratory of 1296, so the family's language of patronage passes unchanged from the founder to his heirs.

The memento-mori verse beginning

> In me cognosce qui transis quam breve posse / est hominis,

which the antiquarian tradition transcribes in connection with the tomb, is not on this tablet. It either stood elsewhere on the monument or has been lost, and its source in the older literature has not been traced.

The signature, moreover, had an afterlife remarkable enough to become evidence in its own right. When Marco Romano signed the tomb of Saint Simeon the Prophet in the Venetian church of San Simeone Grande, in an inscription dated 4 February 1318 (1317 in the Venetian style), he wrote *Celavit Marcus opus hoc insigne Romanus. / Laudibus non parcus: est sua digna manus.* The dependence on Gano's couplet is unmistakable: the same opening verb, the same metrical frame, the same closing boast about the worthy hand, with only the name and the epithet exchanged. Earlier scholarship had noticed the parallel. Previtali made it the starting point of his argument that Marco Romano had passed through Casole and studied Gano's tomb at first hand, an inference that underpins the modern reassignment of the Porrina monument discussed below. For the moment it is enough to register the strangeness of the fact: a provincial episcopal tomb in a Valdelsa hill town supplied the model for one of the proudest sculptor's signatures in early Trecento Venice.

Read from the bottom up, the monument is built in clearly separated zones. Two corbels carved with masks carry the whole structure. Above the epitaph tablet runs a frieze of square metopes divided by small carved uprights; the metopes alternate between shields of arms and rosettes. The charges on the shields are worn and barely legible in photographs. If they repeat the cinquefoil between three roundels carved on the bishop's foundation tablet of 1296, they would confirm that tablet's arms as the Andrea family's; the comparison should be made on the stone. The sarcophagus chest above is divided into five rectangular panels, each with a central rosette between spreading acanthus leaves, and its upper moulding bears the signature. On the lid lies the bishop in pontifical vestments, his mitred head resting on cushions and his hands crossed, his face composed in sleep. Behind him three standing figures hold up a long cloth that falls in swags between their hands, while two smaller figures kneel in vigil at his head and feet. These attendants are often called angels, and the heritage catalogue speaks of angels in describing the monument, but none of the five has wings: they read as acolytes or young clerics performing a funeral office, and the essay calls them attendants.

The upper zone is architectural and pictorial at once. At the ends of the sarcophagus, two slender colonnettes with leafy capitals rise to carry a gable lined with crockets. Within the gable an arch opens onto the lunette behind the effigy, and the painting there has not been lost. A painted curtain, green with an orange fringe and a central tassel, is drawn apart and tied back at either side, a painted counterpart to the carved cloth held up by the attendants below. The intrados of the arch keeps painted ornament in red, green and dark tones. The condition and restoration history of this paintwork have not been established, and its originality is not documented. In the tympanum above the arch, a roundel framed in green and gold holds the Lamb of God, carved in white against a dark ground with its cross-banner; the delicacy of this field has been compared to Duccio. Two small heads in medallions flank it, presumably the angels named in the heritage catalogue's description of the Agnus Dei among angels. At the apex of the gable a small roundel frames the half-figure of the blessing Christ, the risen Redeemer of the catalogue entry. The monument is thus a layered theological statement as much as a commemoration: the dead bishop below, the sacrificial Lamb above him, the triumphant Christ at the summit. Two curtains, one carved and one painted, stage for every visitor the moment in which death is shown and passed beyond.

The type matters as much as the details, because it fixes Gano's place in the history of the Italian tomb. Recent criticism reads the Casole monument as a deliberate reduction, in rank as in scale, of the grandest Sienese funerary project of the preceding generation: the monument to Pope Gregory X in Arezzo Cathedral, dated variously from the 1280s to about 1310 and given by one study to Gano himself, whose architectural cage and part of whose decorative repertory the Casole tomb shares. The reduction is intelligent rather than impoverished, suited both to the episcopal rather than papal rank of the dead man and to the modest size of a collegiate church. The other close relative is the tomb of Bishop Ranieri degli Ubertini in San Domenico at Arezzo, which several scholars give to Gano himself. The motif of attendants revealing the body descends from Arnolfian prototypes. What is new at Casole is its combination with the compact suspended format, carried out by a Sienese sculptor for a provincial church, with a completeness that none of the earlier Sienese funerary fragments can match.

The dating rests on a secure terminus post quem and an uncertain date of completion. The bishop died in July 1303 and his brothers raised the tomb, as the epitaph states, but no document records when it was carved or finished. Proposed end dates differ accordingly: about 1303 to 1304 in some general reference works, 1303 to 1305 in the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale, and 1303 to 1306 for the whole group of Gano's Casole works in the Dizionario Biografico, while some recent popular captions stretch the bracket to about 1315. The earlier datings rest on the plausible assumption that commemorative tombs of this kind were normally completed within a few years of the death, and on the stylistic relation to the commemorative head from Querceto, discussed below, which is tied to the years immediately after 1303.

Finally, the style, since it is the touchstone for every attribution to Gano that follows. A few clear, incisive lines define the bodies, producing solid volumes and a strong continuity of contour; the surfaces are smooth, and the drapery is reduced to shallow incisions that clothe the forms without agitating them. The bishop's face is serene, and the attendants holding the cloth move with a light, measured rhythm that tempers the gravity of the subject, the central figure standing almost frontal while those at the ends lean in toward their task. Critics have consistently recognised Arnolfian memories in this language, in the fluid linear simplification and geometric compactness of the bodies, joined to a sweetness and musicality that seem to come from contemporary Sienese painting rather than from any sculptural source. Bardotti Biasion's analysis of 1984 remains the most influential account. She stressed that in the first years of the Trecento this sculpture still seemed to ignore Giovanni Pisano's new formal language, preferring to reconnect, in a gentler and more familiar key, with Arnolfo and Nicola Pisano. The judgement has been widely accepted, and it is one of the foundations of the "anti-Giovannesque" reading of Gano sketched above.

## The Querceto Head and the Andrea Commemorations

Closely tied to the tomb, and widely accepted as the second fixed point of the corpus, is a marble head of Tommaso d'Andrea from the church of San Tommaso at Querceto, near Casole, the bishop's birthplace. The piece has a respectable antiquarian pedigree. In 1808 a Sienese antiquarian recorded it walled in above the portal of that church, close to the Bargagli villa, with an inscribed slab beneath it, and dated the head to about 1304. Lost from view for a long time, the head surfaced again in the late twentieth century and, after some passing from hand to hand, entered the Museo d'Arte Sacra at Colle di Val d'Elsa. [Michael Semff](/scholars.html#semff-michael), in a short article of 1991 in the *Kunstchronik*, published it as a portrait by Gano of about 1303, and the attribution has met with wide assent. The identification of the sitter rests on more than provenance: the shape of the head, the short cut of the mouth and the strikingly fleshy double chin are too close to the effigy at Casole to belong to anyone else.

What sets the head apart from the effigy is its life. The marble, about 30 cm high and with the tip of the nose restored, shows the bishop with wide-open eyes and engaged pupils, lively arched brows, a furrowed forehead, firmly modelled ears and, under the mitre, the fine ends of the cloth of the infulae rendered with near-veristic precision. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* treats it as a significant example of portraiture from life, and the *Dizionario Biografico* finds in it stronger accents of portrait realism than in the idealised tomb figures. The comparison matters: it suggests that the softening and generalising of the effigy at Casole was a choice suited to the image of a dead man laid in sleep, not a limit of the sculptor's ability. The head is also unfinished in a revealing way. The face is worked to the smallest detail, but the mitre and the back of the head are only roughed out; the band across the forehead is drilled with filigree ornament only in part, and the vertical band of the mitre is merely incised. The frontal gaze, and borders worked only on the front of the mitre, suggest that the head was meant to be seen from the front, within a wall setting rather than as a figure in the round. Semff raised, and left open, the possibility that a standing monument like the Porrina opposite the Casole tomb had first been planned for the bishop and abandoned when his sudden death called for a tomb of the conventional type.

The inscribed slab gives the head its context, and its text, now published in full, corrects what earlier summaries reported. It records that the lord Iacopo and Sozzo, brothers and sons of the late lord Andrea of Casole, had this church built, together with its adjoining buildings, in honour of Saint Thomas the Apostle and for the soul of their brother Tommaso, bishop of Pistoia, of pious memory. It adds that the first stone was laid in its foundations in the year 1304, on the feast of Saint Matthew, apostle and evangelist, that is, on 21 September. The text yields three facts. The surname d'Andrea is a patronymic, the bishop being the son of an Andrea of Casole. The two brothers who signed the epitaph at Casole as patrons of the tomb also founded a memorial church at Querceto, so the family's commemoration of the prelate took the form of a coordinated campaign: a tomb in the Collegiata, a church in the native village, and in both cases the language of patronage, *fecerunt fieri* on the tomb and *fecerunt hedificari* on the slab. Finally, the Querceto church is a foundation of 1304, distinct from the oratory of the Virgin and Saint Thomas whose foundation in 1296 is recorded on the tablet now in London. Whether the new church rose on the site of that earlier oratory, absorbing it, cannot be said from the inscriptions alone.

Two uncertainties remain. The first concerns the original relation between the head and the slab. Semff asked whether so prominent a foundation inscription can have been set up together with the portrait, in the manner of an epitaph, on the outer wall of a small, rather private church as early as 1304. He suggested instead that the portrait, perhaps made for another context, was joined to the slab later and set into the wall of the bishop’s native church in his memory. Nothing in the head itself argues against an original wall setting, but nothing proves it either. The second uncertainty is the head's present location, on which the reference works disagree: the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* of 1995 places it in the Museo d'Arte Sacra at Colle di Val d'Elsa, where it was published in 1991, while the *Dizionario Biografico* of 1999 places it in the Museo Archeologico e della Collegiata at Casole. Since the Colle museum was merged in 2017 into the new Museo San Pietro, the head is today either there or at Casole, and its present location has not been established.

## The Porrina Monument and the Marco Romano Question

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-03.jpg" alt="Tall Gothic marble wall aedicule on volute brackets, with a crocketed gable, a pierced tracery wheel and two pinnacled piers. In a plain round-headed niche stands a life-size man in a tall cap, long belted gown and heavy mantle, holding a book to his chest and gripping a sword at his hip. Outside the piers, on separate consoles, stand a bearded elder holding a scroll and a beardless youth in a gentle sway.">
  <figcaption>Marco Romano (formerly attributed to Gano di Fazio), Funerary monument of messer Porrina (Bernardino degli Albertini?), marble, c. 1309–1312. Casole d’Elsa, Collegiata di Santa Maria Assunta.</figcaption>
</figure>

No sculpture has weighed more heavily on Gano di Fazio's reputation than this one, and none belongs to him less. Facing the signed tomb of Bishop Tommaso d'Andrea across the nave of the Collegiata of Santa Maria Assunta, the standing marble figure universally called "messer Porrina" is the most famous sculpture in Casole and the pivot of Gano's critical fortune. For half a century it was hailed as his masterpiece. Since Previtali's article of 1983 it has been recognised as the work of Marco Romano. Both the identity of the sitter and the identity of the carver have had to be rebuilt from the ground up, and the monument belongs on a page about Gano not as his work but as his counterpart: the answer of a younger and more Gothic sculptor to the tomb opposite.

The architecture is a tall wall aedicule raised on two brackets carved with volutes. Two slender piers rise to crocketed pinnacles, and between them a steep gable climbs to a finial, its rakes edged with crockets that curl into spirals. A small lobed oculus is pierced in the gable field. Below it, a cusped trefoil arch frames the niche, and in the spandrel above the arch opens a wheel of radiating tracery, cut right through the stone so that the wall shows behind it. A small shield hangs from the central cusp. Its blazon, a horizontal band with a small chequered pattern, and its relation to the Albertini or Aringhieri family have not been established. The shield also keeps clear traces of colour. The monument has lost most of its original polychromy, but not all of it: the shield and the eyes of the main figure, where traces of azurite survive, show that the whole aedicule was once painted.

Within the plain, round-headed recess, on a simple marble base, stands a life-size man in contemporary civilian dress. He wears a tall cap, a long gown gathered at the waist by a belt, and a heavy mantle falling from the shoulders in broad, deep folds. With his right hand he holds a closed book against his chest; with his left he grips a sword at his hip, its hilt clearly visible and its scabbard partly hidden in the drapery. Between the hem of the gown and the pointed shoes a band of scale-like texture is visible. It may represent mail hose, which would strengthen the knightly reading of the sword, but the point remains to be verified on the stone. The face is broad and heavy, with full cheeks and a fleshy chin, unidealised and unmistakably a particular man. This physiognomic realism makes the statue a key work in the history of early Trecento portraiture. Within Tuscan sculpture of its moment the monument is described as unique in presenting a full-length standing figure beneath a Gothic canopy.

The flanking figures stand on their own consoles outside the piers. On the left is a bearded elder with deep-curled hair and a long beard, one hand raised to his chest and the other holding a scroll. On the right is a beardless youth with soft curls, his head inclined and his body set in a gentle Gothic sway, one hand at his waist. The literature reads them as prophets. The young figure's swaying elegance shows at a glance how far this sculptor stands from the upright, compact attendants of Gano's tomb.

What the visitor sees today is not the monument's original state. Besides most of its colour, it has lost a series of decorative and epigraphic elements, and it no longer stands where it was built. It originally stood beside the entrance to the family's funerary chapel of Saint Nicholas, frescoed about a decade after the Andrea tomb and intended for the remains of the brothers Bernardino and Ranieri. The plain front of the base now carries no text. Whether the epitaph discussed below was ever carved there, or stood elsewhere among the lost elements, cannot be decided from the present arrangement.

The sitter first. The older literature identified the figure as a Ranieri del Porrina, sometimes coloured as a Ghibelline rebel; the 1931 *Enciclopedia Italiana* entry on Casole still lists "the monument of Ranieri del Porrina" among Gano's works. The Italian national heritage catalogue entry for the statue, compiled in 1983 and updated in 2006, states explicitly that this identification is mistaken. Following a study of 1981, it names the sitter as messer Porrina, a doctor of law and consistorial advocate at the papal court, already dead by 1313. Research connected with the 2010 Casole exhibition carried the identification further. An epitaph for the Porrina survives only in Giovanni Boccaccio's<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giovanni-boccaccio" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> autograph Zibaldone, in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana (Plut. XXIX 8), and it certainly belonged to the monument's explanatory apparatus. It allows the figure to be identified as Bernardino degli Albertini, called "il Porrina". He was a jurist who had practised at the papal curia since 1279, obtained Sienese citizenship in 1292–93, and died between 1308 and April 1309. On this reading the book and the sword become paired emblems of the learned jurist and the knight. The commission would then fall after his death, promoted by his brother Ranieri, bishop of Cremona from 1296 until his death at Casole in December 1312, together with Bernardino's son, also named Ranieri.

One terminological warning is indispensable. The family name is unstable across the literature, appearing as Aringhieri, Albertini and "del Porrina", and some popular sources call the sitter Beltramo rather than Bernardino. These forms are not necessarily contradictions, since the branches and nicknames of Sienese families overlap notoriously, but a publication must choose one form and explain it. The two death dates in circulation, 1309 and 1313, reflect the difference between the date suggested by recent research and the *terminus ante quem* of 1313. In that year, after the death of the Emperor Henry VII, the family was exiled, and Bernardino is recorded as already dead.

The attribution history is a textbook case of a strong stylistic idea holding the field for decades and then giving way to a better one, and the heritage catalogue entry summarises its stages. A general history of Italian art published in 1906 first gave the Porrina to Gano, the signer of the tomb opposite, an attribution of proximity as much as of style. A study of 1939 rejected it and proposed a northern sculptor trained in Giovanni Pisano's school. In 1942 an article in *Emporium* forcefully restated the attribution to Gano, hailing the Porrina as his masterpiece, and two years later Bacci rejected it decisively in the same study that established Gano's documentary biography. In the late 1940s and early 1950s an American scholar of Trecento sculpture linked the Porrina to three statues on the porch of Cremona Cathedral, a Virgin and Child with Saints Imerio and Omobono, previously given to Giovanni di Balduccio. He later also linked it to the circle of the Venetian tomb of Saint Simeon. Ranieri degli Albertini's Cremonese episcopate supplied the historical bridge between Casole and the Lombard city. Yet as late as 1984 an important contribution on Gano's work at Massa Marittima could still accept the Porrina as his, so tenacious was the old construction.

Previtali's article, "Alcune opere 'fuori contesto': il caso di Marco Romano", turned these scattered observations into a coherent reconstruction resting on three supports. The first is epigraphic: the Venetian signature of 1318, discussed above, shows that Marco knew this church and its monument. The second is comparative. Previtali compared the Cremona Saint Omobono with the Porrina point by point and found the same compact volumes, smooth surfaces, broad undulating drapery, similar gestures, half-open mouths and portrait-like faces. He also gave Marco, as early works, four heads and two lions on the inner side of the portals of Siena Cathedral, datable around 1290, which roots Marco, like Gano, in the Sienese cathedral yard. The third is a personal manner. Previtali identified it in abundant, finely drawn drapery deeply undercut against polished surfaces, soft modelling of the flesh, hair in full curls ringed around drill holes, and the Gothic sway of the young prophet. Previtali also read the Porrina's face as a deliberate reworking of the Andrea head and placed the statue shortly after the bishop's tomb. That dating sits uneasily with the later identification of the sitter. If he is Bernardino, who died in 1308–09, a funerary commission is unlikely to predate his death, and the bracket of about 1309 to 1312 used in the caption below follows from that identification rather than from Previtali.

The reassignment is now consensus. The *Dizionario Biografico* entry on Gano accepts it, stressing how far the Porrina and the Cremona figures stand from Gano's manner in their surer and more modern Gothic postures, their vividly portrait-like features, their mobile and virtuosically detached drapery, and their drill-ringed curls. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* states that criticism is by now agreed in excluding Gano. The exhibition *Marco Romano e il contesto artistico senese fra la fine del Duecento e gli inizi del Trecento*, held at the Museo Civico Archeologico e della Collegiata in Casole from 27 March to 3 October 2010, confirmed and widened Marco's oeuvre. It added the wooden Crucifix from Radi di Montagna, now at Colle di Val d'Elsa, the ivory Crucifix in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Annunciation figures from San Marco in Venice and a marble prophet's head at Casole. Some of these additions remain debated, and part of the criticism gives the Venetian Annunciation to a later follower. The heritage catalogue records the Porrina as attributed to Marco Romano, and the local museum system presents it under his name.

The consequences for Gano are considerable, and they explain why so much popular literature about him is out of date. Widely used online biographies descending from older dictionaries still credit him with the Porrina, and sometimes with the Cremona statues as well. The familiar claim that the Porrina is among the earliest standing funerary effigies in Italian sculpture may well be true, but it is a claim about Marco Romano's monument, and its transfer to Gano is the most widespread error in his popular fortune. Paradoxically, the reassignment has made the relation between the two monuments more interesting, not less. Gano lays his bishop down in sleep, beneath a revealing cloth and a sequence of sacred images. Marco stands his jurist upright in the dress and with the weapons of his life, as if still present among the living, flanked by prophets rather than mourners. Part of the difference lies in the two subjects, a prelate and a layman; the rest belongs to the two sculptors. If Marco copied Gano's signature and reworked the face of the Andrea head, he was in a precise sense answering Gano. Conversely, the *Dizionario Biografico* suggests that the sculptor of the Cortona Annunciation, if that sculptor is Gano, learned something in turn from Marco. The two men appear joined in an exchange that may have begun in the Sienese cathedral workshop in the 1290s and continued, across the nave at Casole, into the new century. The older criticism's confusion of their works was therefore not random. It registered a real artistic proximity, which modern scholarship has learned to describe as dialogue rather than identity.

## Before Casole: The Ubertini Tomb in Arezzo and the Cathedral of Siena

### The Tomb of Ranieri degli Ubertini in San Domenico, Arezzo

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-04.jpg" alt="Gothic marble wall tomb under a plain steep gable with a small carved shield at the apex. A pointed arch on two colonnettes with leaf capitals frames a fragmentary fresco of the Madonna and Child enthroned between two angels, its lower half lost. Below, the front of the sarcophagus shows in relief a bishop lying on a bier with mitre and vestments, his hands crossed. A Dominican friar reading a book stands at his head and another at his feet. Heraldic shields mark the ends of the chest, which rests on two corbels.">
  <figcaption>Gano di Fazio (attributed), Funerary monument of Ranieri degli Ubertini, bishop of Volterra, marble with frescoed lunette, c. 1295–1300. 300 x 200 cm. Arezzo, San Domenico.</figcaption>
</figure>

If the Casole tomb of 1303–1305 is the work of a mature master, and its assurance leaves little doubt of that, then Gano di Fazio must have had an earlier career. The search for it has concentrated on two places, the fabric of Siena Cathedral and Arezzo, and of the two Arezzo offers the stronger candidate. It is this wall tomb of Ranieri degli Ubertini, bishop of Volterra from 1273, in the Dominican church of San Domenico. The architectural and figural closeness of the Ubertini and Casole tombs led in 1985 to the proposal that both are by the same hand. Recent surveys of Sienese Gothic sculpture have taken the proposal up, and the Dizionario Biografico registers it with its usual caution, recording that the tomb has been “brought close” to Gano rather than simply assigned to him. The caption keeps that caution. After restoration the monument has been returned to its original position on the left wall of the presbytery.

The architecture is severe and almost entirely linear. A steep gable, with no crockets or figure sculpture, is defined only by the parallel mouldings that follow its rakes. At the apex a small carved shield bears a heraldic charge, presumably the arms of the Ubertini; the blazon has not been verified. Beneath the gable a pointed arch with a cusp on its inner edge opens onto a deep lunette. The arch rests on two slender colonnettes with foliate capitals, which stand on the ends of the sarcophagus and carry square abaci. The whole is a reduced, sober version of the suspended tomb with a canopy, without the crocketed gable, the sacred images in roundels and the carved cloth that would enrich the type at Casole a few years later.

The lunette holds a fresco of the Madonna and Child enthroned between two angels. The Virgin, in a blue mantle over a red dress, sits on a throne whose back rises into small Gothic pinnacles, and holds the Child in red robes on her knee. The haloed angels at either side are only partly preserved, and against the dark blue ground the colour has largely flaked away. The lower half of the painting is lost; near the bottom right only a small head survives, perhaps a kneeling donor or friar, but too fragmentary to identify. The fresco completes the programme of the monument. The bishop lies below in death, and above him the Virgin and Child receive his memory, as at Casole, where the painted curtain and the Lamb of God fill the same zone. The authorship and date of the fresco are not discussed in the sources consulted here and have not been established.

The design differs from Casole in one telling respect. The effigy is not laid on the lid of the chest but carved in relief on its front, the bishop stretched on a bier with his head on a cushion at the left. He wears his mitre and episcopal vestments, the chasuble marked by a cross of geometric ornament, and his hands are crossed. The relief is enriched by inlays of dark material in the cushion, the vestments and the shoes, which give the white marble a polychrome accent; their material and condition remain to be verified against the restoration records. Two standing Dominican friars, hooded and in long habits, frame the bier. The friar at the head holds an open book as if reading the office for the dead; the friar at the feet stands in quiet vigil. Their clear, compact masses, with drapery reduced to long, simple folds, already speak the language of the Casole figures, and it is above all this kinship that suggested the attribution. At either end of the chest, outside the frame of the relief, a shield repeats the heraldry of the gable.

The friars also give the monument its context. Ranieri belonged to the Ubertini, one of the great Ghibelline families of the Aretine territory, and although he was bishop of Volterra, he was buried in the Dominican church of Arezzo, among his own people. The two friars are therefore more than conventional mourners. They place the dead bishop under the prayer of the Dominican community that kept his tomb, and they give the funeral liturgy a permanent form in stone, carved beside the body itself.

The dating depends on the bishop’s death, and here the literature is in open conflict. Older scholarship placed the death in 1301; the Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale gives a span between 1290 and 1296. A more recent survey, in its plate captions, prefers a death between 1297 and 1300, and a footnote in the 2003 Duccio exhibition catalogue is cited for new documents disproving the traditional 1301. Proposed datings of the monument range accordingly from about 1295 to about 1300, so the tomb in any case predates Casole.

If the attribution is accepted, as it widely though not universally is, the Ubertini tomb becomes Gano’s earliest surviving work and one of the first Tuscan funerary monuments with an effigy. The hypothesis fits the city’s sculptural context closely: a young Sienese sculptor working on the Ubertini commission would have had before his eyes, if that monument is indeed of the last decade of the century, the tomb of Gregory X in Arezzo Cathedral, the probable model of the Casole tomb. Worked in relief and on a smaller scale, the Ubertini tomb would be the sober first statement of a formula that Casole brings to full eloquence.

The second place in which the young Gano has been sought is, inevitably, the fabric of Siena Cathedral itself. His documented residence in Vallepiatta, hard against the cathedral, and the absence of any other identifiable early work have made it natural to look for his hand among the many carvings produced by the workshop in the 1280s and 1290s. A survey of Sienese sculptors published in 1980 proposed his name for some of the blessing busts set in the large windows on the flanks of the cathedral — figures that the *Dizionario Biografico* describes as showing a uniform softness, with a consequent waxy weakening of plastic and expressive individuality, a generalised reworking of the Nicola and Arnolfo tradition typical of much of that generation's routine production. The busts plausibly come from the workshop in which Gano is presumed to have been formed, and his participation is entirely possible; but nothing in them displays the specific signs of the Casole hand clearly enough to sustain more than a tentative attribution, and the honest formula is that Gano may be among their authors rather than that any one of them is his. The same discipline must govern the common statement that Gano trained under Giovanni Pisano. It rests on circumstantial evidence — his residence beside the yard and the presumed chronology of the cathedral works during his youth — and on no record of apprenticeship whatsoever. He grew up, almost certainly, in Giovanni's Siena; whether he grew up in Giovanni's workshop, in any formal sense, the documents do not say.

## Narrative Relief: The Miracles of the Blessed Gioacchino Piccolomini

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-05.jpg" alt="Three marble relief panels side by side in moulded frames. Left: a small haloed youth climbs steps to knock at the arched door of a tall crenellated gateway. Centre: a haloed friar collapses at the left, held by the arm by a hooded companion, while a table laid with bowls, cups and knives tilts steeply before three seated friars, one praying; nothing falls from it. Right: a priest raises his arm at an altar with an open book, a tall candle stands unsupported in the air beside him, and a haloed friar lies collapsed on the ground.">
  <figcaption>Gano di Fazio (attributed), Scenes from the Life of the Blessed Gioacchino Piccolomini: his admission to the Order; the miracle of the table; the miracle of the candle. Marble, c. 1310–1312. Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale (from his tomb in Santa Maria dei Servi).</figcaption>
</figure>

Among the extensions of Gano di Fazio’s corpus beyond Casole, the most widely accepted concerns these three narrative reliefs in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Siena. They come from the dismantled tomb of the Blessed Gioacchino Piccolomini, the Servite<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-order-servants-of-mary" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> friar who died in 1305, in the Sienese church of Santa Maria dei Servi. The attribution to Gano was first proposed as early as 1906 and developed with full argument by Bardotti Biasion in 1984. Even a contribution of 1990, sceptical about much of the expanded corpus, singled out these scenes as an enchanting example of “predella style” in sculpture. Bardotti Biasion also reconstructed the original ensemble as a double complex: a suspended wall tomb with the narrative reliefs on the sarcophagus front, and beneath it an altar at which mass was celebrated in honour of the beato, whose cult in early Trecento Siena was intensely alive. If that reconstruction is correct, Gano had a second major funerary commission in Siena itself, of a more experimental kind than Casole: a tomb that doubles as a liturgical station, its sculpture serving the devotion of a living cult.

The three scenes are set side by side, each within its own moulded frame, and the joints between them run along the frames. Whether the reliefs were carved on three separate blocks or on a single slab later broken has not been established on the object, since the earlier literature speaks of one slab. Read in sequence, they tell a story of vocation and of illness. Gioacchino’s hagiography records that he suffered from epilepsy, and two of the three miracles are miracles of the falling sickness: at the moment of collapse, divine protection keeps the fit from doing harm. The cycle thus turns a bodily affliction into a sign of grace, a choice that suited both the devotion of the Servites and the pilgrims who would have sought the friar’s help at his tomb.

- The Young Gioacchino Seeks Admission to the Order

The first scene shows the young Gioacchino seeking admission to the Order. A crenellated gateway, seen at an angle so that its two faces recede like an open box, rises over almost the whole field, its parapet carried on a little arcade of corbels and its walls pierced by narrow arched windows. Within the round-headed doorway a short flight of steps climbs to the right. Up these steps, small and haloed, the youth stretches up to knock at the convent door. The disproportion between the boy and the fortress-like building is deliberate. It makes the gateway the image of the religious life he is asking to enter, and the steps the measure of his eagerness. The box-like rendering of the architecture, with its oblique recession and flat, smooth walls, has been compared with the buildings in Duccio’s Maestà.

- The Miracle of the Table

The central scene, the largest and most crowded, is the miracle of the table. The friars sit at their meal behind a long board laid with bowls, cups, loaves and knives. Seized by a fit, Gioacchino, haloed, at the left, collapses backward with bent knees, and in his fall he drags the table with him, so that the board tips steeply on the diagonal. Yet nothing on it moves: dishes, cups and knives cling to the slanting surface as if fixed there, and the miracle lies precisely in this immobility. A hooded companion seizes the falling friar by the arm. Behind the board, three seated brethren react in a graded sequence: one leans toward the scene, one turns with a questioning glance, and the last joins his hands in prayer. The diagonal of the board crossing the frame is the boldest compositional stroke in the group. It carries the eye from the collapsing body at lower left to the praying hands at upper right, from the accident to its recognition as grace.

- The Miracle of the Candle

The third scene repeats the illness in a liturgical setting. Before a plain altar, on which lies an open book, a priest in a chasuble raises one arm high, evidently at the elevation of the Host. At the right Gioacchino, who was serving the Mass, has fallen to the ground, curled forward with his head and halo close to the floor. Beside the priest, the candle he had been holding stands upright in mid-air, a tall, slender shaft with no support. The sculptor stages the miracle with great economy. The candle is a single vertical line against an empty ground, and its unnatural stillness answers the collapse of the body below it, so that the most sacred moment of the rite and the humblest accident of the server’s illness meet in one image.

The carving joins a soft, pictorial modelling of surfaces to sharp linear contrasts, the same clean, precise incisions that Bardotti Biasion recognised at Casole. The figures stand parallel to broad, smooth grounds, in the manner of Sienese relief around 1300, and the miraculous is conveyed through gesture and posture rather than through any supernatural apparatus: no angel appears, no ray of light descends. Here, more clearly than anywhere else in the corpus, the sculptor works as a contemporary of Duccio, translating the measured narrative culture of Sienese painting into marble.

The dating calls for explicit caution, because the reference literature contradicts itself in a way that most summaries pass over. The Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale places the reliefs around 1311, on the strength of their links with the back panels of Duccio’s Maestà, installed in that year. The Dizionario Biografico entry on Gano says around 1310, and other summaries give 1308–1311 or 1310–1312. Yet the Dizionario Biografico entry on Gioacchino himself dates his monumental tomb to about 1330, in connection with the composition of his Legenda. It attributes the reliefs to an unidentified Sienese sculptor and names both Gano and Goro di Gregorio as candidates. Both positions cannot be right: if the tomb was built around 1330, Gano, dead by 1318, cannot be its author. The specialist literature on Gano favours the earlier dating, which fits the Duccio comparisons and the vigour of the cult immediately after 1305. The conflict nevertheless exists within a single reference work and should be stated openly.

## The Cortona Question: The Tomb-Altar of Saint Margaret and Its Satellites

A small marble fragment provides the hinge between the Sienese works and the most contested chapter of the corpus. From the basilica of San Domenico in Siena comes a fragment of a funerary gable with a blessing Christ that closely resembles the Redeemer in the trefoil of the Casole tomb; published in 1992 in the German inventory of the churches of Siena, it has been taken as evidence that Gano built at least one further tomb in Siena itself, now lost. Even this modest piece is not free of uncertainty, since the sources disagree on its present location, placing it either in San Domenico or in the Pinacoteca — one more of the small verifications that work on Gano continually demands.

### The Tomb-Altar of Saint Margaret of Cortona

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-06.jpg" alt="Gothic marble wall tomb set in a pointed recess in a banded wall. Under two crocketed gables with trefoil openings, two standing figures lift a coffered canopy with hanging drapery above the effigy of a woman in a plain habit. The sarcophagus front bears four relief scenes, and a lower register on carved corbels holds smaller reliefs. On the outer columns, at the height of the gables, stand Gabriel and the Virgin of the Annunciation.">
  <figcaption>Gano di Fazio (attributed; traditionally given to Angelo and Francesco di Pietro), Funerary monument of Saint Margaret of Cortona, marble, before 1317 or second half of the fourteenth century. Cortona, Basilica di Santa Margherita.</figcaption>
</figure>

The most ambitious and most disputed addition to Gano di Fazio's corpus lies outside Sienese territory altogether. It is the funerary monument of Saint Margaret of Cortona, who died in 1297, in the basilica that bears her name. Everything at stake in the question of a "late Gano" hangs on this marble. If the attribution to him holds, it is his final masterpiece, carved before his death in 1317 or early 1318. If the traditional reading of a document of 1362 holds, it belongs to another generation entirely. The monument is best described, following Bardotti Biasion, as a tomb-altar: a suspended wall tomb conceived from the outset to serve the liturgical traffic of a living cult, like the dismembered Piccolomini complex in Siena.

The architecture is of unusual richness for the suspended type. The ensemble stands within a tall pointed recess in a wall of alternating light and dark bands, framed by a border of inlaid quatrefoil ornament. That setting reflects the basilica's nineteenth-century reconstruction rather than the monument's original placement. Within the recess two crocketed gables rise side by side, each enclosing a deep trefoil opening, their rakes studded with leaf crockets. Below the point where the two arches meet projects a small carved figure. Columns with foliate capitals carry the canopy; the outer pair, on both sides, are twisted in a spiral. On slender colonnettes at the outer edges stand two full-length figures facing one another across the whole width of the monument. They form an Annunciation that flanks the gables: Gabriel at the left with his gesture of address, the Virgin standing at the right.

The central invention is the apparatus of revelation. Two standing figures, traditionally described as angels, are placed on the cornice of the sarcophagus and raise above the effigy an entire coffered slab, a marble baldachin, from whose edges heavy swags of drapery fall through their hands. The motif of attendants unveiling the body descends from Arnolfian prototypes and appears, in gentler form, in the three wingless attendants who hold up the cloth on Gano's tomb at Casole. Here the cloth has become architecture, and the gesture of showing has become a permanent theatrical act performed for every pilgrim who approaches.

Beneath this canopy lies the effigy of Margaret, her head on a cushion, her body wrapped in the plain habit of a Franciscan tertiary, the drapery falling in long, quiet folds. The contrast with the richness above is surely deliberate: the penitent of Cortona is shown in death with the same renunciatory plainness she chose in life. The saint's body is venerated on the high altar of the basilica, so the marble chest serves today as a cenotaph within a monument originally built to exalt the body itself.

The sarcophagus front carries four relief panels with scenes from the saint's life, small figures set against smooth grounds in the Sienese manner, framed by plain mouldings. The right-hand panel shows a figure laid out on a bed surrounded by mourners, evidently the saint's death or a deathbed episode. The subjects of the other panels are left unnamed here, since their identification should rest on the published literature rather than on a photograph. The narrative manner, with few actors, broad empty grounds and the event carried by gesture, is the same "predella style" that links this front to the Piccolomini miracle reliefs in Siena. Below the chest runs a second register that gives the monument its double nature: an entablature carried on foliate corbels, between which smaller relief panels showing the posthumous miracles address the devotee at close range, at the height of an altar mensa. This lower zone, together with the overall structure, allowed the ensemble to be reconstructed as tomb and altar in one, with the cult of the new beata celebrated directly beneath her displayed effigy. The parallel with the reconstructed Piccolomini tomb-altar in Santa Maria dei Servi is close, and it is one of the strongest internal arguments for placing both works in the same milieu, if not in the same hand.

For most of the twentieth century the monument was given to two otherwise unknown masters of Assisian origin, Angelo and Francesco di Pietro. The attribution rested on a notarial document of 1362, published in 1896 and read as a guarantee connected with the commission. Several critics nevertheless kept noticing stylistic links with Gano, links impossible to reconcile with so late a date: either the eye or the document had to yield. Bardotti Biasion's article of 1984 resolved the impasse by re-reading the document. In her interpretation the notarial act of 1362 concerns only the arrangement of the saint's coffin, not the making of the monument. That freed the tomb for a dating before 1317 and an attribution to Gano at the very end of his career. Around the tomb-altar she gathered a small Cortonese group attributed to the same hand: a slab with seven small busts of saints in clipei, a gable apex with a blessing Redeemer in the sacristy, and a Madonna and Child from the façade, now in the Museo Diocesano.

The proposal has never won universal assent, and the dissent is weighty. In 1990 a leading specialist of Sienese sculpture restored the monument to the second half of the Trecento under its traditional attribution, and judged the Cortona Madonna Umbrian rather than Sienese. The *Dizionario Biografico* is visibly reserved. It finds an attribution to a final phase of Gano only faintly plausible, and only for the tomb-altar itself. More probably, it suggests, this and other contested works belong to a group of sculptures bridging Gano and the following generation, produced by one of the many documented but largely faceless artists who worked in the same circles. Against this, many recent surveys present the Cortona tomb under Gano's name, and the art-historical photographic archives catalogue it so; the attribution is probably now the majority view. But it should be held with its construction visible. It rests on a stylistic argument joined to a contested re-reading of a single document, and it is not secure in the way that Casole is secure.

Read as Gano's, the monument shows his style in its final, partial opening toward the Gothic of Giovanni Pisano. The architecture grows complex and vertical, the figures take on movement, the drapery becomes rich, fringed and deeply cut, and the chiaroscuro darkens with the use of the drill. Yet the volumes remain compact and legible, the contours continuous, the faces composed, as at Casole; the drapery still stresses, and never cancels, the body beneath it. Read as a work of the later Trecento, the same features become retrospective rather than prophetic. If the attribution falls, the picture of a "late Gano" opening toward Giovanni Pisano loses its principal monument. If it stands, Cortona becomes the place where one of the most restrained sculptors of his generation came to terms, on his own conditions, with the Gothic he had long held at a distance. Either way, the monument is the most complete surviving statement of the Sienese tomb-altar idea, standing at the point where the history of Gano di Fazio either culminates or ends.

## The Late Gano? Massa Marittima, Berlin and the Scattered Fragments

### Saints and Prophets from the Cathedral of Massa Marittima

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-07.jpg" alt="Five marble statuettes of standing male saints displayed in a row on a dark table. From left: a bald elder with a long wavy beard; an elder with a long beard in deep corkscrew curls and a blue-lined mantle; Saint John the Baptist in a shaggy camel-hair garment beneath a blue-lined mantle; a beardless youth with short curls holding a closed book, his mantle lined in bright blue; and Saint Peter with a short beard, a brooch at his chest and a key in his hand. Several figures keep traces of blue paint and patterned borders.">
  <figcaption>Gano di Fazio (attributed), Five statuettes of saints and prophets, including Saint John the Baptist and Saint Peter, marble with traces of polychromy and gilding, c. 1315–1318. Massa Marittima, Museo di Arte Sacra (former convent of San Pietro all’Orto), from the cathedral of San Cerbone.</figcaption>
</figure>

The other major group of late attributions in Gano di Fazio’s corpus comes from the Maremma. From the cathedral of San Cerbone at Massa Marittima, and now in the Museo di Arte Sacra in the same town, comes a set of marble statuettes of saints, apostles and prophets, probably once part of the cathedral’s choir screen. The museum displays eleven of them, and the five shown here are among those most closely associated with Gano’s name. The place of the group within his corpus is itself unstable, and instructively so. The article of 1984 in Prospettiva that first gave the group to Gano treated all eleven as his. Recent accounts are more divided. The regional tourism literature states that eight, described as white marble, are attributable to Gano, and other recent accounts assign the remaining three, described as painted white-grey marble, to Camaino di Crescentino. The Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale places the figures in the late, intensely Gothic phase of Gano’s activity, in the years before his death. Dissenting voices have dated them instead to the second quarter of the Trecento: one scholar did so in 1968, and in 1990 a critic proposed a collaborator of Goro di Gregorio as their author.

The attribution to Gano has nevertheless recently received wide international exposure. On 25 September 2024 four of the figures left the Museo di Arte Sacra on loan. The museum’s own Italian designations call them a bearded old priest-saint, Saint John the Baptist, a beardless apostle with a book and a second bearded priest-saint, the last presented in the exhibition literature as Saint Peter. They travelled to the exhibition Siena: The Rise of Painting, 1300–1350, shown at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York from 13 October 2024 to 26 January 2025 and at the National Gallery in London from 8 March to 22 June 2025, where they were presented as works by Gano of about 1315–1318.

The figure at the far left is an elderly man with a bald crown, curling locks at the temples and a long, wavy beard. He wears a tunic with an embroidered collar and a mantle drawn diagonally across his body. His right hand is raised toward his chest and his left gathers the folds at his side, and he turns three-quarters, as if engaged by someone beyond the frame. He carries no attribute, and the photograph shows no surviving colour. Next to him stands an elder with a dense crown of hair and a beard that falls to his chest in deep, corkscrew curls. His brow is furrowed and his eyes look sharply outward, the most intense of the five faces. His mantle, with a patterned hem and a dark blue lining, is gathered at the waist by his left hand. His right hand, at his chest, may once have held an attribute now lost.

At the centre is Saint John the Baptist, identified beyond doubt by the garment of camel hair that hangs in long, shaggy locks beneath his mantle down to his bare feet. His hair and beard are wild and curling. His mantle, lined in blue, is drawn across the body by his left hand, and his right hand rests at his breast. A dark knotted element on his shoulder may be a clasp or the knot of the hair garment. To his right stands a beardless youth with short, rounded curls and a gently inclined head. He holds a closed book upright against his side and lays his right hand on his chest. His mantle falls in heavy, layered folds, its blue lining and patterned borders more fully preserved than on any other figure in the group. Last on the right is a saint with short, curling hair and a square-cut beard, his mantle fastened at the chest by a lozenge-shaped brooch. In his right hand he holds a dark object that appears to be the handle of a key, which supports the identification as Saint Peter, although the museum designates the figure more generically as a bearded priest-saint. Like the youth and the Baptist, he stands barefoot, as apostles and prophets conventionally do.

The style is what has made these figures the touchstone of a “late Gano”. Compared with the calm, compact attendants of the Casole tomb, the drapery here is cut deeply, with the drill opening dark channels between the folds. Hems and borders are worked in patterned bands, and the mantles fall in heavy, overlapping layers that animate the whole figure. The faces, too, have grown more charged: the furrowed brow of the long-bearded elder, the Baptist’s untamed mass of hair, the grave watchfulness of Peter. If the figures are Gano’s, they show the partial opening toward the Gothic of Giovanni Pisano that his attributed late works are said to display. How far that reading can be trusted is discussed below, under style.

The surviving polychromy deserves attention in its own right. Four of the five figures keep the azure of their mantle linings, with traces of colour or gilding along the borders, so the group was evidently conceived in colour rather than in the bare white marble the visitor now sees. That observation bears on the attribution as well. The published division of the eleven figures sets eight white figures by Gano against three painted ones by Camaino di Crescentino. In this photograph, colour survives precisely on figures that the exhibition literature presents as Gano’s. Either the division rests on a different criterion, such as the type of marble, or the published accounts need correcting. The point remains to be verified against the museum’s own catalogue.

Two further remarks keep the Massa group in focus. The first is a warning against a recurrent confusion. These statuettes must be kept rigorously distinct from the Arca di San Cerbone in the same cathedral, the great reliquary tomb signed by Goro di Gregorio and dated 1324, with which popular accounts regularly entangle them. The second is a point of real historical interest that the confusion obscures. If the attribution and the late dating are correct, the statuettes adopt elements of Giovanni Pisano’s language in a restrained, selective way that brings them close to Goro’s art. They would then stand at the threshold of the next generation of Sienese sculpture, in the very building where, within a decade, that generation produced one of its defining works. The older sculptor and the younger would share not only a city and a tradition but a single church.

At the margins of the corpus lie fragments whose identifications have shifted with almost every publication. The most discussed of them, the Berlin half-figure, is treated separately below. The others shade off into the periphery: a marble Adoring Angel of about 1315 in the Museo Civico Amedeo Lia at La Spezia; a [Christ Blessing](https://harvardartmuseums.org/collections/object/227638?position=0) at the Harvard Art Museums, Fogg inventory 1941.135; angel statues in the Chigi-Saracini collection given to Gano's workshop; a Saint Catherine of Alexandria in a private collection, published in 1990 as possibly his and rejected in 1995 on the telling ground that its fine decorative treatment cancels precisely the corporeality that Gano's figures always assert. A marble head of a prophet has recently been offered on the art market under Gano's name, dated 1315–1317 by comparison with Massa Marittima; commercial attributions of this kind are not scholarly opinion, and none has been published and discussed in the literature.

### A Young Saint with a Covered Vessel (Mary Magdalene, Saint Galgano, or one of the Magi?)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Gano-di-Fazio/gano-di-fazio-08.jpg" alt="Marble half-figure of a beardless youth, cut off at the waist, turning his head to look up and away with lips slightly parted.   <figcaption>Gano di Fazio (attributed), Half-figure of a young saint holding a covered vessel (Saint Mary Magdalene or Saint Galgano; proposed as Caspar from an Adoration of the Magi), marble, c. 1310, 46 x 29 x 16 cm. Berlin, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, Skulpturensammlung (Bode-Museum), inv. 2955.</figcaption>
</figure>

Few works in Gano di Fazio’s corpus show as clearly as this one how unstable the identification of a medieval fragment can be. The marble half-figure in the Skulpturensammlung of the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, inventory 2955, now in the Bode-Museum, holds a covered vessel and has been named in at least three ways. The museum’s own designation leaves the choice open between Saint Mary Magdalene and Saint Galgano, the Sienese knight-hermit; the Enciclopedia dell’Arte Medievale entry of 1995 calls it a Magdalene; one recent survey reads it simply as a young saint holding a reliquary. In 2019 a German specialist in Trecento sculpture proposed a fourth reading, which, if it holds, transforms the piece from an isolated devotional bust into a fragment of a lost narrative group. The museum dates the figure to about 1310 and records its dimensions as 46 by 29 by 16 cm.

The figure is cut off at the waist, its base irregular, so that we see a youth from the hips upward, turned three-quarters to his left with his head swung further in the same direction. The face is beardless and smooth-planed, the nose long and straight, the lips slightly parted, the eyes fixed on something above and beyond the viewer. The whole head has an air of sudden attention, as if the figure had just caught sight of what it was looking for. The hair is the most striking passage of carving: it falls to the nape in rolled curls, each coiled into a tight spiral whose centre is hollowed by a ring of drill holes, giving the coiffure a regular, almost ornamental rhythm.

The dress is described with equal care. Over a tunic with a round, plain neckline the figure wears a mantle fastened at the right shoulder by a square brooch carved with a small cross-shaped motif, from which a bordered strap runs diagonally across the chest. The borders of mantle and sleeves are decorated with bands of drilled dots between incised lines, the cuffs are trimmed in the same way, and on the left breast a square panel of ornament—an interlace enclosing a field of drilled dots—suggests an embroidered apparel or a piece of applied textile. These are the details of rich contemporary costume, rendered with a goldsmith’s attention to surface, and they give the piece a courtly rather than a penitential character.

With his right hand the youth grips the lid of a small vessel, which his left hand supports from below. The vessel is a piece of micro-architecture: a polygonal casket with arched openings framed by colonnettes, crowned by a frieze of lozenges and a shallow lid. Its form explains the range of identifications. For the Magdalene it would be the jar of ointment with which she anointed Christ; for a generic saint it could be a reliquary; for one of the Magi it would be the precious gift carried to the Child. The figure’s sex, with short curled hair, flat chest and youthful masculine features, sits uneasily with the Magdalene reading, and the courtly costume, rather than the mantle-of-hair or the cloak of the penitent, points away from her as well. Saint Galgano, a young knight who renounced the world, accounts better for the youthfulness but less well for the vessel.

The 2019 proposal starts from a second, previously unknown bearded half-figure, likewise holding a lidded vessel, which its publisher attributed to Gano and connected with the Berlin youth on the grounds of matching style, measurements and attribute. Both figures, he argued, belonged to an Adoration of the Magi modelled on Arnolfo di Cambio’s group in Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome of about 1290, with the bearded figure as Balthasar and the Berlin youth as Caspar, traditionally the youngest of the three kings. The hypothesis has much in its favour. It explains the beardless face and the courtly dress; it explains the vessel as a royal gift; and it explains the sudden turn of the head and the upward gaze, which would have been directed toward the star or the Child in a lost centre of the group. It also places Gano once again in direct dialogue with Arnolfo, the sculptor whose compact volumes and clear contours critics have long recognised in the Casole tomb. If the reading is correct, the inventory label “Magdalene or Galgano” records a question that no longer needs to be asked; until the article’s arguments have been tested by further discussion, however, the identification should be presented as a proposal, not as a settled fact. It is also the only point at which a “reliquary” enters serious discussion of Gano: the literature offers no reliquary bust or reliquary arm by him, only this vessel, read among other ways as a reliquary.

One feature of the carving deserves a cautionary note. The curls ringed with drill holes are precisely the motif that Previtali, in 1983, listed among the personal marks of Marco Romano in his reassignment of the Porrina monument. Their appearance on a work attributed to Gano does not by itself undermine either attribution—the motif may have belonged to the shared practice of the Sienese cathedral workshop in which both men were formed—but it shows how cautiously such details must be used as signatures of a single hand. The comparison that matters for Gano is rather the clear, continuous contour of the shoulders and arms, the drapery laid in broad, shallow planes, and the composed, softly rounded face, which recall the Casole figures more than the mobile, deeply undercut manner of the Porrina. Against the late, intensely Gothic Massa Marittima statuettes, which the museum’s date of about 1310 would precede, the Berlin youth would stand at an intermediate point in Gano’s career, between the Casole tomb and the works of his last years.

## What Gano Did Not Make

A responsible profile must also mark the works and roles that do not belong to Gano, because several of them circulate persistently in derivative accounts. Nothing in the specialist literature links Gano with any work in San Gimignano: the altar of San Bartolo in Sant'Agostino there is a Renaissance work of Benedetto da Maiano, carved more than a century and a half after Gano's death, and no ciborium or altar fragments in the Collegiata of San Gimignano have been attributed to him in the scholarship reviewed here. No source connects him with the Cappella dei Nove or with any other project in the Palazzo Pubblico of Siena, associations that would in any case sit awkwardly with the building history of the palace and with his death by 1318. His involvement with the fabric of Siena Cathedral, as we have seen, rests on his residence and on stylistic hypotheses about the window busts, not on a single document. Nor, as the Berlin case shows, does he have a reliquary bust or reliquary arm to his name. The real geography of the corpus is compact and coherent: Casole d'Elsa, Siena, Arezzo, Cortona and Massa Marittima, with dispersed fragments in Berlin, La Spezia, Cambridge in Massachusetts and private collections. It is the geography of a Sienese master who worked for patrons within the Sienese orbit and whose works travelled only after his monuments were dismembered.

## Style, Technique and Relations

Any definition of Gano's style must begin by acknowledging its evidentiary base: one certain monument, around which everything else is arranged by resemblance. The marks of the Casole hand are the clean contour, the compact volume, the shallow incised drapery and the serene, softly rounded faces. The crucial historical observation about this manner is a negative one. Around 1303–1305, a Sienese sculptor who had almost certainly grown up within sight of the cathedral workshop could still carve as if Giovanni Pisano's façade had never happened. That places Gano in a line of descent that runs back to Nicola Pisano and to Arnolfo di Cambio, not forward from Giovanni, and it makes him, within his generation, the most deliberate of classicists — if classicism is the right word for an art that prefers continuity of contour and repose of surface to torsion and pathos. If the attributed corpus is accepted in its fullest form, his development then consists in a gradual and strictly partial opening toward Giovanni's Gothic: the stronger emotional characterisation of the Piccolomini reliefs; the drilled, freely carved drapery of the Cortona Annunciation; the "intense Gothicism", as the encyclopaedia entry has it, of the Massa figures, which one recent synthesis describes as a late and rather external awareness of the dynamism and sharp contrast proper to Giovanni Pisano. The narrative is attractive, and it is also partly circular, since the late works are attributed to Gano precisely because they fit it; the *Dizionario Biografico* adds the sobering note that the sculptor's relatively early death leaves little room for extensive evolution after the Piccolomini reliefs. The developmental Gano, in short, is a construction built on the attributed corpus; the documented Gano stands still at Casole.

His relations to the other protagonists of Sienese sculpture can be stated with corresponding care. With Camaino di Crescentino the relation is social and topographical before it is stylistic: the two were neighbours in Vallepiatta, both moved in the orbit of the cathedral works, and recent museum attributions now place statuettes by both masters within the Massa Marittima series — a proximity that is suggestive of collaboration without proving it. With Tino di Camaino, Camaino's son and the dominant tomb sculptor of the next decades, the relation is typological rather than personal. The suspended wall tomb with a life-size effigy, of which Gano's Casole monument is among the earliest complete surviving examples, became in Tino's hands, from Pisa to Florence and Naples, the ruling funerary formula of the early Trecento; yet one recent survey draws the stylistic contrast sharply, observing that unlike Tino, Gano carefully avoided any allusion to the rough and restless sculpture of Giovanni Pisano. It would go beyond the evidence to call Gano Tino's teacher or even his direct model, and no document joins their names; what can be said is that the type Tino raised to European importance already stood, fully worked out, in Gano's monument at Casole. And with Marco Romano, finally, the relation is the one traced above: a documented verbal imitation, a probable physiognomic response, and a critical history in which the two hands were confused for decades precisely because they had once worked side by side.

On technique the accessible evidence is thinner than one would wish, and the thinness should be stated rather than papered over. The Casole figures are marble, worked with shallow incised drapery and little undercutting; the Cortona and Massa figures, if they are Gano's, introduce the drill and drapery carved free of the ground. The Massa statuettes preserve traces of polychromy, and the Casole tomb keeps its painted lunette, so that Gano's monuments were conceived as polychrome ensembles — architecture, sculpture and painting collaborating in a single coloured apparatus — and not as the bare white marble the modern visitor sees. No technical study of the Casole tomb, whether an analysis of the marble's provenance or a restoration report, surfaced in the accessible sources; the stone's origin and the monument's conservation history therefore remain to be verified against the records of the competent Soprintendenza.

## Workshop and Legacy

No document names an assistant, a pupil or a workshop of Gano. The records describe a property-owning *magister lapidum* who left two daughters as his heirs, and they are silent about apprentices, partners and shop organisation. When the literature speaks of a "workshop of Gano" — for the Chigi-Saracini angels, for instance, or for some of the cathedral window busts — the phrase is stylistic shorthand: it designates works close to his manner but lacking the quality or the specific character that would justify an autograph attribution, and it should never be mistaken for evidence of a documented bottega with a roster of names. His legacy, accordingly, has to be traced through types and motifs rather than through people, and along three distinct channels.

The first is the suspended wall tomb with effigy, present complete at Casole at the very opening of the century and developed by Tino di Camaino into the dominant funerary formula of the age. The second is the clear, essential narrative relief of the Piccolomini scenes, which anticipates the "pictorial" relief that Goro di Gregorio and Giovanni d'Agostino would bring to maturity in the following decades — the specifically Sienese art of carving as if drawing, with few figures, smooth grounds and an economy of gesture learned from panel painting.

The third, and the strangest, is epigraphic: the adoption of his signature formula, almost word for word, by Marco Romano in Venice in 1318, the year of Gano's own posthumous record — as if the younger master were signing, at the moment of the older one's death, a debt contracted at Casole fifteen years before. If the Massa Marittima figures are indeed Gano's, a fourth channel opens, a direct passage from his late style into Goro di Gregorio's art within the walls of a single cathedral. The legacy is real, then, but it is diffuse, transmitted through monuments rather than through a school; and the recurring claim that Gano "founded" Sienese sculpture should be read as a retrospective interpretation of that diffuse presence, not as the record of an institution.

## Critical Fortune: From Antiquarian Notice to Modern Corpus

Gano's critical fortune falls naturally into four phases, and each has left sediment in the accounts that circulate today — which is why reading about him without a map of that fortune is so hazardous. The first phase is antiquarian. Sienese local historians from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century knew the Casole tomb and its inscriptions, described the Querceto head over its church door, and drew on the fourteenth-century tax registers; an early nineteenth-century compilation of Sienese artists' biographies records vernacular entries naming the heir of "master Gano, sculptor" among the property owners of Vallepiatta. These notices preserved the name and the monument through centuries in which no one would have called Gano an artist of consequence, but they also sowed confusion, since the same compiler mentions a second sculptor of the same name — a datum not yet verified against the documents, which warns us how easily homonyms multiply in the Sienese record.

The second phase, running from the general history of 1906 to the early 1940s, built a heroic Gano. The attribution of the Porrina, first advanced in 1906 and restated with eloquence in the *Emporium* article of 1942 that hailed the statue as his masterpiece, made him one of the inventors of the modern portrait in stone; general histories fixed this enlarged figure in their narratives, and it is this Gano, essentially, who survives in the Web Gallery of Art biography and in the many texts that descend from the older dictionaries. The third phase is one of documentary and critical correction, and it begins and ends with the two demolitions already described: Bacci's biography of 1944, which dissolved the conflation with Galgano di Giovanni, anchored the life in the Sienese archives and rejected the Porrina attribution; and Previtali's article of 1983, which, gathering the threads spun after the war between Casole, Cremona and Venice, gave the Porrina definitively to Marco Romano — a reassignment described by the mid-1990s as critical consensus. Between those two dates the heroic Gano was taken apart piece by piece.

The fourth phase, from the 1980s onward, is reconstruction on new foundations, and its central figure is Bardotti Biasion. Her article of 1984 and her encyclopaedia entry of 1995 assembled the modern corpus around the signed tomb: the Piccolomini reliefs, the Cortona tomb-altar with its satellites, and — following the Massa Marittima proposal published in the same issue of *Prospettiva* — the late statuettes; other scholars added the Ubertini tomb, the Querceto head, the San Domenico fragment and the Berlin half-figure. Alongside the builders a sceptical counter-current has always run: in 1990 the very author of the 1942 *Emporium* article rejected the Cortona and Massa attributions, and the cautious *Dizionario Biografico* entry of 1999 still registers a lingering suspicion that several hands may shelter under the one name. The most recent syntheses of Sienese Gothic sculpture — a collection of studies of 2005 and an illustrated survey of 2011 with a chapter on Gano di Fazio and sculpture at the passage from the Duecento to the Trecento — present a substantial corpus with the gradations of certainty marked; and the exhibition *Siena: The Rise of Painting, 1300–1350* of 2024–2025 carried the Massa figures, under Gano's name, to a wider international public than the sculptor had enjoyed at any moment since the Porrina was taken from him. The wheel of his fortune, in other words, is still turning, and the direction of its present turn is upward.

## Conclusion

The secure Gano di Fazio is a small but singularly precise figure. He was a Sienese *magister lapidum* who lived in Vallepiatta beside the cathedral workshop, bought land in May 1302 and paid taxes on it in June, and appears in the fiscal registers of the commune through 1316. Soon after July 1303 he carved, and proudly signed in Latin verse, the tomb of Bishop Tommaso d'Andrea at Casole d'Elsa — one of the earliest Tuscan suspended wall tombs with a life-size effigy and the earliest complete funerary monument of the Sienese area. He was dead by 1318, leaving his property to his daughters Agnese and Ganuccia. Around that core, a century of scholarship has gathered a plausible but uneven constellation of attributions, and their gradations must travel with them wherever they are repeated. The Querceto head and the Piccolomini reliefs are widely accepted; the Ubertini tomb, the Cortona tomb-altar and the Massa Marittima statuettes are accepted by many and contested by some; a fringe of fragments in Berlin, La Spezia, Cambridge and private hands rests on single publications or on market opinion. And the great Porrina statue, the work that made Gano famous in the first half of the twentieth century, belongs to Marco Romano, and takes with it the claim to the earliest standing funerary effigy in Italy.

Gano matters historically less for any single masterpiece than for his position, and that position turns out to be a paradoxical one. At the very moment when Giovanni Pisano's Gothic had transformed the Sienese workshop in which he was formed, Gano carved in a clear, Arnolfian, essentially pictorial idiom, as though quietly insisting that another road out of Nicola's art remained open. On that road he set out, complete, the tomb type that Tino di Camaino would make dominant across Italy; he carved, if the Piccolomini reliefs are his, some of the earliest marble that thinks like a Sienese predella; and he wrote a signature so memorable that another major sculptor copied it almost word for word in Venice in the year of his death. A sculptor known securely from one monument thus stands at the joints of three histories — of the Italian tomb, of Sienese narrative relief and of the sculptor's signature itself. A responsible account of him can claim no more; but it need claim no less, provided only that it keeps document, inscription and attribution each in its proper place, and says plainly where the record ends and interpretation begins.

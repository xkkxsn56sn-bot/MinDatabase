## Introduction: a name between two famous relatives

Sienese painting around 1300 has its acknowledged protagonists: Duccio, Simone Martini, Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti. In their shadow sit figures whose standing depends less on firmly attached works than on how archival notices, family ties and reconstructed catalogues come together. *Memmo di Filippuccio* is a clear case. For most of the historiography he was what one reference entry calls "only a name in the margin" of the biographies of his son Lippo Memmi and his son-in-law Simone Martini. He became an artistic personality in the twentieth century, by a route that runs in the opposite direction from the usual one. Normally a corpus is gathered first and a name attached later. Here there was a name with an unusually concrete civic career, and scholars set out to give it a body of work.

This essay tries to keep the two halves of that operation apart. The first half is the documented Memmo: a man fined in Siena in 1288, paid by the commune of San Gimignano, named as the father of a young painter in 1317, and selling a house to Simone Martini in 1324. The second half is the attributed Memmo: the painter of the Camera del Podestà, of the Madonna in San Jacopo, of the dossal from Santa Chiara and of fresco fragments in the Collegiata and in San Pietro, and in some accounts a member of Giotto's crew at Assisi and an illuminator of choirbooks. Much of the confusion that now surrounds Memmo, especially in English-language reference material and on the web, comes from collapsing an attribution into a document or a hypothesis into a fact.

Three scholars are named here because they built the modern Memmo. <a href="/scholars.html#longhi-roberto">Roberto Longhi</a> supplied the founding attributions. <a href="/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni">Giovanni Previtali</a> extended the catalogue backwards to Assisi and Pisa and outwards to manuscript illumination. <a href="/scholars.html#carli-enzo">Enzo Carli</a> worked through the San Gimignano documents and gave the painter his civic setting. Later contributions, including the correctives that have dismantled parts of their constructions, are described here without naming their authors. That reflects the format of this essay, not a judgement on those contributions.

## Sources and historiography: from the Lives to the Paragone years

No early source preserves an independent biography of Memmo. The literary tradition about the Memmi family, from the sixteenth-century Lives onward, centred on Lippo and on Lippo's partnership with Simone Martini. It also produced a confusion that has never quite gone away: Simone himself was called "Simone Memmi". The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica already corrected this, explaining that Simone was "called also Simone di Martino, and more commonly, but not correctly, Simon Memmi". The family name passed to him only because his brother-in-law Lippo so often worked beside him. Seventeenth-century Sienese antiquarians had already noticed that Simone was the son of a Martino, while Lippo was "figliuolo di Memmo, e non della famiglia de' Memmi". In other words, "Memmi" was a patronymic, not a surname.

Memmo's documentary existence was established gradually by Sienese and Florentine archival research in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The house sale to Simone Martini in 1324 entered the literature through the great nineteenth-century collections of Sienese documents. The San Gimignano notices came to light through two publications. One was a German historian's extracts from Florentine manuscript compilations, published in 1900. The other was a 1909 article in the Rassegna d'arte senese devoted to a "school of art" at San Gimignano in the Trecento. That second article first assembled the evidence for a family workshop in the Valdelsa town. A short note of 1920 in the same journal attached Memmo's name to a specific work for the first time: the fresco of the Madonna and Child with Saints James the Greater and John the Evangelist in the apse of San Jacopo al Tempio.

The decisive moment came in 1948. In a long footnote to his essay "Giudizio sul Duecento", Roberto Longhi assigned to Memmo the cycle of profane scenes in a room of the tower of the Palazzo del Popolo. He also accepted the San Jacopo fresco. The cycle had been partly visible under whitewash in the nineteenth century. The great Italian connoisseur of the 1860s–80s had picked out the fragments and judged them the work of a Sienese painter. The whitewash was removed only around 1921, and a monographic study of the room's frescoes appeared in the Rivista d'arte in 1938, still without a convincing attribution. Longhi's proposal, made on stylistic grounds, linked the documented civic painter to the most conspicuous civic decoration in the town. Every later catalogue grows from that link.

The early 1960s were the decisive years, and the forum was the journal Paragone. In 1962 Longhi assigned to Memmo the central panel of a polyptych with the Madonna and Child in San Francesco at Pisa, together with a lateral saint in the Lindenau-Museum at Altenburg. In the same year Giovanni Previtali published "Il possibile Memmo di Filippuccio", whose title signals its conjectural nature. He reconstructed the painter's career before San Gimignano, identifying his hand in the dossal with the Madonna and saints in the episcopal palace at Oristano in Sardinia. He also took up Longhi's suggestion that Memmo had worked in the Upper Church at Assisi. That same year Enzo Carli, in his chapter on art in a co-authored volume on San Gimignano, used the documents to place Memmo firmly in his adopted town. Carli added the entrance-wall (controfacciata) frescoes of the Collegiata and the dossal from the destroyed convent of Santa Chiara to the catalogue. In 1963 he returned to the subject in Paragone with "Ancora dei Memmi a San Gimignano", recognising Memmo's hand in the frescoes of San Pietro, alongside an assistant more open to Simone Martini's innovations. In 1964 Previtali completed the expansion with "Miniature di Memmo di Filippuccio". Starting from the 1303 document of book decoration, he gathered around the name a group of illuminated manuscripts of high quality. In 1967 he fixed Memmo's place in the Assisi workshop in his monograph on Giotto and his bottega.

The 1970s and early 1980s were a period of further expansion, especially of the miniatures, and the 1982 exhibition "Il Gotico a Siena" gave the painter a monographic section in its catalogue. The reaction began with a review of that exhibition published in Arte cristiana in 1983. Its author noted a real discrepancy between the panels and frescoes on one side and the miniatures on the other, and proposed separating them. Later comparative work, summed up in the 1997 entry of the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale and the 2002 survey of Sienese illumination, confirmed the separation. The miniatures were redistributed among anonymous illuminators, and Memmo went back to being a fresco painter and panel painter of Giottesque formation. The 2009 entry in volume 73 of the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani represents the current consensus and is the most detailed modern synthesis. Since then the most substantial contributions have concerned the conservation and interpretation of the Camera del Podestà, discussed below.

## The documentary biography

The documented life of Memmo di Filippuccio fits into a handful of notices. They should be read slowly, because a good deal has been loaded onto them.

The earliest is a fine. In the Biccherna<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-biccherna" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> register of 1288, the main financial record of the Sienese commune, Memmo appears paying a penalty jointly with his brother Mino, also called Minuccio (Archivio di Stato di Siena, Biccherna 97, fol. 2r). The importance of the entry is mainly legal-demographic. A man who could be fined in his own right was of age, so modern scholarship puts his birth around the early 1260s. The 2009 Dizionario Biografico reasons in exactly this way, and it is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact. Birth dates such as "c. 1250" given in some databases have no documentary basis. The same Sienese context tells us, from secondary notices, that his father Filippuccio was a goldsmith and that Mino was also a painter. One frequently reproduced biographical note gives Filippuccio an activity range of 1273–1293. I have not been able to trace the underlying documents for that range, which should be treated as reported, not verified. A second notice in the Biccherna, from 1294 (Biccherna 110, fol. 125r), confirms Memmo's presence in Siena. The published summaries do not say what it concerns.

The San Gimignano period begins in 1303. Extracts from records of the commune, transmitted in Florentine manuscript compilations and published in 1900, show Memmo working in 1303 and again in 1305 on the decoration of books for the podestà. The books are lost. This notice is the only documentary basis for calling Memmo a miniaturist in any sense. As we shall see, an entire corpus of illuminated manuscripts was later built on it, more than it can bear. Other payments, gathered in the 1909 article, show that Memmo lived in San Gimignano with his whole family and was a regular salaried employee of the commune. He painted, as civic painters did in Siena too, not only monumental works but also banners, gonfalons and objects for everyday official use. Enzo Carli's reading of these records led him to describe Memmo as a "pittore civico" avant la lettre: a painter on the communal payroll, not a contractor hired for single jobs. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale goes further and speaks of the "office" of civic painter as attested by the documents. On that basis it argues that Memmo is unlikely to have left San Gimignano for long periods between 1303 and 1317.

That is the full extent of his documented civic role. The commission for this essay asked about his career as a "civic official" or "castellan". On the evidence available in the published literature, no such office is attested. None of the modern syntheses records Memmo as a castellan, councillor or magistrate in either San Gimignano or Siena. What is documented is employment, salaried and continuing, but employment as a painter. The 1909 article and Carli's 1962 chapter are known to the present author only through their summaries in the reference literature, so their full texts would have to be checked before the absence of an office could be declared absolute.

A related uncertainty concerns a group of lost frescoes. The Dizionario Biografico records that Memmo painted in the communal palace works that are now lost. Following Carli, it suggests that they may have been connected with the earlier celebratory frescoes in the Sala del Consiglio. Those frescoes, painted by the Florentine Azzo di Masetto, show the homage paid to an Angevin king and carry a long inscription of 1292 recording the arbitration of the San Gimignanese prelate Scolaio Ardinghelli. Opinions differ on whether the sovereign is Charles I of Anjou<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-charles-i-anjou" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, whose passage through the town in 1267 is often cited, or Charles II<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-charles-ii-anjou" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, as some recent descriptions of the cycle have it. The question lies outside Memmo's biography, except for one thing. Some popular summaries attribute these Angevin frescoes to Memmo himself, dating them "1292". That is a confusion: the documented author of the surviving cycle is Azzo, and Memmo's own work in the palace is lost.

The year 1317 brings the most discussed document. A payment from the San Gimignano commune, again known through a Florentine manuscript extract, records the "figure del palazzo cioè della Sala del Consiglio del Popolo" executed "per Memmo pittore e Lippo suo figliuolo". It is Lippo's first appearance in the archives, and he appears explicitly as Memmo's son. Memmo is named as the payee. The interpretation of this notice is taken up separately below, because the reference literature contradicts itself on it.

After 1317 Memmo disappears from the San Gimignano records. He reappears in Siena in 1321, when a document records him receiving a sum of money. The published summaries give no payer, amount or reason. The last notice dates from early 1324. Between 2 January and early February of that year, Simone Martini bought a house from Memmo for twenty florins<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gold-florin" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> and at the same time married his daughter Giovanna, giving her the generous sum of two hundred and twenty florins as a donatio propter nuptias. The exact closing date is reported inconsistently. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale gives 8 February, while another reference lexicon gives 4 February and places the house in the Sienese district of San Egidio. The discrepancy can only be settled by going back to the notarial transcription. The Dizionario Biografico treats this as the last document concerning Memmo. No death date is documented. The total silence of the archives after 1324 suggests that he did not live much longer, but that too is an argument from silence. The "c. 1325" and "d. Siena" found in some databases are conjectures presented as data.

The later documents concerning Memmo's children belong to their own biographies. They include Lippo's 1333 Annunciation signed with Simone, the payments of 1341–42 "pro pictura turris" from the Sienese commune to Lippo and his brother Tederigo (also written Federigo), the brothers' joint purchases and commissions of 1343–47, and Lippo's last mention, in a transaction for his sister Giovanna in March 1348. No documented work by Tederigo survives. None of this later documentation involves Memmo, who had disappeared from the record almost two decades earlier.

## Before San Gimignano: Assisi, Pisa and Sardinia

The Dizionario Biografico notes that Memmo must have arrived in San Gimignano with a formation, and probably with a career, behind him. Everything said about that earlier career is attribution. It deserves particular scrutiny because some of it has been turned, in secondary literature, into documented fact.

The founding hypothesis, again Longhi's, is that the young Memmo worked in the Upper Church of San Francesco at Assisi in the early phase of the decoration given to Giotto and his large workshop, around 1290. Previtali developed it in 1962 and 1967. In the form summarised by the Dizionario Biografico, Memmo would have painted many of the half-length saints and prophets in the medallions (compassi) of the ornamental bands framing the Biblical stories, and would also have worked on the first Franciscan scenes. Previtali was so convinced by the Giottesque character of Memmo's later frescoes that, in the words of the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale, he took Memmo's participation at Assisi "as certain". He went so far as to regard him as one of the main protagonists of the enterprise alongside Giotto. The Dizionario Biografico adds that this participation "has not since been called into question", and later surveys of the Assisi workshop routinely list Memmo among the hands proposed for the Franciscan cycle.

Two cautions are needed. First, "not called into question" describes a scholarly consensus, not proof. The Assisi Memmo is a connoisseur's construct, based on the observation that the San Gimignano frescoes depend profoundly on the Assisi idiom and on the identification of matching morphologies in parts of the Assisi decoration. It may well be right, but it sits on a different epistemic plane from the 1288 fine. Second, and more seriously, a widely circulated English-language biographical note states that "a document of 1303 also records him as having worked in the upper church of San Francesco, Assisi". That statement is false. No document places Memmo at Assisi. The only document of 1303 concerns book decoration for the podestà of San Gimignano. The claim appears to merge that notice with Longhi's stylistic hypothesis, turning an attribution into an archival record. It has been copied onto tourist and educational websites and should be withdrawn wherever it appears.

The Pisan phase rests on two panels: the Madonna and Child still in San Francesco at Pisa and a lateral saint in the Lindenau-Museum at Altenburg, recognised as parts of a single polyptych and attributed to Memmo in 1962. The sources diverge even on the basics. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale gives the attribution to Longhi and calls the Altenburg saint a Francis; the Dizionario Biografico gives it to Previtali and calls the saint John the Evangelist, which is how museum and photo-archive records describe it. Later Pisan studies and the 2008 catalogue of the Lindenau-Museum's Sienese and Tuscan paintings moved both panels, with caution, to the problematic Master of San Torpè, and the Fondazione Zeri photo archive files the Pisa Madonna under that master, with Memmo listed as a "different attribution". The debate is complicated further by the hypothesis, advanced by Enzo Carli in 1974, that the Master of San Torpè is the Pisan painter-sculptor Vanni di Bindo. At present the polyptych is a disputed attribution and cannot anchor Memmo's chronology.

The Oristano dossal is more stable but less studied. Previtali in 1962 attributed to Memmo the dossal with the Madonna and saints in the episcopal palace at Oristano, previously considered Pisan. He proposed as its patron Scolaio Ardinghelli, the San Gimignano churchman named in the 1292 inscription in the Sala del Consiglio, who later became archbishop of Arborea and died there in 1300. The proposal is elegant. It would explain how a Sienese painter's panel reached Sardinia, and it would place Memmo within a San Gimignano network before 1303. But it is a hypothesis built on a hypothesis. The Dizionario Biografico notes that critics have not seriously tackled the question, yet argues for keeping the dossal in Memmo's catalogue because of its plastic construction. Recent Sardinian studies of Trecento painting on the island continue to list the dossal under Memmo. The Ardinghelli link raises the possibility, stated explicitly by the Dizionario Biografico, that Memmo had relations with San Gimignano before 1303. That remains possible but unproven.

A Florentine stage is the most tentative of all. The miniatures removed from Memmo's catalogue included books for the Florentine church of Santo Stefano in Pane, yet the Dizionario Biografico, noting that his frescoes seem to know later Florentine developments of the Giottesque idiom, refuses to rule out a Florentine passage completely. That is a cautious stylistic observation and nothing more.

## The civic painter of San Gimignano: the Collegiata and the churches

Carli's achievement in 1962 was to make Memmo's San Gimignano catalogue coherent with his documented presence. The works grouped there form the heart of the attributed corpus. They divide naturally into the Collegiata frescoes, the frescoes in minor churches, the Santa Chiara dossal and the Camera del Podestà.

On the entrance wall of the Collegiata (the town's former pieve, now dedicated to Santa Maria Assunta), above the right-hand door, are the oldest surviving paintings in the church after a pair of peacocks from the thirteenth-century decoration. Carli dated them around 1305 and assigned them to Memmo. They are arranged in registers. In the large upper lunette, badly damaged and cut through by the later opening of an oculus, are reclining female figures beside a saint, probably Saint Nicholas providing dowries for the three poor maidens. Other fragments have been read as two women asleep or dead and as the martyrdom of a saint in a cauldron, which suits Saint John the Evangelist. Above the door, two angels flank a fictive tabernacle containing a female saint and a Madonna and Child, both almost completely repainted in the early fifteenth century. The Dizionario Biografico notes how close the architectural framing remains to the Assisi Giotto. These frescoes were studied again in the large 2009 volume on the Collegiata's architecture, mural cycles and restorations, which has a dedicated essay on Memmo.

A small correction is needed. Several popular and semi-popular sources describe the entrance-wall frescoes as "documented" or "dated" in 1305, and one widely copied English note says Memmo "is documented in 1305 as having worked in the Collegiata". As far as the published literature shows, the date 1305 comes from Carli's stylistic placement and from the coincidence with the 1305 notice of book decoration for the podestà. No published record of a Collegiata payment to Memmo in 1305 is known to me. The frescoes are plausibly datable around 1305. They are not documented in that year.

The apse of San Jacopo al Tempio, a small Romanesque church on the edge of the town, contains a fresco of the Madonna and Child enthroned between Saints James the Greater and John the Evangelist. It was linked to Memmo in 1920 and accepted by Longhi in 1948, and is now one of the most widely accepted works in the catalogue. Other frescoes in the same church (a Crucifixion, an Entombment and a Saint John the Baptist) belong to the Simonesque wing of the Memmi workshop, close to the anonymous Master of the Straus Madonna, and should not be credited to Memmo.

In the small church of San Pietro in Forliano, Carli in 1963 recognised Memmo's hand in remains of Stories of the Virgin and in a Maestà with a donor on the right wall, which various sources also list as an Annunciation, a Madonna enthroned between two female saints (Catherine of Alexandria and Mary Magdalene) and an Adoration of the Magi. He also saw the intervention of an assistant whom the Dizionario Biografico, endorsing his view, calls already "Martinian". The church also has the rare iconography of the "Madonna del Passeggio", with the Child walking hand in hand with his mother, in frescoes the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale describes as merely close to Memmo's manner. San Pietro is therefore a mixed site, where Memmo's late manner meets the first reception of Simone. It is important evidence for the workshop's transition, but a poor place to look for unmixed autograph work.

The dossal from the Clarissan convent of Santa Chiara, a convent founded outside the walls in the 1260s and later suppressed, is now in the Pinacoteca of the Musei Civici. It shows the Madonna and Child with saints and a kneeling nun. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale dates it around 1310, and the communal inventory of the museum's holdings gives "circa 1310–1317". The Dizionario Biografico notes that in it Memmo "displays, precisely in the study of the plasticity of the figures, a return of updated Giottesque language", to which he adds "a chromatic vivacity of pure Sienese origin". It is the most secure panel in the corpus, and the most useful for judging Memmo as a painter on panel independently of the disputed Pisan and Sardinian works.

More peripheral attributions have varying support. The fresco Madonna and Child in the tabernacle on the outer face of the Arco delle Due Porte in Siena was first thought Duccio's by Carli; a 1956 study, comparing it with the conjugal scenes of the Camera del Podestà and the San Jacopo Saint John, proposed instead an artist in Memmo's orbit, which local guides now simplify to "attributed to Memmo". A niche Madonna at Certaldo and seventeen saints on a sacristy cupboard at San Lucchese near Poggibonsi appear in popular summaries of the catalogue but are not verified in the core scholarly literature examined for this essay, so they should be treated with reserve. The fresco of the Virgin suckling the Child with Saint Michael in Sant'Agostino at San Gimignano is generally placed early in Lippo's career, although <a href="/scholars.html#caleca-antonino">Antonino Caleca</a> proposed assigning it to the late phase of Memmo.

## The Camera del Podestà: discovery, attribution and date

The small vaulted room on the second floor of the Torre Grossa, the tallest tower in San Gimignano, completed in 1311, is the work by which Memmo is now known. It is called the Camera del Podestà, although its actual function is not documented and the name is traditional. It was uncovered around 1921. Restorations followed in the 1920s and again in the 1970s. A further campaign began in 2010, promoted by the Soprintendenza for Siena and Grosseto, the commune of San Gimignano and the Fondazione Musei Senesi and funded by the Fondazione Monte dei Paschi. It was carried out by Arc Conservazione e Restauro di Giuseppe e Massimo Gavazzi, the firm that won the Fondazione Musei Senesi tender, to deal with remaining causes of decay and recover the colour quality of the paintings.

The attribution is entirely stylistic. It goes back to Longhi's 1948 footnote, and the Dizionario Biografico notes that the cycle "had to wait for an indication from Longhi" to receive "a convincing and definitive attribution". It has been accepted ever since, and the cycle is the touchstone against which every other work is measured. Still, it should be said plainly, against widespread phrasing on museum websites and in travel literature, that no document names Memmo as the painter of this room. The English Wikipedia article on Memmo, which heads him "fl. 1303–1345", says he "is mentioned in the municipal records as painting several works at the Town Hall of San Gimignano in 1303", and goes straight on to describe the frescoes "in the mayoral chamber". A popular Tuscan art site says "the township commissioned" the frescoes "in 1303". Both sentences merge the 1303 book-decoration notice with a 1948 attribution and a date range that scholars derive from architecture. The resulting false "commission of 1303" has spread widely.

The accepted dating rests on two anchors. One is internal to the corpus: the entrance-wall frescoes of the Collegiata, placed by Carli around 1305. The other is architectural: the completion of the Torre Grossa in 1311. The Dizionario Biografico therefore dates the cycle "between that 1305 which dates the frescoes of the Collegiata and 1311, the year in which all work on the tower containing the room was completed". The lower limit depends on a stylistic date that is itself not documented. The upper limit assumes that the decoration of an interior room was finished when building work on the tower ended, which is likely but not certain. The dates circulate almost interchangeably. The Fondazione Musei Senesi's own museum page says the cycle was "painted by Memmo di Filippuccio from 1303 to 1310", the March 2010 report on the restoration in Il Giornale dell'Arte has "dipinto tra il 1305 e il 1311", other descriptions offer "1300–1310", and one travel writer even assigns the frescoes to Memmo and Lippo "in 1317". The defensible formula is "probably c. 1305–1311, on stylistic and architectural grounds".

## The Camera del Podestà: programme and interpretations

The cycle's subject has made it one of the most reproduced images of medieval daily life. The Google Arts & Culture presentation by the Musei Civici sums up its arrangement. On one wall, in the upper register, a youth emancipates himself and asks his parents for money, and is then lured by a procuress into the pavilion of a harlot, who steals his purse. Below are Aristotle and the courtesan, and a pair of lovers usually identified as Paolo and Francesca. Above the entrance, marriage is presented as the "cradle of love" in three scenes: the bride is led to the groom's house, the spouses bathe together in a large tub screened by a striped curtain and attended by a maid, and the spouses retire to bed, the maid drawing back the curtain as the husband lifts the covers to join his wife. Other descriptions add that the prodigal youth is beaten and returns home. The museum's own title for the cycle sets out a moral contrast: the "positive and negative outcomes of a young man's initiation into love".

Several interpretative questions follow, and they are best treated as open. The first is the identity of the courtesan who humiliates Aristotle. In the medieval exemplum of the "Lai d'Aristote" and its Latin and vernacular derivatives, the philosopher who lets himself be ridden like a horse by a seductive woman is humiliated by Phyllis. The Dizionario Biografico, however, calls the scene "Campaspe e Aristotele", using the name of Alexander's mistress, which crept into some versions of the tale. The two names refer to the same exemplum. The difference reflects a variant tradition, not two different subjects, and is not a disagreement about what is depicted.

The second question is the identification of the two readers. The Dizionario Biografico speaks of young lovers reading the "libro galeotto", the phrase from Inferno V for the book that brought Paolo and Francesca together. If the identification holds, it would be one of the earliest visual responses to the episode in Dante<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dante-alighieri" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>, and its date becomes delicate: a cycle finished by 1311 would precede, or at best barely coincide with, the earliest attested circulation of the Inferno. The question was raised at the study day of 28 October 2017 marking the seventh centenary of Lippo's Maestà. A Romance philologist of the University of Padua, author of the 2007 monograph "Le conseguenze di un bacio" on the reception of Francesca, put it directly in the title of his paper: "C'è il bacio di Paolo e Francesca nel ciclo di Memmo di Filippuccio a San Gimignano?" His contribution was published in the 2018 proceedings, "La Maestà di Lippo Memmi, 1317–2017" (Pacini), at pp. 55–66 (another bibliographic listing gives pp. 57–68). The title alone shows that the identification, often repeated in guidebooks as fact, is debated. In the absence of an inscription, the scene is better described as a pair of lovers reading, perhaps evoking Francesca.

The third question concerns the intended viewer and the moral register. Because the room is in the tower of the communal palace and is traditionally called the podestà's chamber, many readings take the cycle as a set of exempla for the magistrate. Seen this way, it warns him that sensual seduction undermines judgement, a theme pointedly shown in Aristotle, the wisest of men, brought low. It contrasts that disorder with the legitimate and orderly pleasures of marriage. Some descriptions add that public bathing was associated with immorality, so the bath scene too would be admonitory. That reading does not fit the composition well: the bath is part of the conjugal sequence, which by every account is the positive term of the contrast. Another strand, recorded in an English art database, reads the scenes as reflecting "the ideals and the way of life of a courtly society". It suggests the conjugal scenes might even narrate the life of actual lovers, presumably a podestà and his wife, a possibility the Dizionario Biografico also mentions cautiously. None of these readings is anchored in a documented commission. The function of the room and its users can only be inferred. The most economical interpretation, consistent with the arrangement the museum describes, is a didactic contrast between disordered and ordered love, set in a civic building. How it applied to the podestà is plausible but not demonstrated.

Stylistically, the Dizionario Biografico describes the cycle in terms worth quoting in substance. The narrative keeps "a great plastic relief" while paying "less attention to the typically Giottesque spatial framework". Its "minutely descriptive naturalism, associated with a delicate psychological vein", places the cycle "not completely within the figurative culture of Duccio's Siena of those same years". This is the core of Memmo's historical interest. The secular subject freed the painter from the hieratic conventions of devotional imagery. The domestic setting (the striped screen, the tub, the bed hangings, the patterned cloths) let him use a descriptive realism that owes more to the Assisi narratives than to Duccio. The figures are solid and weighty, yet set in shallow, almost stage-like spaces, not in the deep, measured boxes of Giotto's mature manner.

## The 1317 document and the Maestà question

In 1317 Lippo Memmi signed and dated the large fresco Maestà on the end wall of the Sala del Consiglio, the "Sala di Dante", in the communal palace. It was commissioned by Nello di Mino Tolomei, the podestà, who kneels on the right, presented to the Virgin by Saint Nicholas. The composition depends openly on Simone Martini's Maestà of 1315 in the Palazzo Pubblico in Siena, though the Dizionario Biografico notes its more iconic character and still Duccesque, archaising sense of order. The figures on the outer margins were probably added in 1366–67 by Bartolo di Fredi, and an inscription dated 1467 records a minor intervention by Benozzo Gozzoli.

The question for Memmo is whether the 1317 payment for "figures in the palace, that is, in the hall of the Council of the People" made to "Memmo the painter and Lippo his son" refers to this Maestà. The reference literature does not agree. The 2005 Dizionario Biografico entry on Lippo, in volume 65, states that "la decorazione ricordata nell'atto viene concordemente identificata con l'affresco della Maestà, datato appunto 1317 e firmato dal solo L.": in other words, it is "unanimously identified" with the Maestà. The corresponding entry in the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale also links the payment to the fresco. The 2009 Dizionario Biografico entry on Memmo, following the 1909 article, says the opposite: "criticism excludes that the document that sees father and son together can relate to this work, and still less to the lost frescoes". Two entries in the same reference work, four years apart, thus contradict each other on the most important document in Memmo's career. The contradiction needs to be stated openly, not settled by choosing the more convenient version.

Some considerations may help weigh the two readings without claiming to resolve them. In favour of identifying the payment with the Maestà is the exact coincidence of year, room and the plural "figures". Against it is the signature, which names Lippo alone. A press release issued by the Fondazione Musei Senesi on 26 October 2017 to announce the study day suggested a way to reconcile the two. It stated that the father "è probabilmente nominato soltanto perché il grande pittore… non aveva ancora raggiunto la maggiore età per ricevere la commessa". On this view Memmo would be named only because Lippo, though the real author, was still a minor. That is a hypothesis about legal capacity. It is plausible, but no document is known that establishes Lippo's age. Finally, it remains possible that the payment refers to other figures in the same hall, painted by the father-and-son workshop alongside or before the Maestà. That is the reading implied by the Dizionario Biografico's entry on Memmo.

Popular literature has gone well beyond these possibilities. It is often said, without qualification, that Memmo "assisted his son" with the Maestà or that its early phase was "a collaboration of the two", and one short encyclopaedia entry on Lippo states that the Maestà was "eseguita con il padre". As statements of fact these go beyond the evidence. What the evidence supports is narrower. A document of 1317 names Memmo and his son Lippo together for figures in the council hall. The Maestà of the same year carries Lippo's signature alone. No stylistic analysis known to me has convincingly isolated Memmo's hand within the Maestà. For the "Maestà question", the honest conclusion is that Memmo's share is possible but unproven, and that the document's reference is itself disputed.

## Panels and the vanished miniaturist

The history of Memmo as an illuminator is the clearest example of how a document can be overloaded. The 1303–1305 notices of book decoration for the podestà showed that Memmo did some work on books. In 1964 Previtali took them as the basis for attributing to him a substantial group of illuminated manuscripts of remarkable quality. These included a choirbook in the Museo dell'Opera della Metropolitana in Siena (cod. 46-2), two codices in the Biblioteca Comunale degli Intronati (F III 6 and F V 26), and the so-called "Hoepli leaves" in the Fondazione Cini in Venice. Later studies in 1977 and 1978 enlarged the group, adding further codices, including the "Homo astrologicus" leaf and choirbooks now in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo in Pisa.

The review of 1983 noted a stylistic discrepancy between the frescoes and panels on one side and the miniatures on the other. In the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale's phrasing it was above all a discrepancy of quality; the Dizionario Biografico prefers to call it stylistic, "not, one would say, qualitative". The miniatures showed an insistent Duccesque calligraphy, a graphic refinement and a technical range that were hard to reconcile with the Giottesque fresco painter of San Gimignano. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale adds a documentary argument. The office of civic painter, attested from 1303 to 1317, makes it unlikely that Memmo could have left his post for long enough to produce large illuminated programmes elsewhere. The miniaturist was therefore separated from the painter. In the major 2002 survey of Sienese illumination, the codices were redistributed among anonymous masters with homogeneous catalogues no longer assigned to Memmo. The result, in the words of the same encyclopedia, is that Memmo "takes on once again the features of a provincial painter, far from the broad cultural horizons and technical possibilities expressed by the illuminator".

The episode carries a methodological lesson. A documented fact (Memmo decorated books) was used to license an attribution (Memmo painted these manuscripts). That attribution then distorted the image of the painter, turning him for some twenty years into a major figure of Sienese illumination. Popular literature has not caught up with the correction. Online summaries still attribute to Memmo "codices in Siena (Biblioteca Comunale and Museo dell'Opera del Duomo), Venice (Fondazione Cini) and Pisa (Museo di San Matteo)", and image libraries caption a Pisan choirbook leaf as his work. These attributions should be presented as historical hypotheses now largely abandoned. What remains on panel is small: the Santa Chiara dossal as anchor, the Oristano dossal as a maintained but little-discussed attribution, and the Pisa-Altenburg polyptych as a disputed one.

## Family, workshop and the Memmi–Martini nexus

The documents allow us to speak of a family of painters across three generations: a goldsmith father, Filippuccio; a painter brother, Mino; and painter sons, Lippo and Tederigo. The 1909 article already spoke of a "school of art" at San Gimignano. The 2010 restoration press materials describe the Memmi's later activity in the town as "a sort of family monopoly", which went on, after Memmo, to include Lippo's Maestà and the New Testament cycle in the Collegiata. The Dizionario Biografico likewise speaks of a workshop "destined to be always run at the family level". All this is reasonable as a description of a social and economic structure. It is the normal pattern for medieval painters' workshops, and in San Gimignano it was reinforced by Memmo's salaried relationship with the commune.

How far the workshop's artistic output can be broken down into individual hands is much less certain. The Dizionario Biografico's entry on Lippo regards it as "very probable" that Lippo trained in his father's workshop, and notes that passages from his beginnings may survive in the Collegiata and in Sant'Agostino at San Gimignano. The phrasing is cautious, and rightly so. The case for Lippo's training with Memmo rests on the family relationship, the 1317 document and the absence of any alternative, not on a stylistic demonstration of filiation. Lippo's mature style is thoroughly Martinian. The Maestà of 1317, already modelled on Simone's of 1315, shows that by the date of his first documented work Lippo was looking beyond his father.

Simone Martini's relationship with Memmo is subtler. The Dizionario Biografico entry on Memmo says that Simone "having almost certainly spent a period of apprenticeship in Memmo's workshop, later also became his son-in-law". The "almost certainly" deserves a closer look. No document records Simone as Memmo's apprentice. The entry on Lippo, citing a monograph on Simone, speaks of a "hypothetical presence" of Simone in San Gimignano in the years when Memmo was the commune's leading painter. That allows one to sense a long-standing network of encounters "which finds, in the end, some confirmation in the marriage" of 1324. This is a reasonable reconstruction, not a fact, and it is weakened by the older and still widespread view that Simone trained with Duccio. One modern encyclopedic summary goes further and states that the idea of Simone's apprenticeship to his future father-in-law is "believed to be false". The safest formulation is that a link between Simone and the Memmi before 1324 is likely, given the marriage and the closeness of the two workshops thereafter, but that an apprenticeship of Simone with Memmo is a hypothesis.

What is documented is the marriage itself and its economic setting: the house sold to Simone for twenty florins and the donation of two hundred and twenty florins to Giovanna, both in early 1324. These transactions fit a pattern of alliance between workshops more than a romance. After 1324, documented collaboration between Simone and Lippo, in the 1326–27 Sant'Ansano commission and the 1333 Annunciation, binds the two workshops together.

The "Barna question" touches Memmo only indirectly, but his name is sometimes drawn into it. The New Testament cycle in the right aisle of the Collegiata was attributed by a fifteenth-century source, and after it by the sixteenth-century Lives, to a "Barna da Siena". As early as 1927 a Sienese archivist asked in print whether Barna had ever existed, and in 1976 it was proposed that the name was a mistranscription of "Bartolo". Current scholarship tends to give the cycle to Lippo Memmi and his workshop, possibly including Tederigo, and the Dizionario Biografico on Lippo places it in the first half of the 1330s. In the English Wikipedia article on Memmo, however, one reads that "Memmo may also have worked with Lippo Memmi on the fresco cycle of the New Testament in the Collegiate Church of San Gimignano, completed around 1345". That suggestion should be firmly rejected. Memmo is not documented after 1324 and was probably born around 1260. His participation in a cycle of the 1330s or 1340s is implausible on biographical grounds, and no stylistic argument has ever been offered for it. The same article's "fl. 1303–1345" seems to come from mistakenly extending Memmo's activity to the presumed completion date of that cycle.

## Style and position: between Duccio, Assisi and Florence

Memmo is above all a Sienese who is not a Duccesque. The Dizionario Biografico makes this central to his profile. His formation seems to have kept him "definitively" from complete adherence to Duccio's manner, and his frescoes show a Giottesque plasticity and narrative directness that Duccio's immediate followers did not share. The entry offers a suggestive, though speculative, reason. Memmo may have developed an interest in certain proto-Gothic linear patterns found in the earlier celebratory frescoes of San Gimignano, and these models, it notes, were Swabian in character, not Angevin. In the same vein, the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale describes the Giottesque influence in Memmo's fresco work as "a moment of complete adherence, free of the insistent Duccesque calligraphy" which had led scholars to give him the miniatures.

Memmo's Giottism is not that of Padua or of the mature Florentine works. His figures have weight and mass, his faces are broad and his gestures plain, but his spaces are shallow and his architecture often more ornamental than structural. Memmo took from Assisi the body and the narrative, not the box. Sienese colour softens this further. The Santa Chiara dossal's "chromatic vivacity of pure Sienese origin", in the Dizionario Biografico's words, marks Memmo's Giottism as a translation, not a conversion.

This places Memmo in an interesting historical position. In San Gimignano, a town between Florence and Siena politically and geographically, he represents a moment around 1305–1315 when a Sienese painter could still work in a predominantly Giottesque idiom. That idiom would be overtaken within a decade by the Gothic refinement of Simone Martini, which his own son and son-in-law did so much to spread. Seen this way, the shift from Memmo's Camera del Podestà to Lippo's Maestà of 1317, in the same building and perhaps within a decade, condenses a major change in Sienese painting: from the plastic narrative of Assisi to the courtly, linear and decorative idiom of Simone. Memmo is not a minor Duccio. He is the representative of a road Sienese painting partly did not take.

## Errors and their genealogy

The errors met in this essay fall into a few recurring patterns. The most consequential is the transformation of attributions into documents: the "document of 1303" at Assisi, the "commission of 1303" for the Camera del Podestà and the "documented" Collegiata work of 1305 each attach a real notice, the book decoration for the podestà, to a work it does not mention. A second pattern concerns chronology: the "fl. 1303–1345" of the English Wikipedia article, birth dates of "c. 1250" that the 1288 fine does not support, death dates of "c. 1325, Siena" that turn silence into a datum, and a short Italian entry's range of "1294–1317" that leaves out both the 1288 fine and the Sienese notices of 1321 and 1324. A third concerns authorship and place. The Angevin homage frescoes in the Sala del Consiglio, by Azzo di Masetto, appear in at least one widely used biographical note as Memmo's "Carlo d'Angiò Administering Justice (1292)". The hunting and tournament scenes that some descriptions place in the Camera del Podestà belong to the earlier Azzo cycle in the Sala di Dante. And the "Simon Memmi" confusion still surfaces in authority files. A fourth concerns collaboration: Memmo's "assistance" with the Maestà rests on a disputed document, his share in the New Testament cycle on nothing, and Simone's apprenticeship to him on a reasonable but unproven inference. Finally, in the iconography of the Camera del Podestà, the Paolo and Francesca identification, the admonitory reading of the bath and the idea of a portrayed podestà are interpretations that guidebooks present as settled facts.

## Critical fortune and conclusion

Memmo's critical fortune follows the curve typical of twentieth-century Italian connoisseurship. Before 1948 he was a name in the archives. Between 1948 and 1964, the decisive interventions of Longhi, Previtali and Carli turned him into an artistic personality with a career stretching from Assisi to Sardinia and from monumental fresco to choirbook illumination. From 1983 onward a work of subtraction began. It took away the miniatures, questioned the Pisan polyptych and left a more modest but more coherent painter at the centre of a San Gimignano catalogue. The 2009 Dizionario Biografico entry reflects this reduced but stable Memmo. The 2010 restoration of the Camera del Podestà and the 2017 study day, with its 2018 proceedings, show that interest has moved from attribution to conservation, iconography and the civic meaning of his work. The cycle's reach beyond the specialist literature is shown by "Tempo Manifesto", the exhibition of the Cuban painter Laura Carralero at Galleria Continua in San Gimignano (26 September 2026 – 10 January 2027). In works of 2026 such as "The Encounter" and "The Glitch (after Storia del Figliuol prodigo)", she reworked the bath scene and the story of the prodigal youth. She has said that "Memmo di Filippuccio's frescoes are the exhibition's main point of reference".

For the historian, the main lesson of Memmo's case is methodological. The documents establish a career, and the attributions establish an oeuvre. The link between the two rests on a convergence that is persuasive but in the end inferential. A civic painter documented in San Gimignano during exactly the years when its main civic decoration was produced, in a Giottesque idiom unusual for a Sienese, is the best candidate for its author. It is an excellent hypothesis and the corpus deserves its consensus. But it remains a hypothesis, and its strength depends on keeping the documented and the attributed clearly apart. Memmo matters not because he was a great painter, which most of the literature doubts. He matters because, for a few years in a small Tuscan town, he translated the narrative language of Assisi into the secular imagery of the commune, and because the workshop he founded linked that idiom to the Gothic refinement of Simone Martini.

The fresco is a semicircular field painted in the Collegiata of San Gimignano and published as a Madonna and Child with two saints by Memmo di Filippuccio, dated 1305. The date derives from the placement of Memmo's frescoes in the Collegiata around 1305, proposed by <a href="/scholars.html#carli-enzo">Enzo Carli</a> on stylistic grounds and supported by the coincidence with the notices of Memmo's work for the commune in the same years; no document records a payment for this painting. A catalogue title in the Fondazione Zeri photo archive describes a Virgin and Child enthroned between female saints and a donor, which suggests that the lost right half held further figures.

The exact position of the fresco within the church is not recorded with the published photograph. Memmo's paintings in the Collegiata are concentrated on the entrance wall, above the right-hand door, where the Dizionario Biografico describes, among other scenes, a fictive tabernacle with a female saint and a Madonna and Child flanked by angels, both largely repainted in the early fifteenth century. If the semicircle corresponds to that group, its present appearance would owe much to the later intervention; the identification has not been verified.

At the left stands a young female saint, seen almost frontally with her head turned slightly to the right. She wears a small pointed diadem over her fair hair, a red dress decorated with gold bands at the neck and breast, and a yellow mantle lined in green, drawn around her shoulders. Her halo is gilded and outlined in red. With the fingers of her right hand she holds a small round object at the level of her waist, perhaps a vessel or a fruit, while her left hand emerges from the mantle below.

The saint has no inscription and no unambiguous attribute. The diadem suggests a royal saint or a virgin martyr, such as Catherine of Alexandria, who appears crowned in the dossal from Santa Chiara, while the small object could be read as an ointment jar, the attribute of Mary Magdalene. In the absence of clearer evidence, the figure is best described as an unidentified female saint.

At the centre the Virgin is shown in half length or seated, the lower part of her figure merging into the losses. She wears a white veil under a grey-green mantle and looks out towards the viewer with a calm, frontal gaze. Behind her hangs a red cloth of honour with a gold border decorated with small motifs, stretched like a canopy over her head and setting her apart from the blue ground of the saint.

The Child sits on his mother's left arm, wrapped in a red cloak that leaves his body and legs partly bare, and he raises one hand towards her face. The Virgin supports him with her right hand placed under his body, and a white cloth with a gold border falls beneath him. His head is now very faint, and his features are almost illegible against the abraded halo.

The broad faces, the heavy mantles and the firm outlines of the figures correspond to the plastic manner associated with Memmo's activity in San Gimignano, and the female saint in particular can be compared with the young women of the Camera del Podestà. The frame, a red band with a border of small ornaments and, at the upper left, a roundel with green leaves on a red ground, belongs to the decorative system of the surrounding wall.

The fresco is severely damaged. The right half of the field is almost entirely lost, reduced to bare plaster with scattered traces of red, and the surface of the surviving figures is abraded, particularly in the blue ground, in the Virgin's mantle and in the body of the Child. The gold of the haloes and of the cloth of honour is worn, and the colours have faded to pale tones that make the original effect of the painting difficult to judge.

The wall belongs to the small vaulted room on the upper floor of the Torre Grossa traditionally called the Camera del Podestà, whose decoration is attributed to Memmo di Filippuccio on stylistic grounds and dated, on the evidence of the tower's completion in 1311, to about 1305–1311. The paintings fill the lunette under the vault and the zone around the window, and they are organised in two registers interrupted at the centre by the deep niche of the window, which belonged to the room's original structure.

The upper register tells the story of a youth who leaves his family and is ruined by venal love. Its reading, as summarised by the museum, runs from right to left. At the right, before a pink house with an arcade, a man in a pink gown with a white fur collar holds out a green and gold purse to a youth in red, who reaches for it, while an old woman in a white veil watches from behind: the parents give the son his share of money. The purse, clearly painted, is the object that links the episodes of the story.

At the centre three figures with long hair, dressed in red, green and pink, stand in a group, the first holding a yellow flower; they have been read as the youth's companions or as women of the courtesan's household, and the flower may allude to the lure of love. Before the entrance of the pavilion the youth embraces a woman in a dark gown, and behind his back she holds a yellow purse, presumably his own. The painter thus shows the theft within the gesture of the embrace.

The large white pavilion, with its conical roof, its border of coloured squares and its walls of vertical panels framed by bands with rosettes, dominates the register and stands for the place of seduction. At the far left the story ends in violence: the youth, in red, raises his hands to his face, while two figures drive him away, one raising a stick above her head, the other holding the yellow purse. The episode corresponds to the descriptions of the prodigal youth beaten and expelled, although the identity of the two assailants, perhaps the courtesan and the procuress, is not certain.

The lower left scene shows the exemplum of Aristotle ridden by a courtesan, watched by two youthful figures from a window; in the tale they are Alexander and a companion. The lower right scene shows a figure in a red garment standing before striped curtains and holding an open book, his head now lost, while a figure in pink leans on his shoulder from behind. The pair has often been identified as Paolo and Francesca reading the book that led them to their fall, as in Dante's Inferno V. No inscription supports the identification, which was openly questioned at the study day of 2017, and the scene is more safely described as a reader with a companion.

Taken together, the scenes of the wall form a sequence of exempla about the dangers of love outside marriage: the squandered inheritance, the philosopher made ridiculous and the reading that leads to sin. On the entrance wall, the scenes of the bride's arrival, the bath and the marriage bed present the opposite term of the contrast. Whether the programme was intended as a warning for the podestà, as many readings suggest, depends on the function of the room, which is not documented.

The narrative style relies on clear, weighty figures set against flat grounds of blue and green, with trees and buildings reduced to a few elements. The pavilion and the pink house are drawn with an attention to their materials, the canvas and the masonry, but they do not build a coherent space: the scenes follow one another along the wall like episodes of a story told aloud, each figure turned towards the next.

The wall is fragmentary. Large areas of the upper register are lost or reduced to the preparatory layer, especially at the lower centre, between the pavilion and the window, and at the upper left; the head of the reader at the lower right is lost. At the upper right, the springing of the vault preserves painted coats of arms, and at the right edge appear the women and the small dog of the adjoining wall.

The scene occupies the lower register of the window wall, to the left of the window niche. It represents one of the most widespread exempla of the Middle Ages, the humiliation of Aristotle by a woman, which circulated in Latin collections of exempla and in the French "Lai d'Aristote" of the thirteenth century. In the tale, the philosopher reproaches the young Alexander for neglecting his duties for love of a woman; she takes her revenge by seducing the old man and persuading him to let her ride on his back, while Alexander watches.

The woman is named Phyllis in most versions of the story. The Dizionario Biografico, describing the scene, calls her Campaspe, the name of Alexander's mistress that entered some later versions of the tale; the two names refer to the same exemplum. Her identity in this fresco, without an inscription, rests on the iconography alone, which corresponds to the common type.

Aristotle crawls on hands and knees across a green meadow sown with small plants, from left to right. He wears a long blue-grey robe and a red cap with a white band, and his bearded face, turned to the viewer, has the heavy, lined features of an old man. His hands rest flat on the ground in front of him, and his whole body is drawn as a beast of burden, the posture that the exemplum makes the sign of his humiliation.

The courtesan sits sideways on his back in a long red gown with green sleeves, her legs hanging over his flank. With one hand she holds his belt as a rider holds the reins; with the other she raises above her head a whip with long white thongs that stream against the dark blue ground. Her face, framed by fair hair, is calm and turned slightly aside, without any expression of triumph.

At the left, in the window of a red building, two youthful figures with fair bobbed hair and collars bordered with gold look out at the scene. One raises an open hand in a gesture of surprise or address, the other turns his eyes towards the viewer. They are identified as Alexander and a companion, as in the tale, where the king watches his tutor fall into the trap; the identification of the second figure with Hephaestion has been proposed but rests on no inscription.

In a programme painted in a civic building, the exemplum carries a precise meaning. Aristotle was for the Middle Ages the master of knowledge, and his humiliation showed that no wisdom was safe from the power of desire. Placed beside the story of the youth who squanders his inheritance, the scene extends the warning from youth to old age and from money to reason, and it has been read as a lesson addressed to those who governed the commune.

The figures are among the best preserved of the room. The modelling of the faces, with greenish shadows and pink cheeks, and the solid, rounded volumes of the bodies show the plastic manner that the literature connects with Memmo's experience of the Giottesque workshop at Assisi. The space, by contrast, is reduced to a strip of meadow and a dark ground, against which the figures stand out like actors on a narrow stage.

The surface is abraded in the dark blue ground and in the robe of Aristotle, where the blue has darkened in patches, and there are small losses and cracks throughout. Below the scene runs a painted frame of red, green and yellow bands, and beneath it begins the frieze of flowers that crowns the dado of the room.

The band of frescoes runs above the entrance of the Camera del Podestà, where the curve of the door arch cuts into the lower edge of the central scene. It shows the conjugal sequence that the museum describes as marriage, the "cradle of love", in three episodes read from left to right: the bride led to the groom's house, the spouses bathing together, and the spouses retiring to bed. Painted coats of arms of later podestà, one with an inscription, were added on the plain wall below.

In the first scene, at the left, the bride stands under an arcade of pink buildings in a long white gown, beside a companion in a red dress and a dark mantle. A small white dog walks across the flowered ground below them, a common sign of fidelity in marriage imagery, although its meaning in this scene is not documented. To the right, two men in green and pink gowns stand close together in conversation, and in a doorway a man in a red gown and cap turns towards a woman in a dark mantle and white veil, who raises her hands in a gesture of welcome or speech. The scene is read as the arrival of the bride at the groom's house; the identity of the figures, perhaps the groom and his mother or the families of the spouses, is not established.

In the central scene, under a canopy of curtains striped in red, green and yellow, the young husband and wife sit facing each other in a large wooden tub bound with pale hoops. Both are naked and wear close-fitting caps; she rests one hand on his shoulder, and they look at each other calmly. Some descriptions connect the scene with the immoral associations of public bathhouses and count it among the admonitory scenes. Its place in the conjugal sequence, which every account treats as the positive side of the programme, makes a reading as a scene of legitimate intimacy between husband and wife more consistent with the arrangement of the room.

In the scene at the right, a maid in a red and dark blue dress draws back a heavy yellow curtain, opening the space of the room to the viewer. The husband, in a cap and naked to the waist, lifts the white sheet as he climbs into the bed, and the wife lies inside it, her head on a red pillow and one breast uncovered, one arm stretched out above the coverlet.

The bed is painted with particular care. A coverlet in a chequer of green and yellow squares covers it almost entirely, its pattern foreshortened to suggest the depth of the mattress, and the head of the bed rises at the right against hangings striped in red and yellow. The attention to fabrics, furniture and domestic arrangements, from the hoops of the tub to the folds of the sheet, is one of the qualities for which the cycle is valued as a document of daily life around 1300.

The sequence presents marriage as an ordered progression, from the public ceremony of the bride's arrival to the private intimacy of the bath and the bed. Facing the scenes of the window wall, where love outside marriage leads to theft, violence and humiliation, it offers the positive term of a moral contrast. The conjugal scenes may also have narrated the life of a real couple, perhaps a podestà and his wife, a possibility recorded cautiously in the literature but not supported by any document.

The figures share the plastic weight of the window wall. The bodies are modelled in light and shade, the faces are broad and regular, and the gestures are simple and legible. The space remains shallow and stage-like: the arcades, the tub and the bed are pushed close to the picture plane, and the curtains close the scenes behind the figures instead of opening a measured interior.

The band is well preserved in comparison with the window wall. The white gowns of the bride and her companion are abraded, the lower part of the first scene below the arcade is partly lost, and the dark grounds show wear and small losses. The flesh of the spouses, the striped hangings and the chequered coverlet keep much of their colour, recovered in the restorations of the twentieth century and in the campaign begun in 2010.

### Dossal from the Convent of Santa Chiara (San Gimignano, Pinacoteca)

  <img src="/Images/Memmo-di-Filippuccio/memmo-di-filippuccio-04.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Memmo-di-Filippuccio/memmo-di-filippuccio-04-zoom.jpg" alt="Wide rectangular gold-ground panel divided into a tall arched central compartment and four vertical boards, two at each side, each with two arched compartments one above the other. The dossal comes from the convent of Santa Chiara in San Gimignano, a community of Poor Clares founded outside the walls around 1261 on the initiative of a prominent townsman, Ildebrandino di Angiolello. From the beginning the spiritual care of the nuns was entrusted to the Franciscan friars of the town, whose convent stood nearby. The painting is now in the Pinacoteca of the Musei Civici in the Palazzo Comunale, where it is the only panel painting by Memmo di Filippuccio in the town and the most secure in his catalogue.

The community kept its function as an enclosed convent until the reforms of the Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-pietro-leopoldo-asburgo-lorena" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>, who in 1786 reduced it to a conservatory for girls. Other works from Santa Chiara, such as a reliquary cross now in the Museo di Arte Sacra, show that its furnishings were later divided between the civic and the diocesan collections of the town; the circumstances in which the dossal entered the Pinacoteca have not been traced.

The panel measures about 120 by 217 centimetres and is painted in tempera and gold on wood. It has the form of a dossal, a low rectangular altarpiece that stood on the back of the altar table, a type widespread in Tuscany in the second half of the thirteenth century and still in use in the first decades of the fourteenth, before the spread of the polyptych with separate compartments and Gothic pinnacles.

The structure is articulated in nine compartments. At the centre a taller board with a round arch contains the enthroned Virgin and Child; at each side two vertical boards carry two arched compartments one above the other, with a half-length saint in each. The central arch rises above the level of the lateral boards, so that the outline of the dossal steps up towards the middle. The arches are modelled in relief and gilded, and the spandrels are filled with painted ornament; two gilded rosettes mark the upper corners of the central compartment.

The attribution to Memmo goes back to the reconstruction of his San Gimignano activity in the early 1960s, when Enzo Carli grouped around the documented civic painter the frescoes of the Collegiata, of San Jacopo al Tempio and of the Camera del Podestà. The dossal, whose style agrees with those frescoes, became the anchor of Memmo's work on panel. Its attribution is generally accepted, in contrast with the Pisan polyptych and the Oristano dossal, which remain disputed.

The date is not documented, and the proposals vary within a narrow range. The Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale places the dossal around 1310; the inventory of the Musei Civici gives about 1310–1317, the span of Memmo's last years as civic painter; other descriptions propose about 1305–1310 or simply the second decade of the fourteenth century. A date in the years around 1310, when Memmo was working on the Camera del Podestà, is consistent with the style of the figures.

The Dizionario Biografico observes that in the dossal Memmo shows, "precisely in the study of the plasticity of the figures, a return of updated Giottesque language", to which he adds "a chromatic vivacity of pure Sienese origin". The saints have broad, solid heads, firm necks and heavy draperies modelled in light and shade, close to the figures of the Camera del Podestà, while the gold grounds, the patterned cloths and the bright reds and greens belong to the tradition of Sienese panel painting.

The upper register of the lateral compartments presents four figures of the universal Church. At the left are Saint Peter, vested with a pallium marked with black crosses and holding the keys and a red book, and Saint John the Evangelist, young and beardless, with an open book. At the right are Saint John the Baptist, in a red mantle over his garment of skins, holding a cross-staff and a scroll inscribed "Ego vox clamantis in deserto parate viam Domini" (John 1:23), and the Archangel Michael, winged, holding a lance and an orb.

The lower register gathers saints chosen for the nuns. At the left are two virgin martyrs, Catherine of Alexandria, crowned as a princess, and Agnes, holding the lamb with a banner that alludes to her name and to Christ. At the right are the founders of the Franciscan family, Saint Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a>, showing the wound of the stigmata on his raised hand and holding a red book, and Saint Clare<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-chiara-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a>, in the black veil and habit of her order, raising her hand.

The selection of saints reflects the community for which the dossal was made. Catherine and Agnes offered the Poor Clares models of consecrated virginity, while Francis and Clare represented the rule they followed and the order that guided them. The Baptist and the Evangelist, the two Johns who stood closest to Christ, Peter, the head of the Church, and Michael, the guardian of souls, frame these figures within a broader order of intercession.

Each saint is named by an inscription painted in the gold ground on either side of the head and divided by the halo, as in "S. Petrus", "Sca Agnes" or "Sca Clara". The inscriptions, in Gothic capitals with abbreviations, make the programme legible and confirm the identifications.

At the feet of the Virgin, at the lower right of the central compartment, kneels a small figure of a nun in the habit of the Poor Clares, her hands raised in prayer. She is the donor, presumably a member of the community who paid for the painting or on whose behalf it was commissioned. Her name is not recorded, and no document of the commission is known.

Nun donors appear in other Tuscan altarpieces painted for female communities in these decades, where individual nuns could use resources of their own or of their families to furnish the altars of their churches. The small scale of the figure, kneeling at the edge of the throne, expresses her humility before the Virgin while recording her role in the making of the image.

The dossal shows Memmo working within the conventions of the Sienese altarpiece of the Duccesque generation, with its gold ground, its half-length saints and its enthroned Madonna, but giving them the weight and volume he had learned from the Giottesque painting of Assisi. In this respect it stands between the dossals of the late thirteenth century and the polyptychs that Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi would paint for Pisa and San Gimignano in the following decades.

The panel is in fair condition. The dark mantle of the Virgin has lost much of its original colour, probably a blue that has darkened, and the surface shows abrasions and small losses throughout, especially in the lower compartments and along the joins of the boards. The gold grounds are worn in places, revealing the red bole beneath, and the original outer frame, apart from the arches, is lost.

The central compartment, taller than the lateral boards and closed by a round arch, contains the Virgin enthroned with the Child, the devotional focus of the dossal. The Virgin is shown almost full length and on a larger scale than the saints, so that she dominates the whole altarpiece; the small donor at her feet measures the distance between the heavenly queen and the nun who prays to her.

The Virgin sits frontally, her body turned slightly to the left and her head inclined towards the Child. She wears a dark mantle drawn over her head and wrapped around her body, under which the red of her dress appears at the cuffs; a green lining is spread over her knees. Her face is long and regular, with a straight nose, a small mouth and almond eyes looking out towards the viewer, and its modelling, with greenish shadows and pale flesh, follows the Sienese practice of painting over a green underlayer.

The Child sits on his mother's left knee, wearing a pale violet tunic and a gold-coloured garment that covers his legs, with a white cloth with an embroidered border spread behind him. His halo is marked with a cross decorated with lozenges. With one hand he reaches to the edge of his mother's mantle at her neck, a gesture of familiarity drawn from Byzantine and Sienese types of the tender Virgin, while she supports him with her hand on his body. He looks out of the picture, so that the intimacy of the gesture is joined to the frontal address of the image.

The throne is covered with a cloth of honour patterned with gold quatrefoils and small crosses on a red ground, which rises behind the Virgin to the level of her shoulders and fills the space within the arch. Below, a narrow patterned carpet and a painted marble step with inset panels form the base of the throne. The cloth and the step give the compartment a sense of depth, while the gold ground above closes the space.

At the lower right, at the foot of the throne, kneels the donor, a nun in the brown habit and dark veil of the Poor Clares, her hands raised in prayer and her face turned up towards the Virgin. Her small size, a conventional sign of the distance between the earthly suppliant and the sacred figure, does not prevent the painter from giving her a clear profile and a careful description of her habit.

The relationship between the Virgin, the Child and the donor follows a scheme common in Tuscan devotional painting around 1300, in which the patron appears within the sacred image as a permanent supplicant. In a convent of enclosed nuns, where the dossal stood on the altar of their church, the image of one of them kneeling before the Virgin also presented the whole community in prayer.

The figure of the Virgin shows the qualities that the literature recognises in Memmo's panel painting: the solid volume of the body under the mantle, the weight of the folds falling over the knees, and the firm placement of the figure on the throne. These qualities connect the compartment with the Giottesque experience of Assisi, while the gold ground, the patterned cloth and the colour remain those of Sienese painting.

The compartment is the most damaged part of the dossal in its colour. The mantle of the Virgin has darkened almost to black, losing the modelling of its folds, and the green lining shows abrasions. The faces, the hands, the Child's garments and the cloth of honour are better preserved, and the gilded rosettes in the upper corners and the arch survive from the original structure.

  <figcaption>Attributed to Memmo di Filippuccio, <em>Virgin and Child with Six Saints and Angels</em>, late 13th or early 14th century. Tempera and gold on panel. Oristano, Palazzo Arcivescovile. Palazzo Arcivescovile, Oristano, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>

The dossal is preserved in the archbishop's palace at Oristano, in western Sardinia, the seat of the archdiocese of Arborea. It was long regarded as a Pisan work, a natural assumption for a painting in a region whose churches drew much of their art from Pisa in the thirteenth century. In 1962 <a href="/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni">Giovanni Previtali</a> attributed it to Memmo di Filippuccio on the basis of its plastic construction and its kinship with his San Gimignano works, and the attribution, though little discussed, has been retained in the Dizionario Biografico and in recent Sardinian studies of Trecento painting on the island.

Previtali also proposed a patron who would explain how a panel by a painter of San Gimignano reached Sardinia: Scolaio Ardinghelli, the San Gimignano churchman named in the inscription of 1292 in the Sala del Consiglio of the town hall, who later became archbishop of Arborea and died there in 1300. The hypothesis would place the dossal in the 1290s and Memmo in a San Gimignano network before his first documented work for the commune in 1303. It remains unproven, since no document links Ardinghelli to the painting, and the Dizionario Biografico notes that critics have not seriously tackled the question.

The panel is a long, low dossal with a shallow gabled top, a format used for altarpieces in Tuscany and in areas under Pisan influence in the late thirteenth century. Its field is divided into seven round-arched compartments separated by gilded colonnettes with capitals and bases, the central arch taller and wider than the others. In the spandrels between the arches small half-length angels with gold haloes, in red and green robes, look towards the centre against a blue-green ground.

At the centre the Virgin, in half length, wears a dark blue mantle lined with red, drawn over her head, and inclines her head towards the Child. He sits on her left arm in a red tunic and reaches with one hand towards the neckline of her mantle, while she raises her right hand towards him. The gesture of the Child, close to that of the dossal from Santa Chiara in San Gimignano, belongs to the tender type of the Virgin that Sienese painters adapted from Byzantine models.

The three saints at the left are an old bearded monk in a dark habit and hood, holding a staff, who can be identified as Saint Anthony Abbot; a female saint in a bright red mantle drawn over her head, raising her right hand, perhaps Mary Magdalene; and a young beardless saint in a dark tunic and red mantle, holding a book and raising his hand, probably Saint John the Evangelist. No inscriptions are legible in the compartments, and the identifications rest on the usual types.

At the right are a female saint with braided hair in a dark green dress, holding a small red cross and a red object, perhaps a martyr; a young saint with long hair in a red tunic decorated with embroidered bands, whose attribute is unclear; and Saint Francis in the brown habit of his order, holding a red book. The presence of Francis suggests a connection with the Franciscan milieu, but the original destination of the dossal is not known.

The style of the figures supports the comparison with Memmo's San Gimignano works. The broad heads, heavy eyelids and firm modelling of the faces, the solid shoulders and the simple, weighty folds of the mantles correspond to the plastic manner of the dossal from Santa Chiara, while the colour, with its bright reds and greens set against the gold, belongs to the Sienese tradition. The more archaic structure of the dossal, with its row of arches under a single gable, is consistent with a date in the last years of the thirteenth century.

The dossal is in fragile condition. The lower part of every compartment is lost along an irregular line, so that the figures are cut off below the waist and the bare preparation of the panel is visible; the gold ground is worn and stained, and the frame and the gable show losses and cracks. The upper part of the figures, the faces and the angels in the spandrels are better preserved, and they allow the stylistic comparison on which the attribution rests.

The panel in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo in Pisa shows the Virgin in half length holding the Child, who plays with a goldfinch. It is listed among Memmo di Filippuccio's works in several surveys of his catalogue and is dated in the first two decades of the fourteenth century. Its attribution belongs to the problematic Pisan phase of Memmo's career, which rests on stylistic comparisons rather than on documents.

The Pisan group was assembled in 1962, when a Madonna and Child in Pisa and a lateral saint in the Lindenau-Museum in Altenburg were recognised as parts of a single polyptych and attributed to Memmo. Later Pisan studies and the 2008 catalogue of the Altenburg collection moved both works, with caution, to the anonymous Master of San Torpè, while other reference works keep Memmo's name. Whether this panel is the Madonna of that reconstructed polyptych is not certain; the Goldfinch Madonna of San Matteo is in any case excluded from the Master of San Torpè's catalogue in at least one recent list of his works, and its attribution remains open.

The Virgin is shown frontally, with her head inclined to the left and her eyes turned towards the viewer. She wears a dark blue mantle drawn over her head and bordered with a band of gold, a dark brown veil that falls around her face and neck, and a red dress with gold bands at the collar and cuffs. Her face, broad and regular, has a long straight nose, a small red mouth and pink cheeks, and its modelling is soft and continuous.

The Child sits on his mother's left arm, upright and turned towards the viewer. He has a round face, large dark eyes and thick golden curls, painted with an unusual attention to their volume, and he wears a bright pink-red tunic with gold collar and cuffs, from which his bare feet emerge. In one hand he holds a goldfinch by the body, and in the other the cord tied to the bird, a detail that makes the play of a child with a captive bird explicit.

The goldfinch is a frequent attribute of the Child in Italian painting of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Because the bird feeds among thistles and thorns, and because of the red spot on its head, it was associated with the crown of thorns and the blood of the Passion; it could also allude to the soul. The Virgin raises her right hand towards the bird and the Child's feet, a gesture that may express both tenderness and foreknowledge of his sacrifice.

The broad, solid figures, the full face of the Virgin and the robust body of the Child, together with the soft modelling and the warm colour, have been compared with the San Gimignano works attributed to Memmo, in particular the dossal from Santa Chiara. The rich tooling of the gold ground, with foliate patterns incised around the figures and in the haloes, reflects the techniques of Tuscan panel painting of the early fourteenth century.

The panel has been cut down. Its original top, probably pointed or gabled, has been trimmed at the sides, and the gold ground and the figures stop at an irregular edge, beyond which the bare preparation of the wood is visible. The panel may originally have formed the central element of a larger altarpiece, as the reconstruction of 1962 proposed for the Pisan Madonna, but this cannot be confirmed from its present state.

The paint surface is in fair condition, with a fine network of cracks across the whole panel. The blue of the mantle is abraded and has darkened in places, and the gold ground is worn along the edges, revealing the red bole beneath. The faces, the hands, the Child's tunic and the goldfinch are well preserved; two metal clamps hold the panel in its museum mounting.

The fresco fills a shallow gabled niche in the church of Santi Jacopo e Filippo in Certaldo Alto, the upper town of Certaldo in the Valdelsa, about fifteen kilometres north of San Gimignano. It shows the Virgin and Child enthroned between two apostles, with a kneeling woman presented by the apostle at the left. The niche, built into the brick wall, has a pointed gable, and its sides and soffit are painted with fictive marbled panels and a patterned band, so that the image appears as a small painted chapel or tabernacle within the church.

The attribution to Memmo di Filippuccio appears in popular summaries of his catalogue, which date the fresco simply to the fourteenth century. The principal reference works on Memmo do not appear to mention a work at Certaldo, and the attribution should be treated with reserve. No document of the commission is known, and the identity of the donor is not recorded.

At the centre the Virgin sits on a throne built as a white Gothic structure, with slender pinnacles, gables pierced by oculi and trefoil arches, and a high back covered by a red cloth of honour. She wears a dark mantle over a white veil, and her face, long and grave, is inclined slightly towards the Child. With her right hand she supports him at the waist, and with her left she supports his leg, while her mantle spreads in broad dark folds over her knees down to the step of the throne.

The Child sits upright on his mother's left knee, wearing a green tunic and a red mantle wrapped around his legs. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds a closed book in his left, and his halo is marked with the arms of a cross. His frontal pose and the gesture of blessing present him as the teacher and judge, in contrast with the more playful types of the Child in contemporary Sienese painting.

At the left stands an apostle with long brown hair and a full beard, in a green tunic and a pink mantle, holding a book. He lays his right hand on the head of a kneeling young woman, who joins her hands in prayer and looks up towards the Virgin. As the church is dedicated to Saints James and Philip, the apostle may be James the Greater, the titular saint, acting as the donor's protector; without an inscription or a distinctive attribute the identification remains a hypothesis.

The donor wears a gold dress and a dark green mantle dotted with small white motifs, and her fair hair is gathered at the back of her head. She is shown on a smaller scale than the saints but larger than many donors of the period, and her rich dress suggests a woman of some standing in the town. The gesture of the apostle's hand on her head, a sign of protection and presentation, links her directly to the sacred group.

At the right an elderly apostle with short grey curly hair and a short beard, in a pale blue tunic and an ochre mantle, holds keys and a book and looks up towards the Virgin. The keys identify him as Saint Peter. His upward gaze and the turn of his body balance the composition with the group of the apostle and the donor on the other side.

The Gothic architecture of the throne, with its pinnacles and pierced gables, and the elongated, refined proportions of the figures are closer to Sienese painting of the 1320s and 1330s than to the San Gimignano works securely associated with Memmo, which raises doubts about the attribution. The fresco is in fair condition: the Virgin's mantle has darkened and lost its modelling, the background of the throne is stained and abraded, and the colours of the saints and the donor are better preserved.

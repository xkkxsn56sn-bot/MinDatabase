## Introduction

*Niccolò di Buonaccorso* holds an unusual place in the history of Sienese painting. The museums that own his works rank him among the most accomplished Sienese painters of the second half of the fourteenth century, yet only one surviving work bears his signature, no catalogue raisonné exists, and the older literature often confused his work with that of his contemporaries, above all Bartolo di Fredi. His art is known almost entirely through small devotional panels, painted with a minute technique, a dense use of gold and a lively attention to objects, textiles and domestic settings.

His documented life is short and almost entirely civic. The documents record his offices in the government of the city, a single cathedral commission and his burial. Around these few fixed points stand a signed group of Marian panels, a dismembered polyptych and a body of attributed works of uneven security, together with a workshop group and a series of rejected proposals.

His art belongs to post-plague Siena, between the legacy of Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi, the spatial lessons of Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti, and the workshops of Luca di Tommè, Bartolo di Fredi and Paolo di Giovanni Fei. Three modern scholars have shaped what is known of him: the archivist <a href="/scholars.html#milanesi-gaetano">Gaetano Milanesi</a>, whose *Documenti per la storia dell'arte senese* (1854) first gave the painter a documentary identity; <a href="/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos">Miklós Boskovits</a>, whose article of 1980 in *Paragone* brought him back to critical attention; and <a href="/scholars.html#palladino-pia">Pia Palladino</a>, whose catalogue of the Timken Museum exhibition of 1997–1998 remains the fullest re-examination of his career.

## The Documentary Record

No document records Niccolò's birth. The date "c. 1348" found in some museum records is a modern estimate based on his parentage. The biographical entry in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (vol. 78, 2013) states that his date of birth is unknown and identifies him as the son of Buonaccorso di Pace, a painter and architect documented from 1347, who died in 1363 and to whom no works can be attributed. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* (1997) was more cautious and called Niccolò only "perhaps" his son. The same 2013 entry notes that a Paolo di Buonaccorso di Pace, recorded in 1374 as an associate of Luca di Tommè, was probably Niccolò's brother. If so, the family was linked to one of the busiest Sienese workshops of the 1370s, but the relationship remains a probability. All the documents concern Siena, and he signed himself *de Senis*.

His entry into the painters' guild has often been dated to 1355, a date that confuses the statutes with the list of members. The *Breve dell'Arte de' Pittori Senesi*, kept in the Archivio di Stato in Siena and published by Milanesi, collects statutes approved on 19 February 1355 in Sienese reckoning, that is 1356 in modern style, since the Sienese year began on 25 March. Niccolò's name appears in the first list of painters appended to the statutes, in thirteenth place. That list, however, must date from between 1378 and 1386: it omits Bartolomeo Bulgarini, who died in 1378, and includes Biagio di Goro Ghezzi, who died in 1386. Earlier lists were probably lost, and Niccolò is already described as a painter in the early 1370s. The list therefore documents his membership in those years, not the beginning of his career.

The documents show him holding office in the government that ruled Siena after 1368. He was one of the priors of his terzo in May–June 1372 and again in March–April 1377 in modern style, a term that appears as 1376 when dated in Sienese style. In 1375 he sat on the committee to reform the *bussolo*, the bag from which the names of the priors were drawn, and in 1381 he was gonfaloniere of his terzo. The terzo itself is reported differently. The 1997 entry, following Milanesi's notes, places him in the Terzo di San Martino, while the 2013 entry places all his offices in the Terzo di Camollia, "the area of Siena where he lived". The later account appears to rest on fresh archival work, but it cites no document for the terzo, and the question remains open.

Only one cathedral commission is documented, and the work is lost. In 1380 the Opera del Duomo commissioned from Niccolò a panel with predella showing Saint Daniel, for the altar of the saint in the cathedral. A note in the camerlengo's book published by Milanesi under 1383 gave rise to a later date for the commission, but recent research on the furnishings of the cathedral regards that note as an administrative error corrected by another hand, and the commission belongs to 1380. A tradition that in 1376 Niccolò painted the *cappello*, presumably a canopy, over the high altar of the cathedral is not mentioned by either encyclopaedia entry and remains unconfirmed.

The necrology of San Domenico in Camporegio, published in 1937, records his burial in the cloister of the church on 17 May 1388. No separate date of death is recorded, and the year 1388 rests on the burial record. Burial at the Dominican<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> house of Camporegio may reflect his devotional allegiances, but it cannot be linked to any Dominican commission.

## The Signed Marian Group: London, Florence, New York

The anchor of the oeuvre is the *Marriage of the Virgin* in the National Gallery in London (NG 1109), whose original frame carries the painter's name and city in gold capitals. The Gallery's catalogue of 2011, by <a href="/scholars.html#gordon-dillian-rosalind">Dillian Gordon</a>, calls it "the small signed painting by Niccolò di Buonaccorso", while the Gallery's artist page speaks of two signed works, the second being the polyptych of 1387. Both statements are correct once the categories are separated: one signature survives, while a second, dated signature was transcribed in the nineteenth century and is now lost.

The National Gallery, the Metropolitan Museum and the Uffizi agree that two panels of identical size and framing belong with the London picture: the *Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple* in the Uffizi and the *Coronation of the Virgin* in the Robert Lehman Collection in New York. The Uffizi panel, which came from the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova in Florence, was linked with the London picture in the Italian edition (1885) of a standard nineteenth-century history of Italian painting, and an article of 1914 on unpublished Sienese paintings added the Lehman *Coronation*. The three panels share "the same measurements, identical framing and an analogous moment of style", very probably the painter's maturity.

Calling the group a triptych assumes too much. The backs of all three panels are decorated with the same pattern, silvered, punched and painted with blue and red lozenges, which suggests that the backs were meant to be seen. Yet it is not clear how, or whether, the panels were joined, and the National Gallery observes that no evidence of hinges remains. A catalogue of the Florentine galleries of 1965 noted a metal boss on one edge of the Uffizi panel and proposed that the panels served as shutters, with further panels completing a cycle of the Life of the Virgin, a reading that fits the gaps in the narrative, where the Annunciation, the Nativity and the Dormition are missing. The London catalogue instead treats the signed *Marriage* as the central panel. The two readings are incompatible, and the question remains open.

The National Gallery has suggested that the group was painted for a Florentine patron, since the Marriage of the Virgin was a more popular subject in Florence than in Siena, and a painter working at home would hardly have needed to add *de Senis* to his name. The Metropolitan adds that the panels may have been produced for the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova in Florence. Both points are hypotheses: a nineteenth-century location is not evidence of a fourteenth-century commission, and painters used place-name signatures in their own cities too. The date of about 1380 given by all three museums is a stylistic estimate, and the 2013 entry places the group between the late 1370s and the early 1380s.

## The Polyptych of 1387

In 1854 Milanesi recorded in the church of Santa Margherita at Costalpino, just outside Siena, a Madonna and Child with the inscription "Nicholaus Bonachursi me pinxit A(nno) D(omi)ni 1387". The inscription is now lost, and the only date in Niccolò's stylistic chronology rests on a single nineteenth-century reading, generally accepted but not verifiable. The central panel and one lateral panel were removed from the church before 1862. The Madonna disappeared for almost a century and reappeared at a sale in 1953; Boskovits identified it with the Madonna then in the Kisters collection at Kreuzlingen, and the Putnam Foundation acquired it in 1998 for the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego.

The lateral panel is a full-length *Saint Lawrence*. In the sixteenth century it was overpainted as Saint Margaret, the titular saint of Costalpino: the overpainter kept the pose, the book, the cross and even the gridiron, but covered a group of kneeling patrons with Margaret's dragon. The panel was moved to Sant'Andrea a Montecchio and forgotten. After the Madonna reappeared, the saint was identified by its frame and cleaned, and the cleaning revealed Niccolò's handling of *sgraffito* on the gold of the dalmatic. The panel is now in the Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra of Siena, in the Oratorio di San Bernardino; the museum was housed temporarily in the Pinacoteca Nazionale between 1995 and 1999, which explains why the panel has also been recorded there.

Among the patrons revealed by the cleaning is a woman in a dark, possibly Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> habit, perhaps a widow with her children. Palladino proposed that the polyptych was made for Santa Maria Maddalena, a convent of Augustinian nuns outside Porta Tufi founded in 1339 and destroyed in 1526 during the fighting at Camollia. On this reading the panels were moved to Costalpino afterwards, and the saint was repainted as Margaret to suit his new church. The reconstruction is economical, but no contract or inventory connects the work with Santa Maria Maddalena. The original structure has been compared with that of Luca di Tommè's polyptych from San Pietro a Venano, now on deposit in the Pinacoteca Nazionale, a comparison of type only.

Boskovits proposed that a *Crucifixion* in the Szépművészeti Múzeum in Budapest and a *Presentation of the Virgin* formerly in the von Kaulbach collection in Munich came from the predella. Two further pieces have been linked to the polyptych: a head of the Virgin in the Städel Museum in Frankfurt and a *Saint Lawrence* in Kelvingrove Art Gallery in Glasgow. The museums' own records complicate both connections. The Städel dates its fragment about 1377–1383, and its halo carries traces of an inscription read as "AVE GRATIA PLENA", which better suits an Annunciate Virgin, perhaps from a pinnacle, than an enthroned Madonna. Glasgow dates its *Saint Lawrence* about 1370–1375, and its size suggests a smaller complex. Neither can be considered securely part of the Costalpino altarpiece.

The rediscovery of the polyptych matters beyond its reconstruction. Specialists had long debated whether the painter of so many small panels was capable of working successfully on a large scale. The Madonna in San Diego, more than one and a half metres high, and the cleaned *Saint Lawrence* show that he was, and that the small scale of most of his surviving works reflects what has survived rather than a narrow specialism. The lost panel of Saint Daniel for the cathedral points in the same direction.

## The Attributed Oeuvre

The corpus remains unstable. A survey of the Italian schools of 1924 by <a href="/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond">Raimond van Marle</a> listed fourteen works, half of which are now given to contemporaries, and the last edition of the lists of <a href="/scholars.html#berenson-bernard">Bernard Berenson</a> (1968) counted sixteen, five of them queried. The 1980s added important works but also doubtful ones, and Palladino's catalogue of 1997 re-examined the whole career. The works accepted both by the 2013 synthesis and by the museums that own them form the core of the catalogue.

Most of Niccolò's surviving output consists of central panels of portable triptychs and small devotional images. The *Virgin and Child Enthroned with a Bishop Saint, Saint John the Baptist and Four Angels* in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, bought in Gubbio in the nineteenth century, is probably the centre of a portable triptych whose wings are missing. The *Virgin of Humility* in the Louvre, given with retention of usufruct in 1976 and entered in 2011, shows the Virgin seated on the floor of a room, with a workbasket, a piece of needlework and a prayer book on the bench behind her, details that suggest a patron among devout laywomen. The triptych in the Timken Museum, with the Madonna of Humility between Saints Catherine of Alexandria and Christopher and the Annunciation and the Crucifixion above, closes like a book and has painted decoration on its exterior; it is dated to the early or middle 1370s.

The 2013 synthesis accepts several further small works: two small panels in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Siena, a *Nursing Virgin Enthroned with Six Saints* (no. 121), attributed to Niccolò as early as 1914, and a second panel (no. 222); a *Madonna Enthroned with Saints* formerly in the Loeser collection; a *Madonna between Saints Anthony Abbot and Catherine* that hung as a street tabernacle on a Sienese palace until 1984 and whose present location is unknown; a *Madonna of Humility* triptych in Bloomington, Indiana, published in 1977 and perhaps earlier than the Timken triptych; the *Virgin and Child Enthroned with Two Angels and Six Saints* in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin; and the small triptych in the National Gallery in Prague, with the Virgin and Child between Saints John the Baptist and Catherine in the centre and Saints Anthony Abbot and Christopher in the wings.

The diptych from Santa Maria la Nova at Cellino Attanasio, now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo in L'Aquila, pairs the Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine with the Crucifixion; it was attributed in 1938–1939 and is placed around 1380. A small *Crucifixion* in the Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria in Perugia, with the mourning Virgin and Saint John seated on the ground and a cardinal saint, perhaps Bonaventure<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-bonaventure" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> or Jerome<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-jerome" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, embracing the foot of the cross, belongs to the last decade of his activity. The *grisaille* wings in the Alte Pinakothek in Munich, with crowds of saints, show a range beyond colour and gold. The gabled panel with *Scenes from the New Testament* in the Pinacoteca of Siena (no. 163), given to Cristoforo di Bindoccio in the Pinacoteca catalogue of 1933, was returned to Niccolò in 1978 and may be an early work, with a collaborator's hand in the Crucifixion.

The 2013 synthesis also identifies "a subgroup of works with Niccolò's stylistic characteristics but of lesser execution". It comprises the portable triptych of the Salini Collection at Asciano; the small *Maestà* in the Pinacoteca of Siena (no. 141); the diptych divided between the Wadsworth Atheneum in Hartford (*Annunciation*) and the Metropolitan Museum (*Lamentation*); a *Crucifixion* formerly in the Toscanelli collection; and a *Crucifixion* formerly in Cologne. The Metropolitan catalogues its *Lamentation* as the work of the workshop, about 1380, while the Wadsworth keeps the *Annunciation* under Niccolò's name, dated 1370–1388, and describes its medium as tempera and oil, a combination that would be exceptional in a Sienese panel of this date and needs technical confirmation.

Several proposals have been rejected. The *Annunciation* diptych in the Museo Bandini at Fiesole, attributed to Niccolò in 1918, was reassigned in 1972 to Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio; the photographic archive of <a href="/scholars.html#zeri-federico">Federico Zeri</a> still files it under Niccolò, with a note proposing Naddo Ceccarelli, a reminder that such archives preserve the history of attributions rather than their current state. The *Saint John the Evangelist* fragment in the Feigen collection, attributed to Niccolò in 2010 and earlier to Bartolo di Fredi, has been given to Francesco di Vannuccio, and a *Crucifixion* published in 2011 has been given to the young Paolo di Giovanni Fei. A tabernacle in the Vatican Museums, formerly attributed to Niccolò, is now catalogued under the fifteenth-century Master of the Brancacci Triptych. Boskovits's article of 1980, which treated Niccolò together with Benedetto di Bindo and the painting of early fifteenth-century Siena, already pointed to the long afterlife of his types.

## Biccherna, Civic Imagery and the Question of Illumination

The most interesting of the uncertain attributions is a Biccherna<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-biccherna" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> tablet in the Archivio di Stato of Siena (no. 9), datable to 1385. Boskovits discussed it in an article of 1985 on painting "in miniature", while an article of 2008 on the Master of the Richardson Tabernacle gave it to that anonymous painter. The attribution remains contested. No work by Niccolò is documented or reliably attributed in the Palazzo Pubblico, and his civic role is documented through the offices he held, not through public commissions.

The word "miniaturist" recurs throughout the literature, and the Metropolitan speaks of his "highly refined miniaturist technique". No illuminated manuscript has been attributed to him, however, and no document calls him *miniatore*. The term describes a technique: tiny figures, closely described objects and dense gold surfaces worked by incising, punching, *sgraffito* and mordant gilding, all suited to close viewing. It describes the way he painted, not a second profession.

## Technique

The National Gallery's catalogue of 2011 records the punches used on the London *Marriage* against the corpus of Trecento punch marks published in 1994 by <a href="/scholars.html#skaug-erling">Erling Skaug</a>. Punches were workshop property, and a comparative study of the London, Florence, New York, San Diego, Boston and Paris panels would offer the best test of whether the oeuvre holds together. No such study has yet been published.

The 2013 entry singles out *sgraffito*, the technique of scratching paint away to reveal the gold beneath, and praises Niccolò's "attention to details and to the representation of objects and precious stuffs, elaborated with sgraffito on gold, a technique made popular thanks to Simone Martini". The cleaning of the *Saint Lawrence* showed the same refinement at a large scale. The silvered and punched backs and frame edges of the Marian panels show that these objects were made to be seen from all sides; since silver tarnishes, their present appearance is far from the original.

## Style and Lineage

The 2013 synthesis sees Niccolò as continuing "the artistic ideals of Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio and Bartolomeo Bulgarini", painters of the previous generation who had each produced a synthesis of the two main tendencies of Sienese painting, that of "Simone Martini and his followers and that of Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti". From Simone and Lippo Memmi come the sinuous line, the treatment of textiles and gold, and the Madonna of Humility. A study of 2014 argues that this subject was not Simone's personal invention but a "collective invention" of Sienese painters. What is individual in Niccolò's versions is the domestic setting, with its sewing basket, prayer book and needlework, details that have been related to a Lorenzettian theme of Mary at work with her spools of thread.

From Ambrogio Lorenzetti comes Niccolò's concern with coherent architectural space. Boskovits saw this concern as distinguishing him from his generation, in which only Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio shared it, and the Florentine catalogue of 1965 derived the architecture of the Uffizi *Presentation* from Ambrogio's *Presentation in the Temple* of 1342, which now hangs in the same room of the Uffizi.

Both encyclopaedia entries note that Niccolò was often confused with Bartolo di Fredi. The 2013 entry distinguishes them by "the spatiality of bodies and settings and the rounded faces, which never show the expressive deformations of Bartolo", although the Feigen fragment shows that the boundary is still blurred. Luca di Tommè is the contemporary most closely linked to Niccolò by documents, through the probable brother Paolo, and Palladino exhibited the two painters together. The 2013 entry also sees "important convergences" with Paolo di Giovanni Fei, and the most uncertain attributions cluster around the overlap of their careers.

The same entry notes that the Berlin panel closely resembles a panel by the Lucchese painter Angelo Puccinelli in Altenburg, from a group of works made in Siena. Its remark that Niccolò was "again in his home city from 1383" implies an earlier absence, for which no documentary basis is given.

## Workshop and Market

The workshop group points to assistants repeating Niccolò's designs at a lower standard. His most common format, a small triptych with a central Madonna and wings showing the Annunciation, the Crucifixion and saints, was standard in post-plague Siena. The recurrence of formats and saints across the works in Boston, San Diego, Bloomington, Asciano, Siena and Prague, with Saints Catherine, Christopher and Anthony Abbot returning in different combinations, suggests a partly standardised production adapted to each buyer through the choice of saints.

Several separate pieces of evidence suggest a varied clientele that included religious women, laywomen and buyers outside Siena: the Augustinian donor of 1387, the domestic furnishings of the Louvre panel, the possibly Florentine Marian group, the diptych sent to the Abruzzi and the Crucifixion in Perugia. Each point is plausible, but together they amount to an interpretation rather than a documented account. Boskovits praised Niccolò's lively, incisive storytelling but also noted a "monotony" of subject that hinders the reconstruction of his chronology. The two observations fit together: variety lies in invented detail within a narrow range of formats, as one would expect of a workshop supplying a steady devotional demand.

## Chronology

There are few fixed points: the commission of 1380 for the lost Saint Daniel, the inscription of 1387, known only from transcription, and the burial in 1388. The earliest dated mention of Niccolò is the priorate of 1372, and the formulas "documented 1372" of the National Gallery and "active Siena by 1372" of the Metropolitan describe the evidence correctly. All the museum dates of the works are stylistic estimates, and some are mutually inconsistent: the Timken triptych about 1370–1375, the Boston panel about 1380, the Glasgow saint about 1370–1375, the Frankfurt fragment 1377–1383, the Lehman *Lamentation* about 1380 and the Hartford *Annunciation* 1370–1388.

The 2013 synthesis proposes a relative sequence. It begins with the Bloomington triptych and perhaps the panel no. 163 in Siena. The Timken triptych, the Munich grisailles and the small Siena panels nos. 121 and 222 follow in the early to middle 1370s. The Berlin panel, the L'Aquila diptych, the Prague triptych and the Marian group belong to around 1380, and the polyptych of 1387 comes at the end. This is a stylistic hypothesis with a single dated anchor.

## Critical Fortune

Milanesi's *Documenti* of 1854 established Niccolò's documentary identity, publishing the guild list, the civic offices and the inscription of 1387; the 1997 entry calls this "brilliant research" that "revealed the existence of this artist". The manuscript biographies of Sienese artists compiled by Ettore Romagnoli before 1835 also mention him. Stylistic reconstruction began in 1885 with the link between the Uffizi and London panels and continued with the article of 1914 that added the Lehman *Coronation* and the Siena panel no. 121, and with further additions in 1918 and 1920, some of which have since been withdrawn. The surveys of the 1920s and the lists of the 1930s built up a corpus of about fifteen works of uneven reliability, and the Louvre Madonna, the Hartford *Annunciation* and the L'Aquila diptych entered it in the 1930s.

A long lull followed, broken only by isolated articles in 1946, 1966 and 1977, until Boskovits's article of 1980 brought Niccolò back to attention, identifying the Kisters Madonna as the Costalpino panel, proposing predella panels and adding new works. The 1980s brought an article on a reidentified panel (1982), the Avignon exhibition of Sienese Gothic painting (1983) and the catalogues of the Lehman Collection (1987) and of the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston (1994), the latter by <a href="/scholars.html#kanter-laurence">Laurence Kanter</a>. Palladino's *Art and Devotion in Siena after 1350: Luca di Tommè and Niccolò di Buonaccorso*, the catalogue of the exhibition held at the Timken Museum from December 1997 to April 1998, remains the nearest thing to a monograph, and the Timken acquired the Costalpino Madonna in 1998. Later contributions include the catalogues of the Städel (2004), the Salini Collection (2009) and the National Gallery (2011), the biographical entry of 2013 and a study of the Louvre panel published in 2014.

Several tasks remain open: a catalogue raisonné, a comparative technical study of the punches, a new archival check of the terzo and of the cathedral commissions, and a reassessment of the workshop group.

## Conclusion

The documented Niccolò di Buonaccorso was a Sienese citizen-painter, probably the son of Buonaccorso di Pace. He was prior in 1372 and 1377, sat on the committee for the *bussolo* in 1375 and was gonfaloniere in 1381. He received the commission for the Saint Daniel panel in 1380, signed a polyptych in 1387 and was buried at San Domenico on 17 May 1388. His surviving signature is on the London *Marriage of the Virgin*, part of a Marian group of uncertain structure and possibly Florentine destination.

Around these fixed points stands a coherent body of portable altarpieces and devotional images together with a workshop group and a range of contested attributions. The polyptych of 1387 shows that the same refinement could be carried to a monumental scale. Several recurrent errors, such as the guild enrolment of 1355, the date 1383 for the Saint Daniel commission or a career compressed into five years, arise from the same few causes: misreadings of the Sienese calendar, the repetition of superseded reference works, and the confusion of the date of a work with the span of a career. Their correction is a precondition for any history of the painter.

His painting shows what the generation after the Black Death made of the great Sienese inheritance. He did not choose between the elegance of Simone Martini and the spatial construction of the Lorenzetti, but joined them on a small scale: the sinuous line and the precious surfaces of the one, the rooms, loggias and courtyards of the other. The result is a painting in which sacred events take place in recognisable houses, among beds, benches, carpets and workbaskets, and in which the gold ground coexists with a coherent space measured by chequered floors and arcades. In this respect Niccolò belongs with Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio and Bartolomeo Bulgarini rather than with the more expressive manner of Bartolo di Fredi.

His work also shows how devotion changed the uses of painting in Siena in the second half of the fourteenth century. Portable triptychs, diptychs and small panels, made to be opened, closed, carried and held close to the eye, served the private prayer of laymen and laywomen, religious communities and buyers outside the city. The recurring saints of his triptychs, Catherine, Christopher and Anthony Abbot among them, answered the needs of protection and intercession of their owners, while the domestic setting of his Virgins offered them an image of holiness within the household. The workshop that repeated his designs met a demand that was steady, varied in detail and limited in format.

Niccolò's case finally illustrates the conditions under which the history of a Trecento painter can be written. A single surviving signature, a lost inscription transcribed in the nineteenth century and a handful of civic records support a body of work assembled over a century and a half through connoisseurship, technical study and the chance reappearance of dispersed panels. The rediscovery of the Costalpino polyptych changed the image of the painter more than any document, and further works may still emerge from private collections and from the storerooms of museums. Each addition will have to be measured against the few fixed points that the documents and the signed works provide.

The Marriage of the Virgin in the National Gallery in London (NG 1109) is the only work that still carries Niccolò di Buonaccorso's signature, and on it rests the identification of his hand. It is painted in egg tempera on wood, probably poplar, measures 50.9 by 33 centimetres and keeps its original fourteenth-century Sienese frame. The Gallery dates it about 1380 and bought it in 1881 from Charles Fairfax Murray, who had probably seen it for sale in Florence in 1877. It is displayed in Room 58.

The frame is part of the work. A cusped trefoil arch, carved and gilded, closes the painted field at the top, and its spandrels are filled with foliage worked in relief. At the bottom a narrow blue band carries the signature in gold capitals, NICHOLAVS. BONACHVRSI. DE SENIS. ME P[I]NXIT, "Niccolò di Buonaccorso of Siena painted me", in which the bracketed letter marks a modern completion. The same dimensions and the same framing recur in the Presentation of the Virgin in the Uffizi and the Coronation of the Virgin in the Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum, with which the panel formed a series on the life of the Virgin.

The scene is set in the open courtyard of a palace. A grey loggia with round arches closes the background, and above it a balustrade of trefoil arches carries pots of flowering plants, before a tall tree with blue-green foliage that rises against the gold. At the upper right a tower with a projecting roof frames a window from which two women look down, beside a white cloth hung on a pole, and a bird flies across the gold sky. The architecture, drawn in sharp perspective lines and painted in grey, pink and violet, turns the gold ground into a sky above a city.

At the centre the high priest, bearded and wearing a mitre and a cope of gold brocade studded with jewels, joins the hands of the couple. Joseph, at the left, an old man with grey curly hair, wears a pink mantle with a yellow lining over a blue tunic and stands barefoot on the carpet; he places the ring on Mary's finger and holds the rod that, according to the Golden Legend<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-jacopo-da-varagine" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>, flowered as a sign of his election. Mary, at the right, bows her head under a gold halo. Her dark blue dress is almost covered by gold patterns, and she holds a small red book in her left hand.

Behind Joseph stand two haloed figures, an old bearded man and a woman in a grey veil, who can be identified as Mary's parents, Joachim and Anna. Further left crowd the rejected suitors with their bare rods, young men in red, grey and yellow garments. At the lower left a kneeling youth in a dark blue cloak bends his rod in anger, the traditional gesture of the disappointed suitor, and in front of him a small boy in a gold-patterned tunic and red hose looks up at the ceremony.

The right side of the panel belongs to the women and the musicians. Behind Mary stands a young woman in a gold dress and a blue mantle, followed by a group of veiled women. At the far right two trumpeters raise long trumpets towards the loggia, a piper plays beside them, and a young man in a red tunic patterned with gold and pink hose beats a pair of small drums, the nakers, hung from his belt. The music turns the sacred rite into a public celebration, of the kind held in a Tuscan city of the fourteenth century.

The figures stand on a carpet with a pattern of red and yellow octagons and stylised motifs, laid over a floor painted in green and white to imitate marble. The carpet has been studied in surveys of oriental carpets in European painting. A thin column with a carved capital rises at the right edge of the scene, in front of the musicians, and marks the foreground plane. The small scale of the figures, the density of the gold patterns on the garments and the precise rendering of objects show the minute technique for which Niccolò is known.

The panel is in good condition. The gold of the ground and of the frame is worn in places, and the green marbled floor and the gold sky show small losses and abrasions. Technical study has shown that some colours have changed: the leaves on Joseph's rod, now blue, were originally green, because the yellow component of the green mixture has faded. The faces, the patterned garments and the inscription remain clearly legible.

The Presentation of the Virgin in the Temple in the Uffizi (inventory 1890, number 3157) measures 51 by 34 centimetres with its frame and is dated by the museum about 1380. It came from the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova in Florence and is displayed in room A5, with the paintings of Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti brothers. Its dimensions and its frame match those of the signed Marriage of the Virgin in London and of the Coronation of the Virgin in the Lehman Collection in New York, and the three panels are generally regarded as parts of a series on the life of the Virgin.

The frame repeats the type of the London panel: a cusped arch carved and gilded, spandrels filled with foliage in relief, here with a small rosette at the centre of each, and a moulded outer border. Unlike the London panel, it carries no inscription. Two metal fittings are fixed to the right edge of the frame. Their date has not been established, but they bear on the question, still open, of whether the panels of the series were hinged together as the wings of a folding altarpiece or displayed side by side.

The temple is shown as an open pavilion, a baldachin raised on slender grey columns with pink capitals and cornices and covered by a pyramidal roof crowned with a small lantern. On the corners of the roof two winged putti stand and hold a blue garland, a motif derived from classical sculpture that is unusual in Sienese painting of this period. Through the arches the eye reaches a dark blue vault and an altar on which stands a silver Gothic tabernacle. The structure, open on every side, allows the whole ceremony to be seen at once.

The story comes from the apocryphal accounts of the childhood of Mary retold in the Golden Legend. At the age of three Mary was brought by her parents to the temple of Jerusalem to be dedicated to God, and she climbed the fifteen steps of the temple alone, without looking back. Niccolò shows her already at the top of a flight of white steps, a small figure in a dark dress covered with gold patterns, her hands crossed on her breast. The high priest, bearded, in a mitre and a gold cope, bends forward and opens his hands to receive her.

At the foot of the steps, at the left, Saint Anne, in a purple mantle over a white wimple, looks up at her daughter. Behind her stand young women in garments of gold, blue and red; the one in front, in a gold dress, holds a striped cloth over her arm. At the right Joachim, an old man with a long beard, wears a blue mantle and holds a staff. Behind him are a man in a turban and a gold-patterned robe, an old man in a yellow hood and a woman in a red mantle who lays her hand on her breast. Behind a parapet near the priest a group of young girls watches the scene, the companions with whom Mary was to live in the temple.

The composition is built on strict symmetry. The steps rise along the central axis, the groups of figures balance each other at either side, and the columns of the pavilion divide the space into regular bays. The floor, paved with squares of blue-grey and white marble, recedes towards the steps and gives depth to the small scene. Within this order Niccolò sets the lively detail of faces, gestures and patterned garments that distinguishes his work, with gold worked over the dresses of Mary, the priest and the young woman at the left.

The Presentation completes the narrative logic of the series. It precedes the Marriage of the Virgin in London and the Coronation in New York, and the gaps between them, the Annunciation, the Nativity and the Dormition among them, suggest that further panels may have belonged to the group. The 1965 catalogue of the Florentine galleries observed a metal fitting on one edge of this panel and proposed that the panels served as shutters; the London catalogue instead treats the signed Marriage as the central panel.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground and the frame are worn in places and show a fine network of cracks, and the pink and grey of the architecture are slightly abraded. The faces, the patterned garments and the marble floor are well preserved, and the blue of the vault and of Joachim's mantle keeps its depth.

The Coronation of the Virgin in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum (1975.1.21) measures 50.8 by 32.7 centimetres with its engaged frame, and 44.8 by 26.7 centimetres for the painted surface. The museum dates it about 1380. It belonged to the Sciarra collection in Rome and passed through the Vicomte Bernard d'Hendecourt and the Kleinberger Galleries to Philip Lehman in 1914; after a period with Pauline Ickelheimer it returned to Robert Lehman in 1946 and entered the museum with his collection in 1975. A 1914 article on unpublished Sienese paintings first attached it to the London Marriage of the Virgin and the Uffizi Presentation.

The frame is carved in one piece with the panel, an engaged frame of the same type and size as those of London and Florence: a cusped arch, spandrels filled with foliage in relief and a moulded outer border. In each upper corner a small round boss is set into the gilding. The outer edges of the frame were silvered and punched, and the back of the panel is decorated, like those of its two companions, with a silvered and punched surface painted with blue and red lozenges. This shared decoration suggests that the backs were meant to be seen, and therefore that the panels could be folded or turned.

The upper part of the panel is filled by the hosts of heaven. Against the gold, a fan of winged heads painted in dark blue, the cherubim, opens like a crown of rays. Below them runs a band of white clouds with small heads of angels, and around the throne crowd the red seraphim, their wings and faces painted in vermilion and orange. The colours follow the medieval tradition that associated the cherubim with the blue of knowledge and the seraphim with the red of divine love.

At the centre Christ and the Virgin sit side by side on a red cloud scattered with small winged heads. Christ, at the right, wears a pink tunic and a large dark blue mantle and raises his right arm to place the crown on his mother's head. The Virgin, at the left, turns towards him and bows, her hands crossed on her breast in humility. Her blue-grey mantle is almost entirely covered with gold patterns, so that her figure seems woven of light, against the plain dark blue of Christ's mantle.

At either side tiers of angels with gold haloes and gold-patterned garments look towards the central pair. Several sing with open mouths, and two, at the level of the cloud, play pipes. Their faces, small and individually turned, show the minute execution that the Metropolitan describes as "a highly refined miniaturist technique", and their haloes are worked with fine punching.

In the lower half the gold ground gives way to a carpet patterned with interlocking frets in orange, blue and gold. Four angels kneel on it. At the left one plays a portable organ, its pipes rising above the keyboard; at the right another draws a bow across a fiddle. In the centre two angels with wreaths of flowers in their hair kneel facing each other and look up towards the coronation. Music, in this lower register, accompanies the ceremony above, as it does in the Marriage of the Virgin in London.

Within the series to which it belongs, the Coronation is the final episode of the life of the Virgin, after her presentation in the temple and her marriage. Its composition, with the hierarchies of angels above and the musicians below, is denser and more symmetrical than the two narrative scenes, and it turns from the earthly setting of the temple and the courtyard to a purely celestial space. The three panels share dimensions, framing and, in the Metropolitan's words, "an analogous moment of style".

The panel is well preserved. The gold ground and the frame show fine cracks and some wear, especially along the edges of the frame and in the lower corners, and the red of the seraphim and the carpet has darkened in places. The faces, the gold patterns of the garments, the instruments and the punched haloes are clearly legible, and the engaged frame retains its original patina.

The Madonna and Child in the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego (1998:001) is the central panel of the polyptych that bore the inscription "Nicholaus Bonachursi me pinxit A(nno) D(omi)ni 1387", recorded by <a href="/scholars.html#milanesi-gaetano">Gaetano Milanesi</a> in 1854 in the church of Santa Margherita at Costalpino, outside Siena, and now lost. It is painted in tempera and gold on panel and measures 152.5 by 58.5 centimetres. The lost inscription gives the work the only date in Niccolò's stylistic chronology, a year before his burial in 1388.

Removed from Santa Margherita before 1862, the Madonna reappeared at a sale in 1953 and was acquired in 1998 by the Putnam Foundation for the Timken. Its companion, the Saint Lawrence, is in the Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra of Siena.

The panel is tall and narrow, closed by a gable whose apex has been cut, and its field is framed by a carved cusped arch. The size, more than one and a half metres high, makes it Niccolò's largest surviving work and shows him working at the scale of a monumental Sienese polyptych, in the tradition established by Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti.

The Virgin sits on a Gothic throne painted in pink and orange, whose back, shaped as a cusped arch, opens onto a small vaulted niche. She wears a crown over a white veil, and a deep blue mantle marked with a gold star on the shoulder, the traditional sign of her title Stella Maris, over a dress densely patterned with gold. Her face is long and pale, with lowered eyes and a calm, absorbed expression.

The Christ Child sits on her left knee, his curly golden hair framed by a punched halo. He wears a light blue tunic with a gold belt and a red mantle over his legs, and looks up at his mother. With one hand he grasps the stem of a white flower that she holds between her fingers; in the other he holds a small gold basket filled with fruit and flowers. The exchange of the flower and the basket of fruit, emblems of purity and of the fruitfulness of the Incarnation, gives the solemn image a gentle, domestic tone.

The throne rests on a white step decorated with a zigzag pattern in pink and orange, and the figures fill almost the whole width of the field. The broad, simple masses of the blue mantle and the red cloth, set against the gold, give the figure a monumental clarity that differs from the crowded compositions of Niccolò's small panels, while the gold patterns of the dress and the delicate faces keep his characteristic refinement.

The panel is in good condition. The blue of the Virgin's mantle is well preserved, with its folds clearly modelled, and the gold of the dress, the crown and the haloes is bright. The frame has lost part of its gilding and the apex of the gable, and the gold ground shows some wear in the lobes of the arch.

The Saint Lawrence is the only surviving lateral panel of the polyptych of 1387 from Santa Margherita at Costalpino, whose central Madonna is in San Diego. The panel measures about 140 by 47 centimetres. It was moved to the church of Sant'Andrea a Montecchio, and it is now in the Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra of Siena, housed in the Oratorio di San Bernardino, where it is displayed in the first room.

The panel keeps its carved frame, which has lost its gilding and now shows the dark red bole beneath. A cusped trefoil arch closes the gold field, and above it a sloping gable is carved with rosettes and scrolls in relief. The top of the frame has been cut, and the gable that once rose above the arch is missing. The tall, narrow format and the trefoil arch correspond to the lateral compartments of a large Sienese polyptych of the late fourteenth century.

Saint Lawrence, the Roman deacon martyred in 258, stands almost frontally, his body turned slightly to the left, towards the place that the central Madonna occupied. He is a young man with a broad face, a tonsure and an attentive expression, and his halo is finely punched. He wears the vestments of a deacon: a long dalmatic of red cloth patterned with gold, beneath which a pale tunic with a lozenge pattern and a white alb with a red lower border appear at the hem.

In his right hand he holds a slender processional cross together with a dark palm, the sign of his martyrdom, and in his left a red book. Behind him at the left rises the gridiron on which, according to tradition, he was roasted to death. Painted in thin gold lines over the gold ground, it is almost as tall as the saint himself, and it makes the instrument of his torture a frame for the figure.

At the lower left, on a strip of green ground at the saint's feet, kneel the patrons of the altarpiece: a woman in a black habit and a white wimple, her hands joined in prayer, and two small children, one in red and one in blue. The dark habit has been read as Augustinian, and the woman as a widow with her children. On this basis the polyptych has been connected with Santa Maria Maddalena, a convent of Augustinian nuns outside Porta Tufi destroyed in 1526, a hypothesis that no document has yet confirmed.

The history of the panel explains its appearance. Overpainted in the sixteenth century as Saint Margaret, with her dragon covering the donors, it was cleaned after the reappearance of the central Madonna in 1953, and the cleaning recovered the original figure, the donors and the sgraffito on the gold patterns of the dalmatic.

The figure is painted with the same care for patterned cloth and gold as Niccolò's small panels, translated to a scale several times larger. The broad dalmatic falling in long vertical folds and the calm, frontal face give the saint the gravity required of a figure in the main tier of an altarpiece.

The panel shows the marks of its history. The red dalmatic is heavily abraded, with extensive losses in the gold patterns and the underlying paint, particularly in the lower half, and the frame has lost its gilding and part of its upper moulding. The gold ground, the face, the hands, the book and the small figures of the donors are better preserved, and the gridiron remains clearly legible against the gold.

The fragment in the Städel Museum in Frankfurt (inventory 1006) is painted on poplar and measures 36.4 by 27.8 centimetres, with a thickness of 1.6 centimetres. The museum catalogues it as a "Virgin (fragment)" by Niccolò di Buonaccorso and dates it about 1377–1383. It entered the collection in 1965, in an exchange with the Frankfurter Verein für Geschichte und Alterthumskunde, and is not currently on display.

The panel has been cut down to an irregular arched shape that follows the outline of the halo, and its edges are worn to the bare wood. Nothing survives of the figure below the shoulders, and the original format of the panel to which it belonged can only be inferred from the scale of the head, which is considerably larger than the figures of Niccolò's small devotional panels and corresponds to that of a monumental altarpiece.

The Virgin turns her head towards the right and glances sideways out of the corners of her eyes. Her face is long and pale, with pink cheeks, a long straight nose, a small mouth with red lips and finely drawn brows. Wavy blond hair, rendered in parallel golden strands, frames her face beneath a white veil, over which falls a dark blue mantle. The gold border of the mantle is set with small round dots of red and dark colour, imitating a band of embroidery or jewels.

The large halo, now largely worn to the red bole that lay beneath the gold, is decorated with punched rings and carries traces of an incised inscription. It has been read as "AVE GRATIA PLENA", the words of the angel Gabriel to Mary. This inscription points to an Annunciation rather than to an enthroned Madonna, and it suggests that the fragment comes from the figure of the Virgin Annunciate, which in Sienese polyptychs often occupied one of the pinnacles above the main panels.

The fragment has been connected with the polyptych of 1387, but the museum's date and the inscription, which does not suit an enthroned Madonna, leave the link uncertain, and the head may come from another large altarpiece of Niccolò's maturity.

Whatever its origin, the head is one of the few surviving works in which Niccolò's style can be studied at a large scale. The precise drawing of the features, the smooth modelling of the flesh with its greenish underlayer and pink highlights, and the calligraphic treatment of the hair show the same refinement as his small panels, translated into a monumental format.

At the right edge of the fragment appear areas of green and ochre, which may belong to a cloth, a piece of architecture or the background of the original composition. They are too small to be identified with certainty, but they show that the head was set within a larger painted scene or field, not against a plain gold ground alone.

The fragment is in poor condition. A large loss, exposing the wood, runs from below the lips over the chin and down across the breast, and the gold of the halo has almost entirely disappeared. The blue mantle is flaked and abraded, especially at the lower right, and the surface is crossed by a fine network of cracks. The face, the eyes, the hair and the border of the mantle are nonetheless well preserved.

  <figcaption>Niccolò di Buonaccorso, <em>Saint Lawrence</em>, c. 1370–1375. Tempera on panel, 58.8 × 27.6 cm. Glasgow, Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum, acc. 3359, gift of Julius Lewis Lyons, 1980. The Saint Lawrence in Kelvingrove Art Gallery and Museum in Glasgow (accession number 3359) is painted in tempera on panel and measures 58.8 by 27.6 centimetres. The museum dates it about 1370–1375. It was given to the collection in 1980 by Julius Lewis Lyons, a benefactor who also lent, gave and bequeathed to Glasgow silver, antiquities and Old Master drawings.

The panel is narrow and closed by a pointed arch, and the saint is shown in half length. The museum observes that its size and shape show that it once formed part of a large altarpiece of the type made in and around Siena in the late fourteenth century. A half-length saint of this size would fit an upper register of a polyptych, above the full-length saints of the main tier.

Saint Lawrence is shown as a young deacon with a tonsure and a broad, calm face, turned slightly to the right, towards the centre of the altarpiece to which the panel belonged. His halo is decorated with punched rings and small motifs. He wears a red dalmatic, the vestment of the deacon, with a high collar of gold set with red and blue stones and a broad band of gold lattice ornament across the chest, and dark blue sleeves appear at his wrists.

In his right hand he holds the gridiron of his martyrdom. It is painted in black, upright beside him, and rises from the lower edge of the panel almost to his shoulder. In his left hand he holds a palm, the sign of his martyrdom.

The figure shows the features of Niccolò's style: the precise drawing of the face, the attention to the jewelled and patterned textiles and the delicate treatment of the gold. The gold ornaments of the dalmatic, worked as a dense lattice dotted with colour, recall the patterned cloths of his small devotional panels.

A connection with the polyptych of 1387 has been proposed, but the museum's date and the smaller size of the panel argue against it, and the Glasgow saint is distinct from the full-length Saint Lawrence in Siena.

The comparison between them is nonetheless instructive. In both, Lawrence is a young deacon in a red dalmatic with the gridiron at the left and the palm, but the Glasgow saint is half-length and holds the gridiron himself, while the Siena saint stands at full length and the gridiron rises behind him in gold lines. The repetition of the type shows how Niccolò varied a common model from one commission to another.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is worn throughout, revealing the red bole, especially in the upper part and around the halo, and the red of the dalmatic is abraded. The face, the hands and the gold ornaments of the vestment are better preserved.

### Madonna of Humility with Saints Catherine and Christopher, the Annunciation and the Crucifixion (San Diego, Timken Museum of Art)

  <img src="/Images/Niccolo-di-Buonaccorso/niccolo-di-buonaccorso-18.jpg" alt="Small gilded triptych with three steep gables edged with carved crockets.   <figcaption>Niccolò di Buonaccorso, <em>Madonna of Humility with Saints Catherine of Alexandria and Christopher, the Annunciation and the Crucifixion</em>, ca. 1370–1375. Tempera and gold on wood panel, 65.8 × 54.6 cm. San Diego, Timken Museum of Art, Putnam Foundation, 1967:002. Photo: courtesy of the Timken Museum of Art, San Diego, reproduced with permission.</figcaption>
</figure>

The portable triptych in the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego is one of the two works by Niccolò in the museum, beside the central Madonna of the polyptych of 1387. The museum (1967:002) dates it about 1370–1375 and catalogues it as tempera and gold on wood panel, 65.8 by 54.6 centimetres. The 2013 synthesis places it in the early to middle 1370s, after the triptych in Bloomington, among Niccolò's early small works.

The triptych consists of a central panel and two wings, each closed by a steep gable edged with carved crockets. It is a small object, made for private devotion: it could be opened on a table or an altar in a house or a chapel, and closed and carried. When closed, simple painted decoration on the outer faces conceals the richness of the interior.

The central panel shows the Madonna of Humility. The Virgin does not sit on a throne but low on a patterned cushion on the floor, the posture that gives the type its name, a sign of her humility before God. She wears a dark mantle over a dress patterned with gold and holds the Child, dressed in pink, on her knee, inclining her head towards him.

Behind the Virgin runs a low bench set in a shallow interior, on which small objects are placed. The setting, a room rather than a gold ground, gives the image a domestic character, and it connects the Timken panel with the Virgin of Humility in the Louvre, where the Virgin sits in a similar room with a bench along the wall.

The gables carry the Annunciation and the Crucifixion. In the left gable the angel Gabriel kneels, in the right the Virgin Annunciate sits, so that the Annunciation spans the two wings above the saints. In the central gable Christ hangs on the cross between the Virgin and Saint John, with a kneeling figure at the foot of the cross, probably Mary Magdalene. The Incarnation and the Passion thus frame the image of the mother and child.

The wings carry two saints. At the left stands Saint Catherine of Alexandria, crowned, in a pink mantle, holding the wheel of her martyrdom and the palm. At the right Saint Christopher, in red, carries the Child on his shoulder across a river, leaning on a staff that sprouts leaves. Christopher, the protector against sudden death, recurs in the wings of the Prague triptych and of the triptych in the Salini collection.

The figures show the features of Niccolò's small panels: slender proportions, delicate faces, a dense use of gold in the garments and the frames. The colours, the dark blue and black of the Virgin's mantle, the pink of Catherine and the Child, the red of Christopher, are set against the burnished gold of the ground and of the carved frame.

The triptych shows the format in which much of Niccolò's work was produced, the small portable triptych with a Madonna in the centre, saints in the wings and the Annunciation and the Crucifixion in the gables. Its quality and its preserved frame make it one of the best examples of the type, and the comparison with the monumental Madonna of 1387 in the same museum shows the range of the painter's work.

### Virgin and Child Enthroned with a Bishop Saint, Saint John the Baptist and Four Angels (Boston, Museum of Fine Arts)

  <figcaption>Niccolò di Buonaccorso, <em>Virgin and Child Enthroned with a Bishop Saint, Saint John the Baptist, and Four Angels</em>, about 1380. Tempera on panel, 46.3 × 31.7 cm. The panel in the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston (20.1860) measures 46.3 by 31.7 centimetres and is dated by the museum about 1380. It was acquired in Gubbio in the nineteenth century by Mary Rich Richardson, who gave it to the museum in 1920. In a letter of 1907 she recalled that it had been bought "many years" earlier and was then attributed to Lucas Cranach. The museum notes that it may have come from the sale of the Ranghiasci-Brancaleoni collection held in Gubbio on 12 April 1882, although no painting in the sale catalogue can be identified with it. It is catalogued in <a href="/scholars.html#kanter-laurence">Laurence B. Kanter</a>'s catalogue of the Italian paintings in Boston (1994, no. 21) and is not on view.

The museum describes the panel as "probably the central section of a portable triptych (three-part altarpiece) whose wings are now missing". The shape supports this reading. The panel is closed by a gable cut at the top, and on each side of the frame broken projections mark the points where the wings were once attached. Closed, the wings would have protected the central image; opened, they would have flanked it with further saints or scenes.

Within a cusped arch the Virgin sits on a throne whose back, covered with a cloth patterned in red and gold, rises to a point like a gable. She wears a dark blue mantle over a dress densely patterned with gold, and holds the Christ Child on her lap. The Child, in a pink tunic, sits upright and turns towards her, holding a small object in his hand. Mary looks out towards the viewer with a grave expression, her head slightly inclined.

Four angels surround the throne. The two in the upper lobes of the arch lean towards the Virgin with their hands joined in prayer, while the two below, at the level of her shoulders, cross their hands on their breasts and look towards the centre. Their gold haloes, gold-patterned garments and coloured wings fill the space around the throne with a dense surface of gold.

At the left stands Saint John the Baptist, barefoot, in a camel-hair tunic under a pink mantle lined in blue. He looks out at the viewer and points with his right hand to an unrolled scroll that he holds in his left, inscribed with his words. At the right stands a bishop saint with a grey beard, wearing a mitre and a gold cope over a white alb, holding a crozier and a red book and raising his eyes towards the Virgin. His identity is not fixed by any attribute. The panel's discovery in Gubbio invites a connection with Ubaldus, the city's patron bishop, but nothing in the figure confirms it.

The throne rests on a white step with a patterned riser bordered in red, beneath which a floor painted in green and white imitates marble. The contrast between the hierarchical scale of the Virgin and the smaller standing saints, and the arrangement of angels around the throne, follow the formula of the Sienese Maestà, reduced to the size of a portable altarpiece.

The museum observes that the painter "maintained the dedication to sinuous line pioneered by Duccio and seen here in the hems of the draperies but added highly textured effects of patterned cloth through a lavish use of gold". The gold patterns of the Virgin's dress, the angels' garments and the bishop's cope, worked with incising and punching, show the minute technique that runs through Niccolò's small panels.

The panel is in good condition. The gold of the frame is worn along the edges and the projections at the sides are broken, but the painted surface is well preserved. The dark blue of the Virgin's mantle has darkened, and the marbled floor shows some abrasion, while the faces, the gold patterns and the punched haloes remain clearly legible.

The Virgin of Humility in the Louvre (RF 1976 7) is painted in tempera on poplar and measures 54 by 30 centimetres. The museum's collection record assigns it to the Sienese school and dates it to the second half of the fourteenth century, between 1355 and 1388, while the Louvre's "Tableau du mois" no. 186 (7 December 2011 – 7 January 2012) placed it about 1380. The gift from Mme Lanini-Strölin, made with retention of usufruct, was approved in February 1976; the panel had passed through the Berlin art market and the dealer Alfred Strölin. It entered the museum in 2011 and is displayed in the Aile Denon.

The panel is closed by a gable, and its frame, carved in one piece with it, has lost its gilding and shows the bare wood and gesso. The small metal fittings at the edges belong to its modern mounting. The size and the gabled shape suggest that it was the central panel of a small portable altarpiece or a self-contained image for private devotion.

In the gable, against a red ground scattered with gold, God the Father appears in a mandorla, bearded, blessing with his right hand and holding a book in his left. Dark blue cherubim surround him at the top, and red seraphim fill the field below. Under him a small white dove, the Holy Spirit, descends through golden rays towards the Virgin. Together with the Child below, the gable makes the image an image of the Trinity.

The Virgin is shown in a room, not against a gold ground. The floor is paved with pink and white squares in perspective, and along the walls runs a low bench whose panels are painted yellow with small stars, with a hinged panel opening at either side like the wing of a cupboard. The space is shallow and box-like, but it gives the scene the setting of a domestic interior, unusual for a Madonna of Humility.

The Virgin sits on the floor, on a gold-patterned cushion, in the posture of humility that gives the type its name. She wears a black mantle with a gold border over a dress patterned with gold, and she nurses the Christ Child, who lies in her arms in a pink garment. Her head bends towards him with a tender, slightly melancholy expression, and her halo is finely punched.

On the bench at the left lie a round work basket with spools of thread, a piece of needlework and a pair of scissors; on the bench at the right lies a red prayer book. The objects refer to the tradition that Mary, as a girl in the temple, spun and wove, and to her reading at the moment of the Annunciation, and they have suggested that the panel was made for a devout laywoman, who could recognise her own household and devotions in the Virgin's room. The painting was shown in the Louvre's exhibition "Les Choses. Une histoire de la nature morte" (12 October 2022 – 23 January 2023), in the section "Les Objets de la croyance".

The care with which the objects are rendered, the punched gold and the delicate faces correspond to Niccolò's small devotional panels around 1380. The literature differs on its place in his chronology: a date of about 1370 has been proposed, while the most recent synthesis places it after the Madonna of Humility triptych in San Diego.

The panel is in fair condition. The frame has lost its gilding entirely, and the gold and red of the gable are worn, with losses at the top. The black of the Virgin's mantle has lost much of its modelling, while the faces, the gold patterns of the dress, the chequered floor and the objects on the bench are well preserved.

The portable triptych in the Salini Collection at Asciano is one of the few works associated with Niccolò di Buonaccorso that preserve their complete original structure: central panel, wings, carved gable and base. It was shown in a temporary exhibition of the collection in 2017, and it is generally dated about 1370–1380. It belongs to the group of works of lesser execution produced in his workshop.

The structure is that of a small domestic tabernacle. The central panel stands within a gilded frame flanked by twisted colonnettes, under a steep gable edged with carved crockets set with red and green glass stones and crowned by a carved finial. The wings, closed by gables of their own, are hinged to the central panel and could be folded over it. The whole rests on a base decorated with three roundels, now too worn to be read. A tabernacle of this kind could stand on a table or a chest, or be hung on the wall of a bedroom.

In the gable of the central panel Christ appears in half length, blessing with his right hand and holding a book. In the main field the Virgin, in a black mantle over a dress patterned with gold, sits on a throne covered with a red and gold cloth and offers her breast to the Christ Child, who stands on her lap in a white garment and turns towards the viewer. Two angels kneel in prayer behind the throne. The type of the nursing Virgin, the Madonna del latte, was widespread in Sienese devotional painting of the fourteenth century.

Two saints stand in front of the throne. At the left Saint Catherine of Alexandria, crowned, in a pink mantle over a gold-patterned dress, holds a palm and a book beside the wheel of her martyrdom. At the right Saint Anthony Abbot, an old bearded hermit in a brown habit, holds a book and a staff, and at his feet is the small black pig that is one of his attributes, associated with the Antonine order and its care of the sick.

The wings complete the programme. In their triangular tops the angel Gabriel, at the left, and the Virgin Annunciate, at the right, form the Annunciation, which frames the central image when the triptych is open. Below, the left wing shows a bishop saint in a mitre and a gold-patterned cope, holding a crozier and a book, whose identity is not fixed by any attribute. The right wing shows Saint Christopher, in a red tunic, carrying the Christ Child on his shoulders across the water with the help of a staff.

The choice of Saint Christopher, the protector against sudden death, and of Saint Anthony Abbot, invoked against illness and the disease known as Saint Anthony's fire, suggests a patron concerned with protection in daily life. The combination of the Annunciation, the nursing Virgin and Christ blessing above repeats in a domestic form the sequence of the Incarnation found in Niccolò's larger triptych in San Diego, which also includes Saint Catherine and Saint Christopher.

The composition and the types follow Niccolò's models: the gold patterns of the garments, the slender figures and the narrow, crowded space of the central panel are close to his small panels in Boston and San Diego. The execution, however, is less refined, with simplified faces and a flatter treatment of the draperies, which supports its attribution to his workshop rather than to the master himself.

The triptych is in fair condition. The gilding of the frame and of the carved gable is worn, and some of the glass stones are missing. The black of the Virgin's mantle has lost its modelling, and the wings show abrasions and losses, especially in the lower parts and in the figure of the bishop saint. The central group, the saints in front of the throne and the Saint Christopher remain clearly legible.

The small triptych in the National Gallery in Prague (O 11916) is dated about 1370–1380 and is accepted as a work of Niccolò di Buonaccorso by the most recent synthesis of his work, which places it around 1380, in the same phase as the Marian panels of London, Florence and New York. The triptych preserves its three gabled panels and its base. Its provenance has been connected with the collection of Italian primitives at the castle of Konopiště, published in 1946, but the link has not been confirmed.

The structure is that of a portable altarpiece for private devotion. The central panel and the two wings are each closed by a steep gable and framed by a plain gilded moulding, and the wings, half the width of the centre, could be folded over it. Unlike the Salini triptych, the frame has no carved crockets or finials, and the gables are decorated only with incised and punched ornament in the gold.

In the central gable Christ blesses in a quatrefoil. Below, under a cusped arch, the Virgin sits on a throne covered with a red and gold cloth whose back rises to a point. She wears a black mantle over a dress densely patterned with gold and holds the Christ Child, in a pink garment, standing on her knee; the Child turns towards her. Two angels stand behind the throne, their wings rising above their shoulders.

In front of the throne stand Saint John the Baptist, at the left, barefoot in a camel-hair tunic and a green mantle, pointing to an unrolled scroll with his words, and Saint Catherine of Alexandria, at the right, in a pink mantle over a gold dress, holding a palm. The small scale of the saints, set at the foot of the throne within the same field as the Virgin, follows the formula of the Sienese Maestà reduced to the size of a domestic image.

The wings carry the Annunciation in their gables: at the left the angel Gabriel kneels with his wings raised, and at the right the Virgin Annunciate sits on the ground with her hand on her breast. Below, the left wing shows Saint Anthony Abbot, an old bearded hermit in a brown habit, leaning on his tau-headed staff in a rocky landscape with a tree. The right wing shows Saint Christopher in a red tunic, carrying the Christ Child, who holds a globe, across a river with the help of a leafy staff.

The programme is close to that of other portable triptychs connected with Niccolò. Saint Christopher, the protector against sudden death, appears in the wings of the triptych in San Diego and of the Salini triptych, Saint Catherine recurs in all three, and Saint Anthony Abbot appears in the Salini triptych. The repetition suggests a standard repertoire of protective saints offered by Niccolò's workshop to private clients, combined each time in a different way.

The quality of execution distinguishes the Prague triptych from the Salini one. The faces are finely drawn, the gold patterns of the Virgin's dress and of the angels' garments are worked with care, and the landscape of the wings, with its rocks, tree and water, is painted with an attention to detail that corresponds to Niccolò's own hand.

The triptych is in good condition. The gilding of the frame is worn along the edges, and the black of the Virgin's mantle has lost much of its modelling. The gold grounds, the faces, the patterned garments and the figures of the wings are well preserved.

The panel in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Siena (inventory 121) measures 64.5 by 28 centimetres with its frame. The national heritage catalogue assigns it to Niccolò di Buonaccorso and dates it between about 1360 and 1380, with an alternative date of about 1370, and it was first attributed to him in 1914. It was shown in 2016 in an exhibition on the theme of mercy at Senigallia. The most recent synthesis of Niccolò's work places it among the small panels of the early and middle 1370s.

The panel keeps its original frame, carved in one piece with it. A cusped arch closes the main field, with foliage carved in relief in the spandrels, and above it rises a steep gable edged with carved crockets. The tall, narrow format, more than twice as high as it is wide, suggest that it was a self-contained image for private devotion rather than the centre of a triptych.

In the gable Christ hangs on the cross against the gold, on the dark hill of Golgotha, with the titulus above his head. The Virgin, at the left, wraps herself in her mantle, and Saint John the Evangelist, at the right, in a pink mantle over a blue tunic, clasps his hands. The Crucifixion above the nursing Virgin joins the beginning and the end of Christ's earthly life, as in many Sienese devotional panels of the period.

In the main field the Virgin sits on a throne covered with a cloth patterned in gold and nurses the Christ Child, who lies in her arms in a pink garment and turns to her breast. She inclines her head towards him with a gentle expression. Her mantle now appears a brownish ochre, a colour unusual for the Virgin, and it is probable that its upper layer, originally blue, has been lost or has altered, leaving the underpainting exposed.

Six saints stand at either side of the throne, three on each side, one behind the other, in a vertical arrangement that fills the narrow field. The heritage catalogue identifies them as Nicholas, Peter and Lucy at the left, and James, Paul and Catherine of Alexandria at the right. Nicholas wears a bishop's mitre, Peter holds his keys, and Lucy, in a gold-patterned dress, holds a book. James holds a pilgrim's staff, Paul a sword and a book, and Catherine, crowned, in a pink mantle, holds a palm and a book.

The arrangement of the saints, set in tiers rather than in a row, follows the solution used in Sienese Maestà panels of small size, in which the court of the Virgin is compressed into the space around the throne. The female saints in the foreground, Lucy and Catherine, are the largest and closest to the viewer, and their richly patterned garments frame the throne at its base.

The panel shows the qualities of Niccolò's small works: the dense gold patterns of the garments, the finely punched haloes, the attentive faces of the saints and the small, precise scene in the gable. The green floor scattered with leaves, painted beneath the throne, gives the heavenly court a meadow, a motif that recurs in other Sienese images of the Virgin in this period.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground of the gable and of the main field is worn, revealing the red bole in many places, and the carved crockets of the frame have lost parts of their gilding. The Virgin's mantle has lost its original colour and modelling. The faces, the Child, the saints and the scene of the Crucifixion are well preserved.

The diptych in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo in L'Aquila comes from the church of Santa Maria la Nova at Cellino Attanasio, in the province of Teramo. It was attributed to Niccolò di Buonaccorso in 1938–39 and is placed around 1380. Its presence in the Abruzzi shows that Niccolò's small devotional works travelled well beyond Siena.

The two panels, each in its moulded gilded frame, are hinged together and could be closed like a book, protecting the paintings when the diptych was carried or stored. Each panel is closed at the top by a cusped arch carved in relief, with foliage in the spandrels. The format, rectangular rather than gabled, and the pairing of a devotional image with a scene of the Passion are typical of the portable diptychs made in Siena for private prayer.

The left panel shows the Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine of Alexandria. The Virgin, in a deep blue mantle, sits on a throne covered with a red and gold cloth whose back rises to a point, and holds the Christ Child on her knee. The Child, in a tunic patterned with gold, leans forward to place a ring on the finger of Catherine, who stands at the left, crowned, in a pink mantle over a gold dress, holding her palm. According to her legend, she saw in a vision the Christ Child accept her as his bride.

At the right of the throne stands Saint John the Baptist, in a camel-hair tunic and a green mantle, pointing towards the Virgin and Child and holding an unrolled scroll. Two angels with gold haloes stand behind the throne. In the foreground, on a floor of pink and white squares in front of a stepped dais, a young haloed figure in a red tunic and a violet mantle kneels in prayer, holding a slender staff. The figure carries no distinctive attribute, and its identity remains uncertain.

The right panel shows the Crucifixion. Christ hangs on the cross against the gold ground, with the titulus above his head, and four small angels painted in blue fly around him in grief. At the left stands the Virgin, in a blue mantle over a red dress, her hands joined and her head bowed. At the right Saint John the Evangelist, in a pink mantle over a blue tunic, looks up at Christ. Mary Magdalene, in a red dress, her long golden hair loose over her shoulders, kneels at the foot of the cross and embraces it. On the rocky ground of Golgotha lie a skull and bones, the remains of Adam according to tradition.

The pairing of the two scenes is meaningful. The Mystic Marriage shows the soul's union with Christ, and the Crucifixion the sacrifice through which that union was made possible. The diptych offered its owner a meditation that moved from the joy of the mystical espousal to the grief of the Passion, with Catherine and the Magdalene as models of devotion to Christ.

The panels show the qualities of Niccolò's small works: the dense gold patterns of the garments, the finely punched haloes, the expressive gestures of the mourners and the clear, ordered space defined by the chequered floor and the dais. The figure of the Magdalene, with her red dress and flowing hair, introduces a note of intense emotion into the restrained composition of the Crucifixion.

The diptych is in good condition. The gold grounds show a fine network of cracks and some wear along the edges, and the frames have lost parts of their gilding. The blue of the Virgin's mantles, the red and pink garments, the faces and the gold patterns are well preserved.

The two wings in the Alte Pinakothek in Munich (WAF 265 and WAF 266) are painted in grisaille, in tones of grey and white, and show crowds of saints. The left wing, on poplar, measures 83.2 by 18.8 centimetres; the right wing, on wood, 82 by 17.6 centimetres. The Bavarian State Painting Collections catalogue them under Niccolò di Buonaccorso, dating the left wing 1375–1399 and the right wing about 1400, while the most recent synthesis of his work places them in the 1370s. Both are documented in the private collection of King Ludwig I of Bavaria by 1829 and have belonged to the Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds since 1923. They are not on display.

Each panel is tall and narrow and closed by a round arch, with a moulded border painted around the field. The museum's title for the right wing records the Virgin of the Annunciation in its upper part, which suggests that the wings were once crowned by gables with the angel Gabriel and the Virgin Annunciate, as in Niccolò's portable triptychs; these upper parts are not included in the available photographs. The size of the panels, more than eighty centimetres high, indicates a larger tabernacle or altarpiece than the small domestic triptychs.

The left wing shows two groups of saints in tiers. In the front rows stand female saints: a young woman with long loose hair holding a small jar, Mary Magdalene with the jar of ointment; a saint holding a book; a crowned saint, probably Catherine of Alexandria; and a veiled older woman. Behind them rise bearded apostles, among them a saint holding a sword, probably Paul, and another with a staff. All turn towards the right, towards the centre of the lost altarpiece.

The right wing shows male saints in three rows, all turning towards the left. In the front stand a friar in a habit, probably Saint Francis; two young deacons, one of whom holds a gridiron and is therefore Saint Lawrence, the other probably Saint Stephen; and an old hermit with a long beard, probably Saint Anthony Abbot. Behind them are bishops in mitres, a saint holding a banner, a hooded monk and other bearded saints, some of them holding books or crosses.

The two crowds, converging from either side, formed the court of heaven around a central image now lost, perhaps a Coronation of the Virgin or a Virgin and Child in glory. The division of the saints by sex, with women and apostles on one side and male saints, clergy and religious on the other, follows the arrangement of the blessed in Paradise in Sienese and Florentine painting of the fourteenth century.

The use of grisaille, painting in grey and white without colour, is unusual for Niccolò, whose small works are known for their bright colours and dense gold. The two wings show that his range extended beyond colour and gold. In the fourteenth century grisaille was used for the exterior of wings, for images seen when an altarpiece was closed and for objects associated with penitential seasons such as Lent, and any of these functions may have applied here.

The drawing of the figures shows Niccolò's precision on a small scale: the faces are individualised, the folds of the garments are described with fine lines and highlights, and the crowding of the figures is controlled by the converging movement of the groups. Without colour, the quality of the drawing and modelling becomes the main means of expression.

The available images are black-and-white photographs, which do not allow the tones of the grisaille to be judged. They show a paint surface crossed by a fine network of cracks, with some losses along the edges and in the lower parts, while the faces and the outlines of the figures remain clearly legible.

The Annunciation in the Wadsworth Atheneum in Hartford (1951.94) measures 39.1 by 26.4 centimetres for the panel and 46.4 by 31.8 centimetres with its frame. The museum catalogues it under Niccolò di Buonaccorso, dated 1370–1388. It was possibly owned by Oliver B. James in Phoenix and passed to J. Weitzner in New York, from whom the museum acquired it by exchange in 1951 through the Ella Gallup Sumner and Mary Catlin Sumner Collection Fund. It was shown in 1997–1998 at the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego in the exhibition "Art and Devotion in Siena after 1350: Luca di Tommè and Niccolò di Buonaccorso".

The panel forms a diptych with the Lamentation in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum (1975.1.20), which the Metropolitan catalogues as "Workshop of Niccolò di Buonaccorso". The most recent synthesis of Niccolò's work places both panels in a subgroup of works with his stylistic characteristics but of lesser execution. The museum's wall text agrees with this view, while its catalogue record keeps the name of the master.

The scene is set in a house of grey stone. An upper balcony with a balustrade runs across the top, and below it an open loggia of round arches, with blue vaults, divides the space into two parts. The angel kneels in the open courtyard at the left, while the Virgin stands within the loggia at the right. The museum observes that the artist sought to create a sense of space through pattern and line, in the bedspread and the flooring, an effect frequent in Sienese painting of the period.

The angel Gabriel, with golden wings and a punched halo, wears a pink robe and a gold tunic, and kneels on a floor of red and white squares. He holds an unrolled scroll inscribed "Ave Maria Gratia Plena", the words of his greeting. At the upper left a small figure in red sends a golden beam diagonally across the scene towards the Virgin, the ray through which the Holy Spirit descends at the moment of the Incarnation.

The Virgin stands before a red cloth of honour patterned with gold, in a dark blue mantle over a dress densely patterned with gold. She raises her right hand to her breast in a gesture of humility and acceptance, and holds a small red book in her left hand, the book in which, according to tradition, she was reading the prophecy of Isaiah when the angel appeared.

Behind the loggia opens a room with a bed covered by a blue and white checked spread, set against a red wall. The bed, the chequered floor and the furnishings place the event in a domestic interior, a feature that the museum connects with the use of small folding altarpieces in the home: the owners of such a diptych could recognise their own house in the Virgin's.

The museum's catalogue describes the medium as tempera and oil. Oil in a Sienese panel of this date would be exceptional, and the description needs technical confirmation. The composition and the types follow Niccolò's models, but the drawing of the figures and the architecture is simpler than in his autograph works, which supports the attribution of the diptych to his workshop.

The panel is in good condition. The gold of the frame and of the background is worn in places, and the paint surface shows a fine network of cracks. The colours of the garments, the patterned floor and bedspread and the inscription on the scroll remain clearly legible.

The Lamentation over the Dead Christ in the Robert Lehman Collection of the Metropolitan Museum (1975.1.20) measures 41 by 26.7 centimetres overall and 39.4 by 25.4 centimetres for the painted surface. The Metropolitan catalogues it as the work of the workshop of Niccolò di Buonaccorso and dates it about 1380. It forms a diptych with the Annunciation in Hartford, of the same size and framing.

The two panels, the Annunciation and the Lamentation, frame the life of Christ between its beginning and its end: the announcement of the Incarnation and the mourning over the body taken down from the cross. Closed like a book, the diptych could be carried and opened for private prayer, and its owner moved in meditation from the joy of the Annunciation to the grief of the Passion.

The scene is set at the foot of Golgotha, represented as a rocky hill from which the empty cross rises against the gold ground. Four small angels fly around the cross in grief, two painted dark and two red, their bodies foreshortened as they plunge downwards. The cusped arch carved in relief, with foliage in the spandrels, closes the field as in the Hartford panel.

The dead Christ lies on the ground in the foreground, his pale body marked with wounds, his legs covered by a cloth patterned with gold. The Virgin, in a dark mantle, holds his head and bends over him. Beside her a woman with long golden hair, in a red dress and a dark mantle, throws her arms wide in a gesture of lament, and a veiled woman in brown joins her hands in prayer. Mary Magdalene, in a red dress, her long hair loose, kneels at Christ's feet and touches them, recalling the anointing of his feet in the Gospels.

At the left a woman in a yellow mantle sits on the ground with her back half turned, and Saint John the Evangelist, in a red mantle over a dark tunic, stands with his hands open and his eyes raised. At the right stands an old bearded man in a pink and yellow mantle, holding a dark jar of ointment for the anointing of the body; he is usually identified as Nicodemus or Joseph of Arimathea. Beside him a figure in green is seen from behind, turned towards the cross.

The composition, with the body laid horizontally across the foreground and the mourners arranged around it, follows a type of Lamentation widespread in Sienese painting after the example of Pietro Lorenzetti. The expressive gestures, the outspread arms and the grieving faces convey intense emotion within the small format.

The drawing is broader and simpler than in Niccolò's autograph works. The faces are less finely modelled, the draperies are rendered in larger, flatter areas of colour, and the gold patterns are fewer and less dense.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is heavily worn, revealing the red bole over large areas, especially around the cross and in the upper part, and the edges of the panel are damaged. The figures, the colours of the garments and the faces of the mourners are well preserved.

The small Crucifixion in the Galleria Nazionale dell'Umbria in Perugia is assigned to Niccolò di Buonaccorso and dated about 1380–1388, in the last decade of his activity. It is a self-contained panel for private devotion, in a moulded gilded frame, without gable or wings. Its presence in Perugia, like that of the diptych from Cellino Attanasio in L'Aquila, shows that Niccolò's small panels circulated beyond Siena.

The gold ground is framed by a punched border of small rosettes that runs along the edges of the field, a decoration that turns the gold into a precious surface around the scene. The rectangular format and the plain frame give the panel the character of a devotional image to be held or set on a small altar, close to the eye of the viewer.

Christ hangs on a tall cross that rises from the lower edge to the top of the field. His body is slender and pale, with a long, curved torso and thin legs, his head bowed on his right shoulder under a punched halo. Blood flows from the wounds of his hands, his side and his feet, and runs down the cross to the ground below. The figure has the delicacy of Sienese Crucifixions descending from Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti.

Two small angels with spread wings fly at either side of the cross. The angel at the left holds a chalice towards the wound in Christ's side, collecting the blood that flows from it, an image of the Eucharist that refers to the sacrifice renewed in the Mass. The angel at the right raises its hands in grief. Their wings, painted in tones of gold and dark blue, stand out against the gold ground.

At the foot of the cross the Virgin and Saint John sit on the ground, in the posture of humility, and look up at Christ with joined hands. The Virgin wears a dark mantle, now darkened and abraded, and John a pink mantle over a blue tunic. Their seated poses, low on the ground, differ from the standing mourners of most Crucifixions and give the scene a tone of intimate, contemplative grief.

Between them a small figure in a red cardinal's robe and hood kneels and embraces the foot of the cross, where the blood reaches the ground. The figure has been identified with Saint Bonaventure, the Franciscan theologian and cardinal whose writings on the Passion encouraged this kind of meditation. A cardinal saint in red at the foot of the cross may also be Saint Jerome, often shown in cardinal's dress, and the identification remains open.

The composition, reduced to a few figures set against the gold, invites the viewer to take the place of the kneeling figure at the foot of the cross, embracing it in prayer. The panel belongs to the devotional culture of the late fourteenth century, in which images of the Passion were used to guide private meditation on Christ's suffering.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is worn and crossed by cracks, the Virgin's dark mantle has lost much of its colour and modelling, and the frame shows losses of gilding. The body of Christ, the faces of the mourners, the angels and the red robe of the kneeling figure are well preserved.

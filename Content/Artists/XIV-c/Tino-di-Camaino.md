## The Presence of the Absent

*Tino di Camaino* occupies a distinctive position in the history of Italian sculpture. Working during the opening decades of the fourteenth century, he developed an artistic language capable of joining the physical gravity of the human body to the elaborate religious and political meanings of monumental commemoration. His figures possess density. Even when their gestures are restrained, their bodies seem to command the surrounding space through the breadth of their shoulders, the concentration of their movements, and the substantial folds of their garments. Yet this solidity rarely becomes mere heaviness. Tino’s sculpture repeatedly makes weight expressive, transforming the material resistance of marble into an image of authority, vulnerability, affection, or spiritual expectation.

His career unfolded across several of the most important artistic environments of medieval Italy: Pisa, Siena, Florence, and Naples. These cities provided different institutional settings and different expectations of sculpture. Cathedral workshops, ecclesiastical patrons, urban guilds, and the Angevin court all required images through which identity could be preserved and publicly understood. In two of these cities Tino also held the office of *capomaestro*, the master responsible for a cathedral’s fabric, and in Naples he directed royal building sites. He was therefore never only a carver of figures. His achievement lay partly in his ability to respond to such varied circumstances without losing the coherence of his sculptural language. The emperor, bishop, cardinal, queen, and Virgin inhabit different orders of existence, but his treatment of their bodies gives each a persuasive presence.

The funerary monument became his most consequential field of invention. A tomb had to accommodate demands that were related but not identical. It preserved the memory of an individual, marked the location of mortal remains, supported prayers for the deceased, and communicated claims about office, family, virtue, and salvation. Tino organized these demands through architecture and sculpture. The recumbent or seated effigy, the narrative relief, the supporting figure, the opened curtain, and the heavenly intercessor became parts of a coordinated argument about death.

An assessment of his work must also acknowledge how much has changed since its creation. Several major monuments were dismantled, relocated, reduced, or reconstructed. Detached fragments now stand in museums, where their formal qualities are readily appreciated but their original relationships are more difficult to recover. The surviving sculpture therefore offers both substantial evidence and unavoidable uncertainty. This essay treats the works themselves, the published documentary record, institutional object records, and a small number of scholarly studies as its foundation. Its central argument is that Tino’s importance emerges most clearly when sculptural form, devotional purpose, and the public construction of memory are considered together.

## Artistic Identity and the Conditions of a Mobile Career

Tino was a Sienese sculptor and architect active in the first third of the fourteenth century. His birth is generally placed around 1280, probably in Siena; his mother’s name is unknown. The end of his life is equally unsettled, and the reasons deserve stating rather than being replaced by a false precision. On 11 July 1336 two Neapolitan documents name his successor on the royal building sites and refer to Tino as *quondam*, “the late,” which suggests a sudden death. Against this, one document has been read as mentioning him alive on 2 June 1337, while the first secure notice of his death dates from 2 January 1338. Catalogues therefore oscillate between 1336 and 1337, and both dates have documentary support. The outline of his professional movement is considerably firmer than these endpoints: he worked in Pisa, returned to Siena, undertook important commissions in Florence, and spent his final years in Naples.

His name identifies him through his father, [Camaino di Crescentino](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Camaino-di-Crescentino.html), a Sienese master builder who spent decades among the senior masters of the cathedral workshop of Siena. This familial identity deserves attention. Medieval artistic production depended on the transmission of practical knowledge through workshops and personal relationships. Cutting stone, calculating the dimensions of architectural members, organizing assistants, and coordinating installation were skills learned through sustained participation in collective work. Tino’s mature monuments reveal a command of precisely these interconnected activities. Their success depends on the fit between carved bodies and architectural structures, on the relationship between physical support and visual emphasis, and on the ability to produce a coherent whole from numerous separately worked elements.

A frequently repeated claim makes Tino a pupil of [Giovanni Pisano](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giovanni-Pisano.html) who helped his master on the façade of Siena Cathedral and then followed him to Pisa. None of this is documented. Tino’s name does not appear in the abundant records concerning Giovanni’s works, and in Pisa he occupied a role closer to that of an alternative to Giovanni than of an assistant. The earliest archival mention of him is a Pisan notarial deed of 19 October 1312, in which he stands surety for the wife of a painter called Piastra. Proposals that he contributed to Giovanni’s pulpits, including <a href="/scholars.html#kreytenberg-gert">Gert Kreytenberg</a>’s suggestion of his hand in the *Adoration of the Magi* at Pistoia, remain hypotheses grounded in style.

Tino’s own words point elsewhere. In two signed works he names his father with conspicuous respect, and in the inscription of the d’Orso tomb in Florence he declares that he declines the title of *magister* while his father lives. Such statements suggest an education alongside Camaino in the Sienese cathedral workshop. The undeniable Giovannesque features in Tino’s early sculpture can be explained by the culture Giovanni and his father [Nicola](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Nicola-Pisano.html) had left in Siena after long periods of work there. The relationship with Giovanni remains important, but it is best treated as a matter of artistic choices rather than as a documented story of master and pupil.

Where Giovanni’s figures can appear intensely activated by twisting movement and abrupt changes of direction, Tino often concentrates expression within a more compact bodily structure. This difference is visible from his first accepted work, and it persists. Geographical mobility then gave it new tasks. Moving between cities brought a sculptor into contact with different buildings, patrons, materials, and visual expectations. A cathedral commission could require sensitivity to an existing liturgical arrangement; a royal tomb could demand a more explicit statement of hereditary authority. Tino’s repeated use of curtains, supporting figures, and superimposed registers demonstrates how an established repertoire could remain flexible in new institutional settings.

Mobility should not be romanticized as unrestricted freedom. Large commissions depended on patrons who controlled money, access, and space, and in Tino’s case they also depended on politics. As will be seen, his departure from Pisa followed a war in which he fought on the opposing side. Nevertheless, the recurrence of his distinctive treatment of volume across very different commissions indicates that patronal requirements did not erase artistic agency. His career reveals a productive tension between a recognizable sculptural intelligence and the institutions that employed it.

## The Sculptural Language of Weight, Restraint, and Relation

The most immediately recognizable quality of Tino’s sculpture is its concentration of mass. His figures often appear gathered around a stable bodily centre. Drapery may spread broadly across the knees, shoulders may establish a firm horizontal extension, and the head may emerge from the garment with a compact, almost architectural clarity. This does not mean that the human body has disappeared beneath clothing. The cloth becomes a means of making bodily presence legible: its folds translate the figure’s occupation of space into patterns that can be perceived from a distance.

Weight is particularly important because so many of his works belonged to elevated settings. A figure placed above the viewer must communicate through its principal divisions before smaller details become visible. Broad modelling and clearly organized silhouettes can therefore serve practical as well as expressive purposes. Tino’s concentration on substantial forms makes the figure readable as part of a monument. At the same time, those forms suggest endurance. The carved body seems capable of resisting the instability of the world around it, even when it represents someone whose physical life has ended.

His treatment of gesture is equally consequential. A hand extended toward a petitioner, an arm supporting a child, or a head inclined in death does not need to be expansive to become expressive. Meaning arises from the relation between the gesture and the figure’s otherwise stable structure. Small movements acquire force because they interrupt a condition of composure. The viewer notices the precise direction of a hand or the slight deviation of a head because the surrounding body provides a controlled frame.

Faces likewise depend on the organization of a few decisive features. The set of the eyes, the projection of the nose, the compression of the mouth, and the breadth of the cheeks can establish a strong impression of character. Such effects should not automatically be equated with portrait likeness. A face may seem individual because its forms are unusually specific, yet the historical person’s actual appearance may remain unknown. Tino’s sculpture encourages an experience of personal presence without always allowing the historian to determine how closely that presence corresponds to observed physiognomy.

Restraint also shapes the emotional atmosphere of his work. Grief, affection, and reverence are frequently conveyed through bodily proximity and controlled inclination rather than dramatic expression. Figures turn, bend, support, receive, or remain still. Their meanings emerge through contemplation, as the viewer traces relationships within the group.

This style suits funerary sculpture particularly well. A tomb must sustain attention across repeated encounters, not merely illustrate a single action. Tino’s figures can remain solemn without becoming inert because their stability contains the possibility of relation. The dead are surrounded by beings who attend them; petitioners approach sacred figures; supporting bodies carry the structure above. The monument becomes an arrangement of obligations and responses, and its emotional force resides in these connections as much as in any isolated face.

## Pisa Before the Emperor: The San Ranieri Altar, the Font, and the Office of Capomaestro

Tino’s career begins in Pisa, and it begins with a saint rather than a ruler. The first work generally accepted as his is the *arca-altare* of San Ranieri, the city’s patron, made for the cathedral and certainly complete by 1312. It was commissioned by Burgundio di Tado, the *operaio* who administered the cathedral works from 1298 to 1319. A charter of 1306, in which he reserved the right to appoint the chaplain of an altar of Ranieri, has traditionally been read as the date by which the work was finished, but that reading is disputed. Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> already knew it inside the cathedral. Over the following centuries it moved between the cathedral and the Camposanto, and it now stands in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. In the early 1450s Andrea Guardi enclosed it in a new marble frontispiece; in 1539 the altar was reduced in depth, and in 1591 a new altar by Giovan Battista Lorenzi replaced it. The painted polychromy has gone, although red marble inlays survive in the frames and grounds.

The work is nonetheless substantially intact, and it supplies an unusual piece of evidence about itself: its own reliefs represent the *arca*, showing how the ensemble was meant to look. A chest with three figured reliefs is suspended on four corbels above the altar. Above it rises a large cusped dossal with the Virgin and Child enthroned. Ranieri, in his penitential hair garment, and a second saint present two kneeling donors, one of whom is very probably Burgundio. The whole functions as a carved equivalent of a great painted altarpiece. Several details depend on Giovanni Pisano’s models, but the reliefs already prefer a softer, less dramatic plasticity. Here the compact Tino who would later carve emperors and queens is first visible. Its original position in the cathedral is also debated: older studies placed it in the right transept, later ones at the end of the north aisle, and the most recent argues for the north arm of the transept. Even this early monument has a history of displacement.

In 1312 Tino signed a second commission from Burgundio di Tado: a baptismal font for the right transept of the cathedral. Vasari, who knew the sculptor under the garbled name of “Lino,” credited him with both the font and the marble chapel of San Ranieri. The font was gravely damaged in the fire of 1595, and only a handful of small figured fragments and pieces of framing have resurfaced. A sixteenth-century manuscript preserves its inscriptions. In the longest, Tino praised his patron and described himself as *Tini sculptoris de Senis arte coloris*, a phrase that has led some to suppose that he also worked as a painter. No other evidence supports that supposition, and the phrase may describe the coloured finish of the carving rather than a second profession. The fragments are being gradually reassembled into a reconstruction, and further pieces may still emerge from museum storage.

Other works belong to the same Pisan years. A refined, signed *Virgin and Child* now in the Museo Civico of Turin bears an inscription naming Siena and Camaino as the city and the father that begot the sculptor. An unfinished *Virgin and Child* and an unfinished relief with the *Annunciation*, *Nativity*, and *Annunciation to the Shepherds* survive in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo; the relief may be a panel from a pulpit never completed. Tino may also have continued the figured plinth that ran around the cathedral’s base, begun by Giovanni Pisano. Together these works show a young master trusted with the cathedral’s most prominent liturgical furnishings.

By February 1315 Tino held the office of *capomaestro* of the Opera del Duomo. When he received it is unknown. Many handbooks state that he succeeded Giovanni Pisano in 1311, but no published document establishes that year, and the most careful recent account declines to give a starting date. The office matters more than its date. A *capomaestro* was responsible for the fabric and its workforce, not merely for his own carving. The appointment explains why the Opera turned to him for the most politically charged commission of its age: the tomb of an emperor who had died while the city’s hopes rested upon him.

This is the first work that the critical tradition unanimously gives to Tino di Camaino. It is a funerary *arca* conceived as an altar, with the tomb chest raised above the altar table. It was made for the relics of Ranieri, the twelfth-century hermit who died in 1161 and became Pisa’s patron saint. The patron was Burgundio di Tado, *operaio* of the Opera del Duomo from 1298 to 1319, and two inscriptions on the cathedral’s exterior walls record his commission. Vasari already knew the monument inside the cathedral. Its importance is twofold: it opens Tino’s catalogue, and it shows a young Sienese master entrusted with the shrine of the city’s own saint.

The upper zone reads as a sculpted altarpiece. The Virgin sits on a throne beneath a pointed gable, holding the Child on her knee, while two angels lean in at the shoulders of the throne. The axis rises from the Virgin to the dove of the Holy Spirit and the hand of God emerging at the apex. The Trinity is thus set in direct relation to the Virgin and Child, an uncommon pairing. One reading takes the conjunction as deliberate, but later comparable examples are few and differently arranged, so a precise iconographic interpretation remains open.

The scene is a *commendatio animae*, a presentation of souls to the Virgin, and it is atypical in form. Ranieri stands on the left, unmistakable in the rough penitential garment that his legend made his emblem. He presents a kneeling figure in a cap, toward whom the Virgin reaches. On the right, a bearded saint, not yet securely identified, lays his hand on a second, smaller kneeling figure. One of the two donors is very probably Burgundio. The other has been tentatively identified as the archpriest Iacopo Maccaione Gualandi, but neither attribution is certain. All the figures stand on the same ground plane as the throne, so the heavenly court and the Pisan patrons share one space.

The lower register works like a predella in three compartments, framed by small columns crowned with Gothic finials. On the left is the offering of the monument itself. Ranieri appears beside the miniature arca, a tiny gabled chest raised above an altar. In the centre, the saint is enthroned frontally in his hair garment between two kneeling devotees, a cult image within the monument. On the right is the translation of his remains, with figures depositing relics into the same gabled arca. The monument thus represents its own commissioning and its own liturgical purpose. For the historian the self-representation is invaluable, because it records how the ensemble was meant to look.

Stylistically, the work depends on Giovanni Pisano in its figure types and in some of its drapery. Its plasticity is already Tino’s own: softer, more compact, and less dramatic than Giovanni’s. The Virgin’s mass is gathered into a single seated block, and the folds over her knees spread horizontally rather than twisting. The donors’ kneeling bodies are short and dense, their heads set firmly on the shoulders. The cherubs in the lateral niches, with full cheeks and overlapping feathers, have the rounded solidity that will mark the angels of the later tombs. The concentration of volume familiar from the Henry VII<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-henry-vii" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> “councillors” is visible here in embryo.

The surface preserves one element of the original colour scheme: the red-veined marble set into the pilasters, the frames, and the backgrounds of the lower compartments. The painted polychromy has been lost, together with the original altar beneath. The dark recesses and pale figures we see today were once part of a coloured ensemble. The red stone gives some idea of how chromatic contrast articulated the architecture. It also anticipates Tino’s Pisan self-description as a sculptor *arte coloris* on the font of 1312.

The dating rests on two kinds of evidence. A charter of 1306 shows the *operaio* reserving the right to appoint the chaplain of an altar dedicated to Ranieri, and it has traditionally served as a date by which the arca was finished. That reading is disputed. Two further archival extracts confirm the intention to raise the monument and show that it was complete by 1312. A bracket of c. 1306–1312 states what the documents support. The earlier date of c. 1300 has no documentary basis.

The monument’s later history is one of repeated reframing. Between 1451 and 1454 Andrea Guardi carved a new frontispiece to enclose it, and in 1539 the altar was reduced in depth. In 1591 Giovan Battista Lorenzi’s new altar at the end of the north aisle replaced it, and the relics stayed there until 1688. That year they were moved to the end wall of the south transept, where they remain. The arca then moved between the cathedral and the Camposanto before entering the Museo dell’Opera. Its original position is debated: older scholarship placed it in the right transept, later work at the end of the north aisle. The most recent study argues for the north arm of the transept, perhaps near its east wall, close to the sanctuary.

This statuette is one of the few works signed by Tino di Camaino, and the signature makes it a key document of his early career. It belongs to the Pisan years. The museum dates it to 1312–1315, the period of the baptismal font and the tomb of Henry VII. It came to the Museo Civico of Turin from the D’Azeglio collection; its earlier history is unknown. A closely similar but unfinished *Virgin and Child* in the Museo Nazionale di San Matteo in Pisa, also attributed to Tino, shows that the type was developed in that city.

The inscription runs around the octagonal base in Gothic capitals. It has been transcribed as

> VIRGINIS AT TINO FUIT OH Q[U]A(M) CERNIS IMAGHO QUAM GENUERE SEIE CAMAINUS PATER Q[U]AM MAGIS[T]RO.

The Latin is irregular, but its sense is clear: the image of the Virgin that the viewer sees is by Tino, whom Siena and his father Camaino, a master, begot. As in the later d’Orso tomb, Tino names both his native city and his father in his signature, a declaration of origin and of filial respect rare among sculptors of his time.

The Virgin stands upright, her body turned slightly in a gentle Gothic sway as she supports the Child on her left arm. She wears an open crown over a veil that frames her face, and she turns her head toward her son with a grave, tender expression. With her right hand she lifts the hem of her mantle, drawing it across her body in deep diagonal folds. The edges of the mantle are carved with a pierced, lace-like border that catches the light and enlivens the large simple surfaces of the drapery.

The Child is the most lively element of the group. He sits high on his mother’s arm, nude to the waist, a cloth wrapped around his legs. He rests his right arm on her shoulder, his hand touching the brooch that fastens her mantle, and holds a small rolled object in his left hand. Turning his head, he looks up at her and smiles. The exchange of glances creates an intimate relation between mother and child that is the real subject of the statue.

The work depends closely on the standing Madonnas of Giovanni Pisano, which Tino would have known in Pisa: the pose, the lifted mantle, and the intense exchange of glances all derive from them. Tino’s version, however, is calmer and more courtly. The forms are fuller and softer, the drapery less agitated, and the expressions more serene. The statue already shows the qualities that would characterize his later work: a compact volume, a smooth and refined surface, and an emotional register of gentle, inward tenderness.

Traces of polychromy and gilding survive on the surface. They indicate that the statuette was originally coloured, with gilded borders and perhaps a painted mantle, as were most marble sculptures of the period. Its small size, fine finish, and the detail of its carving suggest a devotional image for close viewing, perhaps for a private patron or a chapel. The signature, unusual on a work of this scale, shows how much the young sculptor valued it.

### The Man of Sorrows from the Opera del Duomo of Pisa

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-03.jpg" alt="A white marble relief in a rectangular frame. Under a pointed arch carried on two slender colonnettes, the half-length figure of the dead Christ stands out from a dark ground. His head falls toward his right shoulder, his eyes are almost closed and his mouth slightly open. Long, wavy hair and a curling beard are deeply drilled. His torso is bare and lean, the ribs and breastbone marked in shallow ridges, with a small wound in his side. His forearms are crossed at the wrists in front of his body, and the back of the upper hand shows two round nail holes. A band of cloth wraps his hips at the lower edge of the relief.">
  <figcaption>Attributed to Tino di Camaino, Christ as Man of Sorrows, c. 1300–1325. Marble. Pisa, Palazzo dell’Opera della Primaziale.</figcaption>
</figure>

The relief shows Christ as the Man of Sorrows, the *Imago Pietatis*: the dead Christ rising at half length, his body still bearing the wounds of the Passion. The type, which came to Italy from Byzantine devotional icons, spread rapidly in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. It appeared on altarpieces, on the fronts of tombs, and in images for private prayer. It shows neither a narrative moment nor the risen Christ in glory, but the suffering body offered to the contemplation of the faithful.

Christ appears under a pointed arch carried on two slender colonnettes with small foliate capitals, set within a rectangular frame. His head falls heavily toward his right shoulder, the eyes almost closed, the mouth slightly open. The hair and beard are carved in long, wavy locks, drilled deeply so that the strands stand out in light and shadow. The torso is lean and bare, the ribs and breastbone modelled in shallow ridges, and a small cut in the side marks the lance wound. The forearms are crossed at the wrists in front of the body, and the back of the upper hand shows two round nail holes. A band of cloth wraps the hips along the lower edge.

The crossed arms are the most expressive feature of the composition. They close the figure on itself and give it a stillness that is both bodily and spiritual. They also bring both wounded hands to the centre of the image, where the viewer’s gaze falls first. The inclined head and the crossed arms create a strong diagonal and a counter-movement, so that the composition, though symmetrical in its frame, is never rigid. The polished surface of the flesh, against the dark ground and the deep shadows of the hair, intensifies the impression of a pale, lifeless body.

The relief’s original function is uncertain. Reliefs of the Man of Sorrows under an arch occupied the centre of many tomb fronts in Tuscany and Naples. One of Tino’s Neapolitan tombs, that of Bartolomeo di Capua, may have had such an image in its central medallion, and the type also crowned altarpieces and tabernacles. A provenance from a dismantled tomb or altar in Pisa Cathedral is plausible but not documented.

The attribution and the date both require caution. The dating to the first quarter of the fourteenth century places the relief in Tino’s Pisan years, and its compact volume, smooth flesh, and calm gravity are compatible with his style. The deeply drilled hair and beard recall the head of the Baptist in Florence. The relief could, however, also belong to the large Pisan workshop that continued Giovanni Pisano’s tradition in the same years. The attribution should therefore be read as proposed rather than certain: it has not been verified against the museum’s catalogue or the specialist literature.

## Henry VII at Pisa: The Emperor’s Body and the Persistence of Office

### The Tomb of Emperor Henry VII in Pisa Cathedral: Effigy, Apostles, and the Epitaph of 1494

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-04.jpg" alt="A white marble tomb set against a cathedral wall. At the top, on the cornice against a pale wall and beneath a framed painted panel, the effigy of the emperor lies on his back, his head on a large cushion and his body covered to the feet by a long mantle. Below, the front of the sarcophagus shows twelve standing apostles side by side under a row of round arches, with small carved heads between the arches. Panels of red marble close the chest at both ends, and a thin red marble band runs beneath it. Under a heavy moulded cornice, a deep base carries a five-line Latin inscription in Roman capitals.">
  <figcaption>Tomb of Emperor Henry VII, 1313–1315: effigy and sarcophagus with the Twelve Apostles (one a modern replacement). Marble with red marble inlays. Reassembled in 1494 with the inscribed base and recomposed in 1921. Pisa, Cathedral, east wall of the right transept.</figcaption>
</figure>

The photograph shows the part of Henry VII’s tomb that remains in Pisa Cathedral, in the arrangement it has had since 1921. Three horizontal zones are stacked one above the other. The effigy lies at the top, on the cornice and beneath a framed painted panel. Below it is the chest carved with the Twelve Apostles under a continuous arcade, framed by red marble panels. At the bottom, a broad moulded base carries an inscribed epitaph. Only the effigy and the chest are Tino’s. The base and cornices belong to the Renaissance reinstallation, and the whole should be read as a fifteenth-century reassembly of fourteenth-century parts rather than as the medieval monument.

The tomb established one of the central problems of Tino’s career: how could sculpture acknowledge the death of a ruler while preserving the authority attached to his position? Henry died near Buonconvento on 24 August 1313, the feast of Saint Bartholomew. The agreement between Tino and the Opera del Duomo was drawn up on 12 February 1315. The work was probably already under way before that date and may have been planned soon after the emperor’s death. Tino collected the weekly pay due to him, with which he presumably paid his collaborators. The monument stood in the main apse by July 1315, together with painted decoration on the surrounding wall, now lost.

Tino never collected the final payment. In the summer of 1315 Pisa’s war with the Guelf<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guelphs" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> league, of which Siena was a member, came to a head at the battle of Montecatini on 29 August. Tino left the cathedral works and fought with the Guelf army against the Pisans. The consequence arrived seven years later. In July 1322 the Council of Elders formally deprived him of the office of *capomaestro*, *cum sit guelfus*: because he was a Guelf and had fought against the Pisans in the army and battle of Montecatini. The council also ruled that no Guelf should hold the post in future. The sculptor of the Ghibelline<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ghibellines" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> emperor’s tomb had fought on the other side. Few episodes reveal so clearly how an artist’s career could be bound to the politics of the cities that employed him.

The monument itself did not survive intact. In 1494 it was dismantled, and part of it was reassembled on the east wall of the right transept within a new decorative setting. The surviving pieces then moved repeatedly between cathedral and Camposanto until the 1921 recomposition returned them to the fifteenth-century position, where they remain. The chest certainly belonged to the tomb; eleven of its apostles are original and one is a modern replacement. So did the effigy, two *Annunciation* statuettes, two acolytes, and two angels holding scrolls inscribed in Henry’s praise, recognized only recently while still serving as finials on the cathedral façade. Fragments of carved twisted columns in the Museo dell’Opera and the Victoria and Albert Museum in London complete the core group. Gert Kreytenberg’s study of the monument made reconstruction and the relationship between these components central questions, and no proposed reconstruction has yet won general assent.

The base now carries a Latin epitaph whose regular Roman capitals belong not to the fourteenth century but to the humanist culture of the 1494 recomposition:

> HOC · IN SARCOPHAGO · NON QVIDEM · SPERNENDO · HENRICI · OLIM · LVCENBVRGENSIS · COMITIS · ET · POSTHEC · SEPTIMI · EIVS · NOMINIS · ROMANORVM · IMPERATORIS · OSSA · CONTINENTVR · QVE SECVNDO · POST · EIVS · FATVM · ANNO · VIDELICET · M · CCC · XV · DIE · VERO · XXV · SEXTILIS · PISAS · TRANSLATA · SVMMO · CVM · HONORE · ET · FVNERE · HOC · IN PHANO · ADHVC · VSQ(VE) · DIEM · COLLOCATA · PERMANSERE

“In this sarcophagus, by no means to be despised, are contained the bones of Henry, formerly Count of Luxembourg and afterwards Emperor of the Romans, seventh of that name; in the second year after his death, namely in 1315, on the twenty-fifth day of August, they were brought to Pisa with the highest honour and funeral rites, and they have remained laid in this temple to the present day.”

The phrase *non quidem spernendo* defends the old monument at the very moment it was being reduced. The epitaph’s chronology needs care. Henry’s body was brought to Pisa and buried in the cathedral soon after his death in 1313. The date of 25 August 1315, almost exactly two years later, is best understood as the solemn deposition of the remains in Tino’s finished tomb. It falls four days before the battle of Montecatini, in which the tomb’s sculptor fought against Pisa.

The apostles gave the imperial tomb an ecclesiastical frame. They stand as a single college, differentiated by turns of the head, the fall of their heavy mantles, and a few attributes; Peter’s keys are visible near the centre. Henry’s body rests above them, as the bodies of bishops and saints rested above apostolic chests. In the photograph the effigy can be read only in profile: the head raised on a thick cushion, the long body wrapped in a mantle whose patterned surface includes small imperial eagles.

The recumbent image establishes the emperor’s mortality. Whatever powers Henry exercised during life, his sculpted body lies horizontally, subject to the condition shared by all human beings. Yet the effigy does more than record physical cessation. The richly patterned mantle preserves social identity, while the deliberate ordering of the body gives death a public and ceremonial form. The figure remains recognizably the body of an emperor.

Most scholars now accept that the statue of the enthroned emperor, now in the Museo dell’Opera, also belonged to the monument, together with the figures conventionally called his “councillors.” Four of these survive complete in the same museum; of two others only the heads remain, one in the museum and one at the Villa Reale of Marlia. Their exact identity is unresolved. The usual hypothesis places the monument on the wall above an altar dedicated to Saint Bartholomew, with the chest and effigy on one level and the enthroned emperor among his companions on a higher one. Their precise arrangement remains debated, but the relationship between the two images is intellectually significant. One refers to a mortal individual; the other makes visible the dignity of office. Their coexistence allows the monument to hold together two conditions that ordinary experience separates: the ruler has died, but imperial authority can still be represented as present.

The “councillors” reinforce the social character of that authority. Their association with the enthroned ruler presents power as something embodied in a surrounding human order. Their massive, simplified forms, among Tino’s most admired sculptures in modern times, show his style moving toward a definition of the figure by large plastic blocks. That simplification may owe something to the speed at which the commission was executed, a practical factor too easily forgotten when stylistic development is narrated.

The tomb’s placement within a cathedral also matters. Imperial commemoration took place inside a building organized around Christian worship, above an altar and upon a chest of apostles. This did not simply diminish political authority. It provided a framework in which that authority could claim legitimacy while acknowledging dependence upon divine judgment. The surviving work therefore resists a purely triumphal interpretation. A ruler’s memorial can proclaim dignity precisely because death has made dignity vulnerable. In Pisa that vulnerability was political as well as human, because the emperor’s death had ended the hopes the city had placed in him.

### The Emperor and His Court: Henry VII Enthroned among His Councillors

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-05.jpg" alt="A museum display of white marble statues against a dark wall. At the centre, on a raised plinth, a crowned man sits on a throne. He wears a tall pointed crown, and his mantle falls in deep folds over his knees; his right hand is raised and his left rests near a round object on his lap. Two standing men flank him on each side. The figure at far left wears a hooded mantle and looks upward; the next has long hair and holds a small object; the two on the right wear plain ankle-length tunics, one hand laid on the chest. At both ends stand broken column shafts carved with spiralling foliage and small figures. Above, on dark angled brackets, are four smaller statues: two young figures gathering their robes, a curly-haired youth holding a book, and, at the far right, a veiled woman with a hand on her breast. On a separate bracket at right is a single male head.">
  <figcaption>Emperor Henry VII Enthroned with Four Councillors, with associated statues, a councillor’s head, and fragments of twisted columns from the tomb of Henry VII, 1313–1315. Marble. Pisa, Museo dell’Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

The display gathers in one space the components of Henry VII’s tomb that no longer stand in the cathedral. At the centre is the enthroned emperor, flanked by the four complete standing figures conventionally called his “councillors.” A fifth head stands on a bracket at the right. Fragments of carved twisted columns frame the group at both ends, and four smaller statues associated with the monument stand on brackets above. The arrangement is a museum presentation, not a reconstruction: no description or image survives of the tomb before it was dismantled in 1494, and its original form remains a matter of debate.

The enthroned figure presents Henry not as a corpse but as a ruler in majesty. He sits frontally, crowned with a tall pointed diadem rimmed with fleurons, his mantle drawn diagonally across the body and falling in broad, deep folds between the knees. The right hand is raised, and the left rests beside a round object on the lap, presumably the imperial orb. The face is broad and individualized, without idealization. Most scholars accept that the statue belonged to the tomb, although it has also been read as an independent image of the ruler, and its exact position within the monument is unknown. The usual hypothesis places it on an upper level, above the chest and effigy that remain in the cathedral.

The four standing men are among Tino’s most admired sculptures. Their bodies are reduced to large closed volumes. The long garments fall almost straight to the feet, broken only by a few heavy folds, and the gestures are minimal: a hand laid on the chest, a hand holding a small object, an arm hanging at the side. The heads, with short or shoulder-length hair and plain, fleshy faces, are set squarely on the shoulders and turned slightly toward the emperor or upward. The figure at the far left, in a hooded mantle, differs in dress from the others, which suggests differences of rank or office within the group.

The identity of these figures is unresolved. They have been read as counsellors or electors of the empire, as members of the imperial household, or as representatives of the Pisan commune. Six figures originally belonged to the group. Two survive only as heads: one is the head displayed here on its bracket, the other is at the Villa Reale of Marlia, near Lucca. Whatever their precise identity, their association with the enthroned ruler presents imperial authority as something embodied in a surrounding human order rather than in the ruler alone.

The radical simplification of these bodies is one of the most discussed features of Tino’s Pisan style. Compared with the San Ranieri arca, made only a few years earlier, the forms are larger, more compact, and almost architectural. The change may owe something to the speed with which the commission was executed between 1313 and 1315. It may also reflect the intended viewing height, since figures seen from far below must communicate through their principal masses. To the modern eye the result has an extraordinary gravity, and these figures have long counted among the defining images of fourteenth-century Italian sculpture.

The smaller statues on the upper brackets belong to the same monument. The pieces associated with the tomb include two *Annunciation* statuettes, two acolytes, and two angels holding scrolls praising Henry, the latter recognized only recently while still serving as finials on the cathedral façade. The veiled woman at the far right, her hand pressed to her breast in a gesture of humble acceptance, is presumably the Virgin of the Annunciation, and the curly-haired figure at the far left her angel. The two taller statues in between would then be the acolytes. The column fragments at the ends, carved with spiralling bands of foliage inhabited by small figures, have a counterpart in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London.

The juxtaposition with the effigy in the cathedral is essential to the meaning of the ensemble. The effigy shows Henry as a mortal body lying in death. The statue shows him seated in the enduring form of imperial office, surrounded by the men through whom that office was exercised. In the original monument the two images coexisted, holding together two conditions that ordinary experience separates: the ruler has died, but his authority can still be represented as present. The Annunciation and the acolytes placed both images within a sacred frame, so that the tomb of the Ghibelline emperor was also a devotional monument within the cathedral.

The museum history of the figures is itself part of their meaning. After 1494 they were separated from the chest and effigy and moved between cathedral and Camposanto, where the councillors stood until the late twentieth century before entering the Museo dell’Opera. The present display makes the scale of Tino’s imperial ensemble visible, as the cathedral installation cannot. Read together with the chest, effigy, and epitaph, the pieces confirm that the tomb was a large, multi-level wall monument, perhaps rising above an altar of Saint Bartholomew. How exactly they fitted together remains one of the open problems of Tino’s work.

## Siena, 1315–1320: Capomaestro in His Father’s Cathedral

After leaving Pisa, Tino returned to Siena, where both documents and works attest his presence. The years between 1315 and 1320 are easily passed over as an interlude between Pisa and Florence. They were in fact the period in which he reached the highest office of his native city’s principal building site, and in which the funerary type he would export to Florence and Naples took its definitive form.

The documentary record places him firmly in the cathedral workshop. Several acts of 1318 attest the work of Tino and his father during the first six months of that year. In one, the Opera acknowledged a debt of 300 lire<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lira-senese" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> to Camaino, Tino, and twenty-three other masters for work done that year. In another, father and son were credited with 25 gold florins<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gold-florin" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> they had advanced to pay for red marble brought from Gerfalco. Payments in the early months of 1320 confirm that both were still active. In one of these, from January 1320, Tino is called *chapomastro*. The older literature held that Camaino occupied the same post for most of the period from 1310 until his death, leaving it to his son only in 1319 and 1320. Recent archival research has corrected this: Camaino heads the lists of masters and was consistently among the best paid, but no document calls him *capomaestro*. The title, as far as the sources show, was held in these years by Tino alone, and the Opera’s regard for the family was expressed by rank and pay in the father’s case and by office in the son’s.

What they actually carved or built in these years cannot be separated from the immense collective enterprise of the cathedral. This limitation should be stated plainly. The documents prove responsibility, payment, and presence; they do not identify the hand behind particular blocks. Attempts to assign surviving architectural sculpture at Siena to the young Tino remain unconfirmed.

The same documents show the social standing Tino had reached. In the tax declaration of 1318, Camaino and his son appear as owners of several plots of cultivated land and of a house in Vallepiatta, the quarter below the cathedral. The artisan family had become comfortably propertied. This matters for the interpretation of the later inscription in which Tino defers to his father. Its humility was offered by a man already recognized as a master, and it honoured a father who shared his property and his workshop and who stood first among the masters of the Opera.

Siena also served as a base for commissions in neighbouring towns. The remains of the *arca* of Saint Octavian in the diocesan museum of Volterra are generally attributed to him and dated to about 1319–1320; a *Virgin and Child* in the North Carolina Museum of Art at Raleigh has been connected with it. Reliefs with saints at San Gimignano may be the remains of a presumed *arca* of the Blessed Bartolo in the church of Sant’Agostino, although the reconstruction of that monument is hypothetical. A polychrome wooden *Virgin and Child* in the Badia of Anghiari is also usually assigned to this period. It is the only wooden statue whose attribution to Tino commands general agreement, and it shows that his idiom of compact volume was not confined to marble.

These works, modest beside the great tombs, show how a cathedral master’s reputation radiated into the surrounding territory. Smaller communities and religious houses could obtain the services of the leading workshop of the region. They also show the funerary *arca* becoming a familiar task: Volterra, San Gimignano, and the cardinal’s tomb in the cathedral itself belong to the same years.

### A Fragment from Volterra: The Virgin and Child from the Tomb of Saint Octavian

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-06.jpg" alt="A nearly square marble slab, worn and slightly chipped at the edges, carved with a deep circular recess. Its inner rim is lined with a ring of small square blocks. Within the circle, the Virgin appears at half length, crowned with an open crown of fleurons over a veil, her head tilted toward the Child. Her mantle falls in broad folds from her shoulders and is gathered across her lap. The Christ Child sits on her left arm, curly-haired and smiling. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds a scroll in his left. One of his feet emerges at the bottom of the circle.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, relief from the tomb (arca) of Saint Octavian in Volterra Cathedral, 1318–1320. Marble, 45.7 × 49.5 × 10.2 cm. Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art, Gift of the Samuel H. Kress Foundation, GL.60.17.4.</figcaption>
</figure>

This relief belonged to the tomb of Saint Octavian, the hermit venerated as one of the patrons of Volterra, in the city’s cathedral. The remains of the arca now in the diocesan museum of Volterra are generally attributed to Tino and dated to about 1319–1320, during the years he served the Opera del Duomo of Siena. The Raleigh relief was first connected with them in 1969. Gert Kreytenberg later studied the Volterra tombs as a group and assigned the Octavian arca to Tino. When and how the monument was dismantled is not documented, and the relief’s original position within it is unknown.

The composition is built on the contrast between a square block and a circular field. A deep tondo is cut into the slab, its rim lined with a ring of small square blocks that produce a crenellated border and a strong shadow around the figures. Within this frame the Virgin appears at half length, as if seen through a round window. The format suggests that the relief was one of a series of roundels on the front or ends of the arca. That remains a hypothesis: the measurements and carving of the Volterra fragments and the Raleigh slab have not been compared.

The Virgin wears an open crown of fleurons over a veil that falls to her shoulders. Her head inclines toward the Child, and her face, with its long nose, small mouth, and lowered eyes, has the full, softened forms of Tino’s Sienese years. The mantle descends from the shoulders in broad, simple folds and gathers across the lap in a heavy horizontal sweep. The figure is compact and calm, its expression carried by the angle of the head rather than by gesture.

The Child is livelier. He sits upright on his mother’s left arm, with a round head, tight curls, and a broad, smiling face. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds a scroll in his left, and one bare foot slips out below the drapery at the bottom of the tondo. The contrast between the Virgin’s grave inclination and the Child’s smile is characteristic of Tino’s Madonnas. Theology is expressed through an intimate, almost domestic relationship. Here the Child is awake, alert, and turned toward the viewer, in contrast to the sleeping Child of the Bargello.

The relief belongs to the same moment as the Petroni tomb in Siena and the della Torre tomb in Florence, all made within a few years of 1315–1320. It shows the qualities of that phase: broad masses, a smooth, slightly softened surface, and figures whose volume seems to press against the frame. The circular field concentrates these qualities. The bodies fill the recess almost to its edge, so that the relief reads from a distance as a single compact form and up close as a tender exchange between mother and child.

The later history of the slab is that of many dispersed fragments of Italian Gothic sculpture. It was in the Niccolini collection in Florence, passed through the Florentine dealer Alessandro Contini Bonacossi, and was bought by Samuel H. Kress in 1936. In 1939 it went to the National Gallery of Art in Washington. Deaccessioned in 1957, it was given to the North Carolina Museum of Art in 1961. Its journey from a saint’s tomb in a Tuscan cathedral to an American museum reflects the dispersal of Tino’s monuments described throughout this essay.

### The Lucca Madonna: A Standing Virgin and Child in the Museo Nazionale di Villa Guinigi

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-07.jpg" alt="A white marble statue of the standing Virgin holding the Christ Child, against a dark background. The Virgin wears a crown with a pierced band over a veil that falls to her shoulders, and she inclines her head toward the Child with a gentle expression. With her right hand she lifts the hem of her mantle, which falls in broad, deep folds edged with a pierced, lace-like border. The curly-haired Child sits on her left arm, nude to the waist, a cloth over his legs, both bare feet showing. He looks up at her and clasps something at his chest with both hands. A broken stump projects from his side where a part of the group has been lost.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, c. 1315–1320. Marble. Lucca, Museo Nazionale di Villa Guinigi.</figcaption>
</figure>

This statue is the most important medieval sculpture in the Museo Nazionale di Villa Guinigi in Lucca, where it is attributed to Tino di Camaino and dated by the museum to 1315–1320. It belongs to the same type as the signed *Virgin and Child* in Turin, from Tino’s Pisan years. The museum’s dating places it a little later, at the end of the Pisan period or the beginning of the Sienese one. Its earlier history and original location are not known.

The Virgin stands upright, her body turned slightly in a gentle sway, supporting the Child on her left arm. She wears a crown with a pierced band over a veil that frames her face and falls to her shoulders. Her head is inclined toward the Child, and her expression is gentle and inward. With her right hand she lifts the hem of her mantle, which falls across her body in broad, deep folds. The edges of the mantle are carved with a pierced, lace-like border, drilled through in many places, which catches the light and contrasts with the large smooth surfaces of the drapery.

The Child sits on his mother’s arm, nude to the waist, a cloth wrapped around his legs, both bare feet visible. He turns his head upward to look at her, his mouth slightly open, and clasps something at his chest with both hands. A broken stump projects from his side, a sign that part of the group has been lost; it may have been the Virgin’s hand, an attribute held by the Child, or a section of drapery. The upward glance of the Child and the inclined head of the Virgin create the intimate exchange that is the real subject of the image.

The statue is very close to the Turin Madonna. The pose, the lifted mantle, the lace-like border, and the exchange of glances between mother and son are common to both, as is their debt to the standing Madonnas of Giovanni Pisano. The Lucca statue, however, is broader and more monumental, its drapery heavier and less animated, its forms fuller. These qualities point toward the compact, massive style of the figures for Henry VII’s tomb and of the Petroni tomb. They support the museum’s dating to the years immediately after 1315.

The pierced border of the mantle is particularly characteristic. Tino used the drill to create lace-like edges that animate the drapery without breaking its large forms. The same technique appears on the Turin Madonna and on later works. Originally, these borders were probably gilded, and the mantle may have been painted, as traces on comparable works suggest. The statue would then have been a richly coloured devotional image, its whiteness today the result of loss.

### Tino in Wood: The Polychrome Virgin and Child of the Badia of Anghiari

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-08.jpg" alt="A painted wooden statue of the seated Virgin holding the Christ Child, placed in a niche beneath a carved shell. The Virgin wears a white veil and a dark blue mantle over a red gown gathered under the breast. Her hair is golden brown, and she looks out to her left with a calm, slightly serious expression. In her right hand she holds a small red book. The Child stands on her left knee, curly-haired, wearing a red tunic and a green mantle, and he raises his right hand in blessing while turning his face toward her. Her left hand supports him at his side. The blue mantle falls in broad folds over her knees to her feet; along its edges, paint losses reveal the pale wood beneath.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, c. 1315–1320. Polychrome wood. Anghiari, church of the Badia di San Bartolomeo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This statue is the only wooden sculpture whose attribution to Tino di Camaino commands general agreement. It is usually assigned to his Sienese years, about 1315–1320, when he worked with his father in the cathedral workshop and took commissions in the surrounding territory. The statue belongs to the Badia di San Bartolomeo at Anghiari, in the upper Tiber valley, where it is still venerated. Some general lists place it, wrongly, in the Badia Fiorentina in Florence. It shows that Tino’s idiom of compact volume was not confined to marble, and it gives a rare idea of the colour that his marble works have largely lost.

The Virgin sits frontally, her body built as a single broad block in which the knees, the lap, and the fall of the mantle form a stable base. She wears a white veil and a dark blue mantle over a red gown gathered under the breast. The mantle falls from her shoulders and spreads over her knees in large, simple folds that descend to her feet. In her right hand she holds a small red book, an attribute of the Virgin as Seat of Wisdom. Her face, framed by golden-brown hair, is broad and calm; she looks out to her left, beyond the viewer, with a grave expression.

The Child stands on his mother’s left knee, supported by her hand at his side. He wears a red tunic and a green mantle draped across his body and arm. He raises his right hand in blessing and turns his curly head toward his mother. The relation between the two figures is quieter than in Tino’s marble Madonnas. The Virgin does not look at the Child, and the Child blesses rather than plays. The image presents the Virgin as the throne of the incarnate Wisdom, offering the blessing Christ to the faithful.

The polychromy is an essential part of the work. The red, blue, and green, the gilded hair, and the flesh tones of the faces and hands give the statue an immediate, lifelike presence quite different from the white surfaces of Tino’s surviving marbles. Not all of the present colour is necessarily original. Paint losses along the edges of the mantle reveal the pale wood beneath, and the deep blue may include later layers. Even so, the statue shows how the sculptor’s volumes were meant to be completed by colour. It invites the viewer to imagine the marble tombs and statues, once painted and gilded, in a similar way.

The wooden figure confirms the qualities of Tino’s style in a different material. The broad, closed volume, the heavy folds over the knees, the calm faces, and the restrained gestures are the same as in the Petroni tomb and the Bargello Madonna of the same years. Wood allowed a lighter, more hollowed structure and a softer finish than marble, yet Tino kept the gravity and stability of his marble figures. The statue is thus both an exception within his corpus and a confirmation of its coherence.

## The Petroni Tomb: Resurrection and the Meaning of Witness

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-09.jpg" alt="A tall marble wall tomb against the black-and-white striped wall of Siena Cathedral. Four leafy corbels at the bottom carry a low chest decorated with red marble panels and coats of arms. On it stand four draped figures who support a second chest, carved with three relief scenes: on the left a woman kneeling before Christ in a garden of trees, in the centre Christ rising from the tomb with a banner above sleeping soldiers, and on the right Christ showing his wound to a disciple. Standing statuettes separate the scenes. Above, two angels at the corners draw back a heavy curtain like a tent, revealing the cardinal lying on a bier. At the top, a Gothic tabernacle with three pointed gables holds three statues, the Virgin and Child at the centre between two saints. It is crowned by a small pinnacled gable.">
  <figcaption>Tomb of Cardinal Riccardo Petroni, c. 1315–1316. Marble with red marble inlays. Siena, Cathedral, chapel of Sant’Ansano, in the 1951 recomposition.</figcaption>
</figure>

The tomb of Cardinal Riccardo Petroni in Siena Cathedral is the principal monument of the Sienese years and the only Tuscan work by Tino that survives almost complete. Petroni, a Sienese canon lawyer who had served Boniface VIII<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-pope-boniface-viii" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a>, died at Genoa in 1314. The tomb used to be dated to 1317, and some guides still give 1318; recent work favours an earlier date of about 1315–1316, immediately after Tino’s return from Pisa. The monument originally rose above the altar of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, and a large Gothic canopy standing on the floor covered both tomb and altar. In 1484 the wall that held it was demolished to build the chapel of Saint John the Baptist. The tomb was moved to the adjacent wall and the canopy discarded, and the altar followed in 1591. After further moves the monument was placed in the chapel of Sant’Ansano, in the left transept, in 1951. It is now seen in that twentieth-century recomposition, which must be kept in mind when reading its arrangement.

The structure is the clearest statement of the funerary type Tino developed, and the photograph shows it zone by zone. At the bottom, four leafy corbels carry a first chest decorated with panels of red marble alternating with coats of arms. This chest serves as a base for four caryatids, standing draped figures who support the burial chest proper. That chest is carved with three scenes of Christ’s Resurrection and appearances: the Noli me tangere on the left, the Resurrection in the centre, and the *Incredulity of Thomas* on the right. They are separated by standing statuettes, probably the Evangelists. Above lies the funerary chamber, where two angels at the corners draw back a heavy curtain, gathered like the cloth of a tent, to reveal the recumbent cardinal; acolytes stand behind the bier. A tabernacle in the form of a Gothic triptych, with three pointed gables, crowns the whole and holds statues of the Virgin and Child between two saints. Only the small topmost gable is of doubtful pertinence, though it already appears in a drawing of 1664.

The scheme recombines elements from several sources into an original design. Among them is the type of wall tomb created by [Arnolfo di Cambio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Arnolfo-di-Cambio.html) in the late thirteenth century, with angels drawing back curtains around the effigy. It became the reference for Tino’s subsequent tombs, from the della Torre monument in Florence to the royal tombs of Naples, and the reliefs of its chest represent perhaps the highest point of his spatial and volumetric research. The red marble panels in the chests, and the red accents still visible in the gables, recall that the whole monument was once coloured.

The recumbent cardinal provides the monument’s human centre. Around him, the tomb constructs a wider account of what death means. The deceased’s body is placed above images in which Christ’s death has been overcome and his continuing presence is disclosed to others. The juxtaposition encourages the viewer to understand Petroni’s condition through a promise that exceeds the immediate evidence of the corpse.

<a href="/scholars.html#rafanelli-lisa-m">Lisa M. Rafanelli</a>’s study draws attention to the particular importance of the *Noli me tangere* and the *Incredulity of Thomas* within this cycle. Her interpretation connects the reliefs with testimony, bodily resurrection, and the relationship between devotional life and personal commemoration. The value of this approach lies in treating the narrative scenes as an intellectually coordinated part of the tomb rather than as interchangeable religious decoration.

The two encounters invite reflection on different forms of recognition. Mary Magdalene meets the risen Christ in a scene organized around recognition and the regulation of bodily contact. Thomas confronts the problem of belief through Christ’s wounded body. Read together, these episodes make the body a site at which knowledge is both offered and tested, which gives them a profound relevance to a funerary monument. The tomb contains a body whose future cannot be verified by ordinary sight, and the narratives place that uncertainty within the history of Christian revelation.

Sculpture intensifies the relationship because it gives visible bodies material extension. A carved hand directed toward another figure occupies a spatial relation the viewer can follow. The marble makes contact, separation, and approach intelligible through physical form. Here the medium participates in the subject: bodily presence becomes the means through which a claim about bodily resurrection is considered.

The monument’s vertical organization supports the same argument. The cardinal’s effigy belongs to the sphere of death and commemoration, while the Virgin and saints above establish a heavenly horizon. The narrative reliefs mediate between these conditions by presenting the events through which resurrection becomes imaginable. A funerary monument participates in hope, prayer, and commemoration; it does not certify that the deceased has already attained salvation. Tino makes that framework persuasive without dissolving the reality of the dead body at its centre.

## Antonio d’Orso: The Seated Dead and the Vulnerability of Authority

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-10.jpg" alt="A marble wall tomb set against the bare stone of a cathedral wall. At the bottom, a broad console rests on two round arches. Between them a frontal figure with outstretched arms stands on a dragon-like creature, and groups of small figures crowd the spandrels at either side. Above the console, two lion heads and a central human mask support a sarcophagus flanked by two painted coats of arms, one with wavy bands and one with stripes. The front of the sarcophagus has two relief panels crowded with standing figures, with traces of gilding in the background. On top sits a statue of a bishop in mitre and vestments, his hands folded in his lap and his head bowed.">
  <figcaption>Tomb of Bishop Antonio d’Orso, completed before 18 July 1321 (the cathedral dates it c. 1322). Marble, partly gilded, with traces of polychromy. Florence, Cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore, inner façade, in a modern reassembly of the surviving parts.</figcaption>
</figure>

The monument to Bishop Antonio d’Orso in Florence Cathedral contains one of Tino’s most arresting inventions: a deceased prelate represented seated rather than stretched out upon a bier. The tomb stood on the inner façade, between the central and north doors. It was removed as early as the fourteenth century, moved repeatedly, and partly returned to its original site in the early twentieth century. The cathedral dates it to about 1322 and describes its present form as a hypothetical assembly of surviving parts. The chronology is not settled: the cathedral gives 18 July 1321 as the date of the bishop’s death, while the most recent biographical account reads the same date as that of a solemn translation of his body, a year after death, by which time the tomb would already have been ready. On that reading, which the Dizionario Biografico adopts, the monument was completed before 18 July 1321.

The original site is secured by Tino’s signature, incised directly into the wall:

> +OPERU(M) DE SENIS NATUS EX MAG(IST)RO CAMAINO IN HOC SITU FLORENTINO TINUS SCULPSIT O(MN)E LAT(US) / HU(N)C P(RO) PATRE GENETIVO DECET INCLINARI UT MAGISTER ILLO VIVO NOLIT APPELLARI

“Tinus, born of Siena from master Camaino, carved every side of these works in this Florentine place; it befits him to bow before the father who begot him, so that while his father lives he does not wish to be called master.” The inscription is a rare personal statement by a medieval sculptor. It claims the whole work, names a birthplace other than the city where the work stands, and turns a signature into an act of filial deference.

The photograph shows the monument in its present state, read from the bottom up. A broad console rests on two round arches. In the space between them, the *Allegory of Death* appears as a frontal figure with outstretched arms standing on a dragon-like creature, and groups of small figures crowd the spandrels on either side. Above the console, two lion heads at the corners and a human mask at the centre support the sarcophagus. Painted coats of arms flank it at both ends, the heraldry of the deceased that framed the monument as a family as well as an episcopal memorial. The front of the sarcophagus carries two relief panels crowded with standing figures, their backgrounds still showing traces of gilding. On top sits the statue of the bishop.

These are the parts that certainly belong to the monument and have been reassembled *in situ*: the console with the allegory, the sarcophagus resting on it, and the statue of the bishop. The allegory was apparently inspired by Francesco da Barberino, the poet and notary who acted as executor of the bishop’s will. Two curtain-bearing angels, fragments of a scene of the soul’s elevation, once in the Torrigiani collection, have recently entered the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. The cathedral’s catalogue explains the sarcophagus relief through the *commendatio animae*, the commendation of the soul, with the Virgin acting as intercessor.

The seated posture makes death more unsettling because it preserves an arrangement normally associated with alertness and authority. A bishop seated in his chair might ordinarily be expected to teach, judge, bless, or preside. Tino retains that expectation while making its fulfilment impossible. Mitred and vested, the bishop folds his hands in his lap, closes his eyes, and lets his head sink forward, withdrawn from active participation in the world. The viewer confronts the distance between the continued visibility of an office and the absence of the person who exercised it.

The head is crucial to this effect. Its inclination interrupts the upright structure that the chair and vestments establish. A small deviation becomes a decisive sign of lost vitality, without any exaggerated bodily collapse. Death appears as a withdrawal from the very posture through which authority had been expressed.

The relief imagery broadens this meditation. The deceased is represented in more than one condition: as an identifiable body, and as a soul commended to judgment. Earthly dignity remains visible in clothing and seat, but the commendation places the bishop’s life within an order that office cannot control. The *Allegory of Death* below gives the same idea a universal form, setting the bishop’s individual death within the death that strikes all people. The monument preserves distinction without suggesting that distinction abolishes accountability.

The seated effigy also changes the viewer’s encounter with the deceased. A recumbent body establishes a clear separation between the vertical living spectator and the horizontal dead. Here that distinction is less secure: the bishop remains upright enough to occupy the space of social encounter. Claims that the face reproduces a death mask, or that the pose records a specific circumstance of the bishop’s death, require more caution than the visual effect itself. Tino’s invention can be understood through the form that survives: a body held within the architecture of dignity, with the smallest signs revealing that its sustaining life has departed.

## Florence: Monumental Sculpture in an Urban Religious Landscape

Tino’s Florentine activity was considerable, but it survives almost entirely in fragments. He may have moved to the city as early as 1321. The first secure document dates from 29 November 1322, when the officials of the Arte di Calimala<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-calimala" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a>, the guild responsible for the baptistery, asked that *maestro Tino Camaini da Siena* be engaged to work for the Opera di San Giovanni on the building’s exterior. The same document mentions the intention to make the baptistery doors in metal, a project later realized by Andrea Pisano. Florence’s religious buildings organized movement, ritual, memory, and public visibility, and sculpture at their entrances addressed a passing urban audience as well as the devout.

The baptistery commission is the clearest case. Large marble groups stood above the building’s doors until the early sixteenth century, when they were replaced by new ensembles. Six fragments, almost unanimously attributed to Tino, survive in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. Two female busts and a female head belong to the *Theological Virtues*: the busts of *Faith* and *Charity*, the latter identified by her flaming vessel and horn of plenty, and the head of *Hope*. A bust of the *Blessing Christ* and a head of the Baptist are thought to come from a *Baptism of Christ*; a second, less securely attributed head of the Baptist may be all that remains of a group of the Baptist preaching. The museum’s own records disagree about which door some of these groups surmounted, so reconstructions should remain provisional. What is not in doubt is the setting. Baptism joined an individual to the community, and a sculptural group above a doorway was encountered at the point where bodily movement into the building intersected with the meaning of spiritual entry.

The figure of *Charity* in the Museo Stefano Bardini has also been connected with the baptistery groups. Since the *Theological Virtues* of that ensemble already include a *Charity*, the Bardini statue must have belonged to another group or monument; one view places it in the Pisan years. Its subject is expressed through a woman nursing children, translating an abstract virtue into an act of bodily sustenance. The choice matters because it makes virtue intelligible through dependence. The children require nourishment; the adult figure provides it. Ethical action appears as a concrete exchange, and the substantial forms reinforce the impression that giving involves material support. The same logic governs Tino’s supporting figures beneath tombs, where an abstract relationship becomes visible through gravity, contact, and dependence.

The funerary monuments of the Florentine years have all been dismembered. The earliest is the tomb of Gastone della Torre, patriarch of Aquileia, who died in 1318; it is usually dated to 1318–1320 (the Fondazione Zeri catalogue gives about 1318–1320; the Dizionario Biografico, 1318–1319), which suggests that Tino received the Florentine commission while still based in Siena. It stood in the right aisle of Santa Croce. Its central part survives in the Museo dell’Opera di Santa Croce: sarcophagus, effigy, and two curtain-bearing angels. The structure was a reduced version of the Petroni tomb, whose chest reliefs it repeats. Several dispersed pieces have been associated with it, including an *Annunciate Virgin* in the same museum, the *Virgin and Child* in the Bargello, an adoring angel in Palazzo Vecchio, caryatids in the Bargello and the Liebieghaus in Frankfurt, and a *commendatio animae* group also in Frankfurt. Each association requires separate evaluation.

A third Florentine tomb is known only from an effigy in Santa Maria Maggiore, identified in 1972 as that of Bruno Beccuti, prior of the church from 1317 to 1323. Nothing else of the monument is known. Further fragments, among them a caryatid and an adoring angel in the museum of Santo Spirito and two curtain-bearing angels in the Victoria and Albert Museum, do not fit easily into either the della Torre or the d’Orso monuments. They may belong to the Beccuti tomb or to yet another lost commission.

Taken together, these works reveal an artist operating across several levels of religious experience in a few intense years. His sculpture commemorated a patriarch, a bishop, and a prior, articulated ethical concepts, and contributed to the public face of the city’s most venerable sacred building. Florence provided a setting in which those functions overlapped, and Tino gave them forms clear enough for public visibility and concentrated enough for prolonged attention.

### The Baptism of Christ from the Florence Baptistery: The Blessing Christ and the Head of the Baptist

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-11.jpg" alt="Two marble fragments side by side on wooden stands. On the left, the upper half of a nude male figure, cut off at the waist: Christ, with long hair falling to the shoulders, a forked beard and moustache, and a long, calm face with heavy-lidded eyes. His right hand is raised before his chest in blessing, two fingers extended; his left arm hangs at his side, the hand resting on the stand. His chest and ribs are carved in a series of shallow parallel ridges. On the right, a separate head of an older man turned in three-quarter view to the left, with deep-set eyes and a furrowed brow. A long, flowing beard and thick waves of hair are carved in deep, curling strands, drilled through in many places so that dark holes show between the locks.">
  <figcaption>Blessing Christ and Head of Saint John the Baptist, fragments of the group of the Baptism of Christ from above a door of the Florence Baptistery (the museum’s records name both the east and the south door), c. 1320–1324. Marble. Florence, Museo dell’Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

These two fragments come from the large marble group of the *Baptism of Christ* that Tino carved for one of the doors of the Florence Baptistery. Above each of the three doors stood a sculptural group, and in the early sixteenth century these were removed and replaced with new ensembles by Renaissance sculptors. Six fragments almost unanimously attributed to Tino survive in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. Two female busts and a female head belong to the *Theological Virtues*: *Faith*, *Charity*, and the head of *Hope*. The bust of the *Blessing Christ* and a head of the Baptist are thought to come from the *Baptism*. A second head of the Baptist, less securely attributed, may be all that remains of a group of the Baptist preaching.

The commission belongs to Tino’s Florentine years. On 29 November 1322 the officials of the Arte di Calimala, the guild responsible for the Baptistery, asked that *maestro Tino Camaini da Siena* be engaged to work for the Opera di San Giovanni on the building’s exterior. The museum dates the *Blessing Christ* to 1320–1324. The groups were thus designed in the same years as the d’Orso tomb in the cathedral opposite. The door they originally crowned is uncertain, and the museum’s own records name both the east and the south door.

The Christ is the surviving upper half of a standing figure, cut off at the waist. In the original group he would have stood in the waters of the Jordan, perhaps rendered as waves rising around his body. He is nude, his right hand raised before his chest in blessing, his left arm hanging at his side. The figure is long and slender, the shoulders sloping, the chest and abdomen modelled in a series of shallow, parallel ridges that mark ribs and muscles without breaking the smooth continuity of the surface. The head, framed by long hair falling to the shoulders, is calm and frontal, with heavy-lidded eyes and a forked beard. Some fingers of the blessing hand have been broken and repaired.

The figure is unusual in Tino’s work. His statues are usually clothed, and their expressive force lies in the mass and fall of the drapery. Here he had to model a nude body at large scale, and he did so with restraint, reducing the anatomy to a few clear rhythms that read from a distance. The serene face and gentle gesture give the Christ a gravity that is devotional rather than narrative. He is less the protagonist of a story than an image of the divine Son revealed at his baptism, blessing the water and the faithful who passed beneath him into the building.

The head of the Baptist is the most virtuoso piece of carving in the group. Turned in three-quarter view, it would originally have been directed toward Christ, as the Baptist leaned forward to pour water over his head. The face is lean and intense, with deep-set eyes, a furrowed brow, and a slightly open mouth. The hair and beard are carved in long, flowing locks that curl at their ends. Tino drilled deeply between the strands, so that the dark holes create a contrast of light and shadow visible from far below. The technique gives the ascetic of the desert a wild, almost windswept appearance, in sharp contrast with the smooth, contained surface of the Christ.

The contrast between the two figures is part of the meaning of the group. Christ is still, smooth, and serene; John is animated, hirsute, and intense. The juxtaposition sets divine composure beside the energy of the prophet who recognized and announced him. It also shows Tino adjusting his technique to the character of each figure: broad, polished planes for the incarnate God, deep drilling and restless texture for the voice crying in the wilderness.

The position of the groups determined much of their style. Placed high above the doors, they addressed a passing urban audience and had to be legible from the piazza. The large simplified planes of the Christ and the strong chiaroscuro of the Baptist’s hair both served that purpose. The setting also gave the subject its meaning. Baptism joined an individual to the Christian community, and the group above the door was encountered at the very point where bodily entry into the building coincided with spiritual entry into the Church. Detached from that threshold and seen at eye level in a museum, the fragments keep their sculptural force but lose much of the relation that once explained them.

### Faith from the Florence Baptistery: A Theological Virtue by Tino di Camaino

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-12.jpg" alt="A white marble bust of a young woman, cut off below the chest and set on a wooden stand. She wears a crown of carved leaves over a veil that frames her face, falls to her shoulders, and wraps around her neck like a scarf. Her face is oval and calm, with smooth almond-shaped eyes without pupils, a small mouth, and a broken nose. Her hair is parted in the centre and waves down beside her cheeks. Her close-fitting gown is fastened down the front by two rows of round buttons. The arms are lost, and the edge of one shoulder is chipped.">
  <figcaption>Faith (traditional title), fragment of a group of the Theological Virtues from above a door of the Florence Baptistery, c. 1320–1324. Marble. Florence, Museo dell’Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This bust belongs to the group of *Theological Virtues* that Tino carved for the Florence Baptistery in the early 1320s. Large marble groups stood above the building’s doors until the early sixteenth century, when they were removed and replaced by new ensembles. Of Tino’s groups, six fragments survive in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. Two female busts and a female head belong to the *Theological Virtues*, originally above one of the doors: this bust of *Faith*, a bust of *Charity*, and the head of *Hope*. The commission belongs to the years documented by the request of 29 November 1322, in which the Arte di Calimala asked for Tino to work on the Baptistery’s exterior.

Only the upper part of the figure survives. The bust is cut off below the chest, and the arms, which would have held the virtue’s attribute, are lost. Without them, the identification as *Faith* rests on tradition and on the figure’s place in the group rather than on an attribute visible today. Faith was usually shown holding a cross, a chalice, or a candle. Any of these would have resolved the question. The title is therefore traditional.

The figure is crowned, as befits a theological virtue, the highest order of the virtues. The crown is a band of carved leaves set over a veil that frames the face, falls to the shoulders, and wraps around the neck like a scarf. The face is oval, calm, and youthful, with smooth almond-shaped eyes without carved pupils, a small closed mouth, and a broad, rounded chin. The nose is broken. The hair, parted in the centre, falls in soft waves beside the cheeks. The expression is grave and withdrawn, without any individual emotion: the face of an idea rather than a person.

The dress is close-fitting and fastened down the front by two rows of round buttons. This is a precise detail of contemporary women’s clothing, and it recalls the buttoned bodice of the *Charity* in the Museo Bardini. The shared detail places the two works in the same years and the same workshop culture, although the Bardini statue cannot belong to this group, whose own *Charity* survives in the Museo dell’Opera. In both figures Tino gives an abstract virtue the clothing of a real woman of his time, a choice that makes the allegory immediately legible and familiar.

The forms are broad and simplified, the surfaces smooth and almost polished, and the features reduced to a few clear planes. This simplification was calculated for the bust’s original position high above a door, where it had to be read from the piazza below. Close up, in a museum, the face can seem schematic; seen from a distance, its clear outline and strong shadows would have given it a powerful presence. As with the other Baptistery fragments, the change of viewpoint alters our judgment of the style.

The virtues completed the meaning of the Baptistery groups. Baptism was the sacrament that infused the theological virtues into the soul of the baptized. Faith, Hope, and Charity above the door therefore stated in visible form what the rite performed inside: the believer entered the building, and the Church, under the sign of the virtues that baptism conferred. The bust, now separated from that threshold, preserves a fragment of a programme in which sculpture, architecture, and sacrament were closely joined.

### Hope from the Florence Baptistery: The Head of a Theological Virtue

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-13.jpg" alt="A white marble head with its neck, broken off at the base and set on a wooden stand. The face is full and youthful, with heavy-lidded almond eyes, a straight nose worn at the tip, and a small, full-lipped mouth. Thick, wavy hair is swept back from the forehead and gathered in heavy curls around the head and over the ears. The surface is weathered, with small pits on the forehead and cheek and damage around the chin.">
  <figcaption>Hope (the museum’s identification), head from a group of the Theological Virtues above a door of the Florence Baptistery, c. 1320–1324. Marble. Florence, Museo dell’Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This head of *Hope* belongs to the group of *Theological Virtues* that Tino carved for the Florence Baptistery in the early 1320s. It survives in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo together with the busts of *Faith* and *Charity*. The groups stood above the Baptistery doors until the early sixteenth century, when they were replaced by new ensembles. Of this figure only the head and neck remain, broken off at the base.

With no body, no attribute, and no inscription, the identification as *Hope* cannot be confirmed from the fragment itself. Hope was usually shown with her hands raised in prayer, her gaze lifted toward a crown held out to her from above, or holding an anchor. Whatever attribute or gesture once identified this figure has been lost with the body. The name therefore rests on the museum’s identification and on the head’s place within the group, and the title is cited as the museum’s.

The face differs noticeably from that of the *Faith*. It is fuller and more rounded, with heavier eyelids, a broader nose, and a fleshy, slightly parted mouth. The hair is not covered by a veil but swept back from the forehead in thick waves and gathered in heavy curls around the head and over the ears. Whether a crown was once set on the hair, as on the *Faith*, can no longer be seen, because the top of the head is worn.

The surface is weathered, with small pits on the forehead and cheeks and losses around the chin and nose. This damage is consistent with long exposure outdoors, above a door, for nearly two centuries. It reminds us that the Baptistery groups were architectural sculpture, made to withstand the elements and to be seen from far below. Their large, simplified forms and strong shadows served that purpose. Close up, in a museum, the head reads as a fragment; from the piazza, it would have formed part of a legible and imposing figure.

Even in its damaged state, the head shows the qualities of Tino’s Florentine work. The volume is compact and closed, the features are organized into a few clear planes, and the expression is calm and impersonal, the face of an idea rather than a person. Set beside the *Faith*, the head shows how Tino could vary a single type, giving each virtue a distinct physiognomy within a common idiom of grave, monumental serenity.

### Charity from the Florence Baptistery: Burning Love and Abundance

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-14.jpg" alt="A white marble bust of a young woman, cut off at the waist, against a black background. She wears a narrow diadem over thick, wavy hair that falls to her shoulders, and she turns her face upward to one side, her lips slightly parted; the nose is broken. In her right hand, raised to her shoulder, she holds a vessel with a pierced rim from which rises a mass of flames. With both arms she holds a long curved horn of plenty across her body, its surface carved with overlapping leaves. Her gown has a trefoil-shaped neckline and gathered folds below the breast. The surface is worn, with losses on the hands, the flames, and the horn.">
  <figcaption>Charity, fragment of a group of the Theological Virtues from above a door of the Florence Baptistery, 1320–1324. Marble. Florence, Museo dell’Opera del Duomo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This bust is the third surviving virtue from the group of *Theological Virtues* that Tino carved for the Florence Baptistery in the early 1320s. Together with the *Faith* and the head of *Hope*, it is preserved in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo. The groups stood above the Baptistery doors until the early sixteenth century, when they were removed and replaced with new ensembles. Of this figure the upper half survives, cut off at the waist.

Unlike the *Faith* and the *Hope*, this figure has kept her attributes, and they identify her beyond doubt. In her right hand, raised to the shoulder, she holds a vessel from which rises a mass of flames, the burning love that defines charity. With both arms she holds a long horn of plenty across her body, its surface carved with overlapping leaves, the image of the abundance that charity gives. The pairing of fire and abundance expresses the two sides of the virtue: love of God, burning upward, and love of neighbour, poured out in gifts. Giotto had painted a comparable Charity, offering a heart and holding a bowl of fruit, in the Scrovegni Chapel<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-arena-chapel" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a> a few years earlier.

Her pose distinguishes her from the other two virtues. While the *Faith* looks straight ahead, calm and frontal, the *Charity* turns her head upward to one side, her lips slightly parted, as if her gaze followed the flames toward God. The movement gives the bust an animation the other fragments lack, and it suits the virtue that the theological tradition placed highest among the three. The hair, thick and wavy, falls freely to the shoulders under a narrow diadem, and the gown, with its trefoil-shaped neckline, is simpler than the buttoned bodice of the *Faith*.

The surface is weathered and damaged, with losses on the nose, the hands, the flames, and the horn. This damage is consistent with long exposure outdoors above a door. As with the other Baptistery fragments, the forms are broad and simplified for viewing from far below: the flames and the horn are large and clearly outlined, and the upward turn of the head would have been legible from the piazza.

With the attributes preserved, this fragment completes the identification of the group. Faith, Hope, and Charity stood together above a door of the Baptistery, the three virtues that, according to medieval doctrine, the sacrament of baptism infused into the soul. The *Charity* is the best preserved and most expressive of the three, and through her the original power of Tino’s Baptistery sculpture can still be felt.

### Charity as a Nursing Mother: Tino di Camaino’s Carità in the Museo Bardini

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-15.jpg" alt="A white marble statue of a standing young woman, wearing a low crown over hair braided and coiled around her head. Her head is turned to one side and bowed toward one of the two naked infants she carries against her body. Her long gown is fastened at the neck with buttons, and two pointed openings are outlined over her breasts. On the left, one child, seen from behind, climbs up her body and reaches toward her neckline, supported by her hand beneath him. On the right, the other child nurses at her breast, curled against her and held in her other hand. Below, the gown falls in long, heavy folds to the base. The surface is worn, and the end of one sleeve is broken.">
  <figcaption>Charity, c. 1320–1322. Marble. Florence, Museo Stefano Bardini.</figcaption>
</figure>

This statue of *Charity* is one of the best-known works of Tino’s Florentine years and an invention among Italian allegorical sculptures of the early fourteenth century. It is usually dated to about 1320–1322. Its original destination is debated. It has sometimes been connected with the large marble groups that Tino carved for the doors of the Florence Baptistery. The Theological Virtues of that ensemble, however, already include a Charity, now in the Museo dell’Opera del Duomo, so the Bardini statue must have belonged to another group or another monument. Another view places it earlier, in the Pisan years. It should therefore not be assigned to a particular architectural ensemble without qualification.

The virtue is shown not through an abstract attribute but through an act of bodily care. A young woman stands frontally, crowned, her head turned and bowed toward one of the two naked infants she holds against her body. On the left, a child seen from behind climbs up her front, one hand reaching for her neckline, while her right hand supports him from below. On the right, a second child nurses at her breast, curled against her and cradled in her left hand. The crown marks her as the queen of the virtues; the children make her charity a concrete exchange of nourishment and dependence.

The dress is carved with striking precision. The gown is fastened at the neck by small buttons, and two pointed openings are outlined over the breasts. These are nursing slits, a practical feature of women’s clothing of the period. Tino did not invent a timeless allegorical costume but borrowed a detail from daily life, giving the abstract figure the plausibility of a real mother. The hair is equally carefully observed, braided and wound in a coil around the head beneath the low crown.

The composition holds three bodies in a single compact form. The children are pressed against the mother’s body in shallow relief, their limbs following the curves of her figure. Her arms close around them in a single protective gesture, and her bowed head completes the circle. Below, the long gown falls straight to the base in heavy vertical folds, giving the group a firm architectural foundation. The figure was meant to be seen from the front, and the back is treated far more summarily, consistent with a position in a niche or against a wall.

The type has a significant precedent in Tuscan sculpture. On Giovanni Pisano’s pulpit in Pisa Cathedral, a female figure nursing two infants stands among the supports, though it is identified variously as Charity or as Ecclesia. Tino would certainly have known it. His treatment, however, is gentler and less dramatic. Giovanni’s figures tend toward twisting movement and intense expression; Tino’s Charity is calm and inward, her expressive force concentrated in the inclination of the head and the enclosing arms.

The statue makes visible a principle that runs through Tino’s art. Meaning arises from the relationship between bodies: the support of one body by another, the dependence of the weak on the strong, the physical weight of care. The same logic governs the caryatids beneath his tombs and the mothers and children of his Neapolitan reliefs. In the *Charity*, an ethical idea is translated into a concrete bodily exchange that anyone can recognize, and the virtue becomes intelligible through gravity, contact, and dependence.

### A Guelf Prelate in Santa Croce: The Tomb of Gastone della Torre

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-16.jpg" alt="The central part of a marble wall tomb, lit from the side against a pale wall. At the top, two standing angels at the ends of a shallow chamber draw back carved curtains, revealing the effigy of a prelate lying on a bier in vestments. Below a cornice with a band of leaf ornament, the front of the sarcophagus is carved with three relief scenes separated by standing figures, two of them holding open books with inscriptions. On the left, Christ raises his arm as a disciple touches the wound in his side. In the centre, Christ sits on the edge of the tomb holding a staff, above sleeping soldiers. On the right, a woman kneels before Christ among two trees. Four carved corbels support the chest from below.">
  <figcaption>Tomb of Gastone della Torre, Patriarch of Aquileia, c. 1318–1320. Marble. Florence, Museo dell’Opera di Santa Croce; originally in the right aisle of Santa Croce.</figcaption>
</figure>

Gastone della Torre belonged to the Milanese family of the Della Torre, the great rivals of the Visconti. He had been archbishop of Milan before becoming patriarch of Aquileia. Driven from Milan by the Visconti, he lived in exile in Guelf circles and died in Florence in 1318, after a fall from his horse. His tomb was set up in the right aisle of the Franciscan church of Santa Croce. Its central part, seen here, survives in the Museo dell’Opera di Santa Croce. The monument is usually dated to 1318–1320, which suggests that Tino received the Florentine commission while still based in Siena.

The choice of church and sculptor was not neutral. A recent study has read the tomb as a statement of Florentine Guelf solidarity, honouring a prelate who could be seen as a martyr of the Guelf cause in the city that most fully embodied it. In Santa Croce the monument soon found itself among the great painted chapels of Giotto and his followers. Tino, who had fought with the Guelf army at Montecatini three years before, was an apt choice to commemorate a Guelf exile.

The structure is a reduced version of the Petroni tomb in Siena, whose design it follows closely. At the top, a shallow chamber holds the effigy of the patriarch in pontifical vestments, lying on a bier. Two angels at the ends of the chamber draw back heavy curtains, bending their heads toward the dead. The angel on the left gathers the cloth against his body; the one on the right lifts it high with an outstretched arm. Their gestures make the display of the corpse a ceremonial act, revealing the dead man to the viewer while acknowledging the boundary between the living and the dead.

The chest repeats the narrative programme of the Petroni tomb with significant variations. In the centre, the *Resurrection* shows Christ seated on the edge of the open tomb, holding a staff, above the soldiers asleep at its base. On the left, the *Incredulity of Thomas* shows Christ raising his right arm to expose his side as Thomas reaches toward the wound. On the right, the *Noli me tangere* places the kneeling Magdalene before Christ in a garden marked by two trees. The order of the side scenes is reversed compared with Siena, where the *Noli me tangere* comes first. Four standing figures separate and frame the scenes; they are the Evangelists, two of whom hold open books with inscriptions.

The programme turns the tomb into a meditation on the resurrection of the body. The two side scenes are encounters in which the risen Christ is recognized through his body: Thomas by touching the wound, the Magdalene by being forbidden to touch. Placed beneath the effigy of a dead prelate, they set his corpse within the promise of a bodily resurrection that ordinary sight cannot verify. The Evangelists, with their open books, are the witnesses who transmitted that promise in writing.

The carving shows Tino’s narrative art at its most concentrated. The figures are few and large, the settings reduced to a few signs: a doorway behind Thomas, two trees in the garden, the tomb chest in the Resurrection. Each scene is built on a single clear gesture: the raised arm, the reaching hand, the kneeling body. The drapery falls in broad, heavy folds, and the ornament of the cornice above, a dense band of leaves, gives the chest a firm horizontal frame.

What survives is only the central part of a larger monument. In its original form the tomb, like the Petroni monument, probably included caryatids beneath the chest and a tabernacle with the Virgin and Child above. Several dispersed works have been associated with it, among them the *Virgin and Child* in the Bargello, an *Annunciate Virgin* in the same museum of Santa Croce, an adoring angel in Palazzo Vecchio, and caryatids in the Bargello and the Liebieghaus in Frankfurt. Each association requires separate evaluation. The monument as it now stands should be read as a fragment: its core is secure, but its full design can only be reconstructed by hypothesis.

### The Seat of Wisdom: Tino di Camaino’s Virgin and Child in the Bargello

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-17.jpg" alt="A white marble statue of the Virgin seated on a plain block throne. She wears a crown of fleurons over a veil that falls to her shoulders, and a gown with an embroidered band at the neckline and a belt at the waist. Her face, slightly damaged, looks out calmly. In her right hand she holds a closed book with clasps, resting it upright on the throne. On her left knee sits the curly-haired Christ Child, his head resting against her shoulder and his eyes closed as if asleep. One small hand lies on his chest under her spread fingers; the other grasps the edge of her mantle. The mantle, edged with a decorated border, falls over her knees in deep folds to her feet. The base is inscribed SEDES SAPIENTIAE in Roman capitals.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child (known as the Sedes Sapientiae), c. 1318–1320, probably from the tomb of Gastone della Torre in Santa Croce. Marble; the inscription on the base is a later addition. Florence, Museo Nazionale del Bargello.</figcaption>
</figure>

This statue is one of the finest surviving works of Tino di Camaino’s Florentine years. It is generally associated with the dismembered tomb of Gastone della Torre, patriarch of Aquileia, who died in Florence in 1318. His tomb stood in the right aisle of Santa Croce and is usually dated to 1318–1320. The central part of that monument survives in the Museo dell’Opera di Santa Croce: sarcophagus, effigy, and two curtain-bearing angels. The Virgin and Child would have crowned it, as the Virgin crowns the Petroni tomb in Siena, of which the della Torre monument was a reduced version. The association rests on style, scale, and type rather than on documented provenance, so it remains a hypothesis, though a widely shared one.

The Virgin sits frontally on a block-like throne, a compact mass in which every part of the figure is drawn into a single closed volume. A crown of fleurons sits over her veil, which falls straight to the shoulders and frames the face. The gown is gathered at the waist by a thin belt, and a band of finely carved ornament, imitating embroidery, runs across the neckline. Her right hand holds a closed book with two clasps, standing it upright on the edge of the throne. The face is broad and calm, its features simplified into clear planes, and marked by some surface damage around the eyes and cheek.

The Child is the group’s most distinctive element. He sits on his mother’s left knee but does not bless, hold an attribute, or look out at the viewer. He leans against her shoulder with his eyes closed and his mouth slightly open, apparently asleep. His right hand lies on his chest, beneath the Virgin’s spread fingers, and his left hand grasps the hem of her mantle. A bare foot hangs below the drapery. Few images of the Virgin and Child of this period show the Child so completely abandoned to sleep. The motif turns the theological image into an observation of a mother holding a sleeping infant. On a tomb it may also have recalled the sleep of death, though no text confirms such an intention.

The mantle shows Tino’s handling of drapery at its most assured. It is drawn across the lap in a broad sweep, falls between the knees in deep V-shaped folds, and breaks over the feet in heavy, slightly crumpled ends. A border with a twisted or fringed ornament runs along its edges and sharpens the main lines of the fall. The forms are large and simple, but carefully calculated so that the folds read clearly from below. The figure was meant to be seen at a height, at the summit of a monument, and its principal masses communicate before the details do.

The book in the Virgin’s hand and the inscription on the base point in the same direction, but they belong to different moments. The book identifies the Virgin with Wisdom, the throne on which the incarnate Word sits. The inscription SEDES SAPIENTIAE, “Seat of Wisdom,” cut in Roman capitals on the front of the base, is a later addition and has given the statue its traditional name. It names the theology the medieval sculpture already expressed. It should not be read as part of Tino’s original work.

Compared with the Pisan figures of 1313–1315, the statue shows a shift toward greater refinement. The block-like mass of the Henry VII councillors remains, but the surface is more finely worked, the ornament richer, and the emotional register more intimate. The same direction leads to the small devotional Madonnas of the Neapolitan years, in which the relationship between mother and child becomes the main subject. The Bargello statue stands at the turning point. It is still a monumental figure made to crown a tomb, yet already a meditation on the bodily closeness of mother and child.

## Marble, Colour, and the Construction of a Monument

Modern encounters with medieval sculpture often begin with pale stone. The eye follows the contours of a cheek or the shadow within a fold, and the apparent unity of the material encourages an appreciation of form as something independent of colour. Tino’s monuments require a more historically complex understanding. The catalogue of the d’Orso tomb identifies marble, gold, and pigments as its materials. The documents for Henry VII’s monument record painted decoration on the wall around it, installed together with the tomb. The San Ranieri altar has lost its polychromy, while the Neapolitan tomb of Archbishop Orso Minutolo still preserves a rich painted surface, and the great arch of the Donnaregina monument retains polychrome inlays. The original visual experience included elements that most surviving surfaces preserve only incompletely.

Colour could distinguish categories that unpainted marble now brings together. Flesh, cloth, heraldic devices, and architectural ornament might have been differentiated through surface treatment. Gilding could draw attention to selected details, creating points of brilliance whose visibility changed with illumination and the spectator’s position. These possibilities do not justify inventing a complete original palette. They do require recognition that carving and painting formed parts of a shared visual conception, and Tino’s Pisan self-description as a sculptor *arte coloris* may point in the same direction.

The physical making of a monument was equally composite. A large tomb consisted of numerous elements that had to be produced, transported, fitted, and secured. The marble itself had to be procured, and the Neapolitan documents repeatedly record royal orders to acquire it in Rome or at Terracina, a reminder that Naples had to import its stone from afar. Architectural members and figures were related through practical decisions about dimensions and weight. The convincing appearance of the completed ensemble depended on its construction as an actual object occupying a specific site.

This practical reality helps explain the importance of repetition. A workshop required procedures through which a complex commission could be completed. Familiar figure types and architectural solutions offered a common basis for coordinated work, while still allowing significant differences in gesture, scale, and symbolic emphasis. The recurrence of a curtain-bearing angel does not by itself indicate an absence of invention. Its meaning changes according to what it reveals and how it participates in the surrounding structure.

Marble imposes its own conditions. A projecting arm, a narrow architectural member, or a deeply separated fold creates different technical risks from a compact block. Tino’s concentration of form can be understood partly as a productive engagement with these conditions. The figure’s visual solidity corresponds to a practical intelligence about stone, even when the resulting effect is primarily emotional or ceremonial.

The surviving object should consequently be approached as a layered historical record. Tool marks, traces of pigment, joins, losses, and replacements can all affect interpretation. They reveal that the monument was made and remade through time, and Tino’s artistic conception survives within this material history rather than outside it.

## Naples and the Demands of Angevin Commemoration

Tino moved from Florence to Naples probably at the end of 1323 or the beginning of 1324, and he remained there until his death. In little more than a decade he became the principal interpreter of the Angevin dynasty’s need for self-representation. The southern commissions are fundamental to his career because they show how forms developed in Tuscany could be adapted to the representation of a ruling family. The tomb now had to do more than preserve the identity of an individual prelate or ruler. It could articulate relationships across generations, make dynastic continuity visible, and connect a particular family to sacred models of authority.

The Neapolitan years were long judged less favourably than the Tuscan ones. They were often treated as a repetitive appendix, marked by the reuse of tested models and by the growing presence of a large workshop. Only recently has the period been reassessed, partly because the number of works known to belong to it has grown considerably. Beyond the royal tombs discussed below, the corpus now includes the tomb of Archbishop Orso Minutolo and the *Virgin and Child* in the lunette of the main portal of Naples Cathedral, the tomb of Giovanni di Capua in San Lorenzo Maggiore, and remains of the tombs of Philip of Taranto<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-philip-i-taranto" class="footnote"><sup>10</sup></a> and John of Durazzo. A payment of 14 July 1332 concerns a marble tomb for Matilda of Hainaut, princess of Achaea, whose location is unknown. Outside the capital, numerous fragments at the abbey of Cava de’ Tirreni, including a *Massacre of the Innocents*, belong to a marble altarpiece in several compartments and probably to a tomb. Further pieces survive at Amalfi, at Massa Lubrense, and in the tomb of Enrico Sanseverino at Teggiano.

The Neapolitan setting encouraged sustained production. Several members of the royal family required monuments within a short period, which increased the importance of workshop organization and of designs that could be adjusted for related commissions. Repeating an architectural arrangement could establish a recognizable family resemblance between tombs, allowing each to participate in a broader commemorative environment.

Religious institutions were central to that environment. Royal patronage of churches and convents connected material benefaction with prayer and remembrance. A tomb placed within such a setting referred to the deceased’s relationship with the institution while benefiting from its continued religious life. Public grandeur and devotional function were closely connected: visibility sustained memory, while the community supplied the practices through which that memory retained spiritual significance.

This relationship should not be reduced to a simple opposition between sincere devotion and political display. A ruler could understand the support of a religious institution as a genuine pious obligation while recognizing its public importance. Monumental expenditure expressed status, but it could simultaneously serve intercession, thanksgiving, or the preservation of a family’s place within sacred history. Tino’s sculpture was effective because it accommodated these meanings within a single visible structure.

The significance of Naples is therefore not adequately expressed by treating the later works as more elaborate versions of Tuscan prototypes. The patronal problem had changed. A series of related monuments could construct a collective identity over time, and what appears as repetition at the level of individual motifs could become purposeful continuity at the level of dynastic representation. Tino gave that continuity bodily form, through figures who sit, kneel, lie in death, support, or intercede.

### The Tomb of a Chancellor: The Virgin and Child from the Monument of Bartolomeo di Capua

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-18.jpg" alt="A broken marble slab, its upper right corner missing along a diagonal break. At the top left, only the lower half of the Virgin’s face survives, with nose and mouth, framed by her veil. On her mantle, at the shoulder, is a small star. Her open right hand is raised beside the Christ Child, who sits frontally on her lap. He has a plump face, curly hair tied with a small bow, and bare feet. He raises his right hand in blessing and holds a small bird with spread wings in his left, while his mother’s hand supports him from below. A curved moulding, part of a circular frame, sweeps across the lower part of the relief. Along the bottom and down the right edge runs an inscription in Gothic capitals, partly legible.">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, fragment of the front of the tomb of Bartolomeo di Capua, Naples Cathedral, 1328–1329. Marble with traces of red and blue paint, 44.5 × 39.4 × 6.4 cm. Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art, Gift of W. R. Valentiner, G.57.34.11.</figcaption>
</figure>

This fragment comes from the tomb of Bartolomeo di Capua, protonotary and logothete of the Kingdom of Naples, the highest officer of the royal chancery under Charles II<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-charles-ii-anjou" class="footnote"><sup>11</sup></a> and Robert of Anjou<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-robert-anjou" class="footnote"><sup>12</sup></a>. He died in 1328 and was buried in his family chapel of Saints Peter and Paul in Naples Cathedral, against the penultimate pillar of the south aisle. The monument belongs to Tino’s late Neapolitan years. It shows that, beyond the royal family, the great officers of the Angevin court also turned to his workshop for their commemoration.

The tomb did not survive. In 1586 the descendants of Bartolomeo dismantled the decayed fourteenth-century chapel and rebuilt it in Renaissance style at the left end of the transept, and the original monument was lost. Some of its parts may have been reused in the new tomb, which was reduced in 1805 and taken down in 1842; its marbles then disappeared after being stored for a time in the archbishop’s garden. The fragment surfaced on the antiquarian market in the twentieth century. <a href="/scholars.html#valentiner-wilhelm-r">W. R. Valentiner</a> acquired it while he was director of the Detroit Institute of Arts, probably between 1938 and 1944, and gave it to the North Carolina Museum of Art in 1957.

A recent reconstruction places the relief on the front of the sarcophagus. The front would have been divided into three medallions, each with a single half-figure, and framed on all sides by a long inscription beginning at the upper left. The Virgin and Child would have occupied the left medallion, a Man of Sorrows probably the centre, and a saint the right. The surviving curve of moulding across the lower part of the fragment belongs to the circular frame of that medallion. The proposal is consistent with what survives. The other two medallions are lost, so it cannot be verified in full.

The figures are cut by the break in a way that sharpens their effect. Of the Virgin only the lower part of the face remains, with a straight nose and a small closed mouth, together with the veil and the mantle. A small star is carved on the mantle at the shoulder, recalling the Virgin’s traditional title of Star of the Sea. Her open right hand is raised beside the Child, and her other hand supports him from below. The Child, now the visual centre of the fragment, sits frontally and looks out at the viewer. He blesses with his right hand and holds a small bird with spread wings in his left. Such birds, often goldfinches, were commonly read as allusions to the Passion. On a tomb whose central medallion may have shown the Man of Sorrows, the allusion would have had particular point.

The style is that of Tino’s Neapolitan maturity. The Child’s full, round face, tight curls, and small tied bow belong to the type found in the small devotional reliefs of the 1330s. The drapery is simpler and smoother than in the Tuscan works, falling in long, regular folds over the Virgin’s lap. The surface is finished with care, and the museum records traces of red and blue paint. These indicate that the relief, like Tino’s other monuments, was once coloured, perhaps with a blue ground and red accents on the garments or the inscription.

The inscription ran around the edges of the slab in Gothic capitals. On this fragment, part of the lower border and a vertical strip along the right edge survive. The lower line can be partly read as …RIA · · CVNCTA · REGNOR…, and the vertical letters as PROT…. The latter may be the beginning of *prothonotarius*, Bartolomeo’s title, but the reading is uncertain and cannot be confirmed without the rest of the text. The epitaph was evidently long and formal, as befitted the head of the royal chancery. Even in fragmentary form, it confirms that the relief belonged to an inscribed public monument rather than an independent devotional image.

Valentiner, who did more than anyone to rediscover Tino in the twentieth century, kept the relief near him and later wrote that he was glad to have even a fragment of Tino’s work around him. The remark captures the condition in which much of Tino’s art survives. Broken, dispersed, and detached from its setting, a fragment like this can still convey the sculptor’s handling of form, but the monument it once belonged to can only be reconstructed in the mind.

### A Trecento Madonna in a Quattrocento Portal: Tino di Camaino and Antonio Baboccio at Naples Cathedral

  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-19.jpg" alt="The pointed lunette of a Gothic portal, framed by deep mouldings. Its archivolt is carved with a row of small half-length figures and, at the apex, winged angels. At the centre of the lunette, on a pedestal inscribed in Gothic letters, sits the crowned Virgin, holding the Christ Child standing on her knee. To the left stands a bearded saint in a heavy mantle. The central portal of Naples Cathedral brings together sculpture from two different centuries. The portal itself, with its pointed arch, carved archivolts, and inscription, was made by Antonio Baboccio da Piperno for Cardinal Enrico Minutolo, archbishop of Naples, and completed in 1407. At the centre of its lunette, however, Baboccio set a *Virgin and Child* carved by Tino di Camaino some seventy years earlier. Lions supporting the columns of the portal, worn by time, have also been attributed to Tino. The lunette is thus a composite work. Its central image belongs to the Angevin era, and its frame and flanking figures to the early fifteenth century.

The Virgin sits crowned on a throne set on a pedestal whose front is inscribed in Gothic letters. She holds the Christ Child standing on her knee, turning slightly toward him. The forms are broad and compact, the drapery falls in heavy folds over the knees, and the group has the calm monumentality of Tino’s Neapolitan Madonnas. The surface is worn by exposure. Its original position is not documented. It may have come from an earlier portal of the cathedral, from an interior altar or tomb, or from another building.

Around this central image, Baboccio created a sacred conversation in the taste of his own time. On the left stands Saint Peter, bearded and wrapped in a heavy mantle. On the right stands Saint Januarius, the patron of Naples, in bishop’s mitre and cope. Before him kneels Cardinal Minutolo, wearing his broad-brimmed cardinal’s hat, his hands joined in prayer. Presented by the city’s patron saint, the cardinal places himself and his work under the protection of the Virgin. Baboccio’s figures, with their more agitated drapery and slender proportions, contrast visibly with the compact forms of Tino’s Madonna.

The inscription on the band below records the commission. Its four lines run in two columns, in Gothic capitals with many abbreviations. Several words can be read in the photograph. The third line of the left column calls the cardinal *presul*, prelate, and plays on his title with the words *cardo columne*, “the hinge of the column,” a pun on *cardinalis*. The right column names *Henricus*, the cardinal, and refers to the work done at his expense. The date is given in the last line, which begins

> HOC OPUS EXACTUM MILLE CURRENTIB(US) ANNIS

and continues with

> QUATER CENTUM SEPTEM: the work was completed in 1407.

The reuse of Tino’s Madonna is itself significant. Seventy years after his death, his work was still valued enough to occupy the most prominent place on the façade of the city’s cathedral. For Cardinal Minutolo, the Angevin Madonna may also have carried a historical meaning, linking his new portal to the cathedral completed under Robert of Anjou. Baboccio’s lunette shows how Tino’s Neapolitan production became part of the visual memory of the city, reused and reframed by later generations.

## Catherine of Austria: The First Angevin Tomb

Tino’s first royal commission in Naples was the tomb of Catherine of Austria in the Franciscan church of San Lorenzo Maggiore. Catherine, a Habsburg princess born in 1295, was the first wife of Charles, Duke of Calabria<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-charles-calabria" class="footnote"><sup>13</sup></a>, the heir to the throne. She died in January 1323. In May 1324 Charles himself urged the purchase in Rome of the marble needed for her monument, which places its execution in the first months of Tino’s Neapolitan activity. The tomb stands in its original position in the ambulatory of the choir.

Its form is unusual within his work and within the Angevin series. It is not a wall tomb but a free-standing monument set between two piers of the ambulatory, open to view from both sides. A sarcophagus with the effigy of the duchess is carried by figures of virtues, among which *Charity* and *Hope* are recorded in the national catalogue, and curtain-bearing angels frame the dead. The national catalogue also lists reliefs of Saint Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>14</sup></a> and of the *Stigmatization*, and statues of Saint Paul and Saint Elizabeth, as elements of the ensemble. The tomb is richly inlaid with glass mosaic, on the spiral columns, around the arch, in the lunette, and on the front of the sarcophagus; how much of it is original remains to be established.

The Franciscan imagery suits both the church and the family. San Lorenzo was the principal Franciscan house of the city, and the Angevins claimed a saint of their own in the order: Louis of Toulouse<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-louis-toulouse" class="footnote"><sup>15</sup></a>, Charles’s uncle, canonized in 1317. If the Saint Elizabeth is, as seems likely, Elizabeth of Hungary, a princess venerated in Franciscan circles, she connected the dynasty’s Hungarian ancestry to the same devotional world. The tomb thus placed a foreign-born duchess within her husband’s family through the saints the family claimed as its own.

The monument long puzzled scholars. Its open structure and its execution aroused doubts, and Tino’s authorship was fully accepted only from the mid-twentieth century. More recent work has reassessed the complexity of its structure rather than treating it as an awkward beginning. Its significance lies in its position as a prototype. In this first Neapolitan tomb Tino tested how virtues, effigy, angels, and dynastic saints could be combined for a royal patron. Within two years he would solve the same problem, against a wall and on a far greater scale, at Donnaregina.

Catherine’s monument also reveals the commemorative logic of the court. She died young, without heirs, and her husband soon remarried. Her tomb could not proclaim a line of descent. It proclaimed instead her place within a royal and Franciscan order of virtue and sanctity. Where genealogy failed, devotion supplied the frame of meaning.

This is the first royal tomb that Tino made in Naples. Catherine of Austria, a Habsburg princess born in 1295, was the first wife of Charles, Duke of Calabria, heir to the throne. She died in January 1323. In May 1324 Charles himself urged the purchase in Rome of the marble needed for her monument. Its execution therefore belongs to the first months of Tino’s Neapolitan activity. The tomb stands in its original position in the choir ambulatory of San Lorenzo Maggiore, the principal Franciscan church of the city.

Its form is unusual within Tino’s work and within the Angevin series. It is not a wall tomb but a free-standing monument, set between two piers of the ambulatory and open to view from both sides. Two spiral columns rest on lions and carry a pointed trefoil arch, crowned by a crocketed gable with pinnacles. Beneath the arch, the effigy of the duchess lies on her sarcophagus, which is supported from below by standing figures, among them the virtues *Charity* and *Hope*. The structure combines the canopy of the great Roman and Tuscan baldachins with the funerary chest of the wall tombs. It produces a monument that the faithful could walk around and see from both sides of the ambulatory.

The most striking feature is the extensive use of glass mosaic. The spiral columns are inlaid with bands of coloured tesserae. The arch is framed by a band of blue mosaic with white flowers. The lunette under the arch holds a mosaic of the enthroned Virgin and Child in an oval frame against a gold ground, flanked by two kneeling figures. The front of the sarcophagus is set with mosaic around its carved medallions. This polychrome decoration belongs to the Roman tradition of the Cosmati, with its inlaid columns and coloured marble. It may reflect the involvement of Roman craftsmen, which would fit the purchase of the marble in Rome. The tomb shows that Tino’s monuments were conceived as coloured ensembles, an aspect his other works have largely lost.

The front of the sarcophagus carries three lobed medallions with half-length friars in Franciscan habits, holding books. The national catalogue also records reliefs of Saint Francis and of the *Stigmatization*, and statues of Saint Paul and Saint Elizabeth, as elements of the ensemble. The Franciscan imagery suits both the church and the family. San Lorenzo was the principal Franciscan house of the city, and the Angevins claimed a saint of their own in the order, Louis of Toulouse, Charles’s uncle, canonized in 1317. Through these saints, the tomb placed a foreign-born duchess, who had died young and without heirs, within her husband’s family and its devotion.

The monument long puzzled scholars. Its open structure and the quality of its execution aroused doubts, and Tino’s authorship was fully accepted only from the mid-twentieth century. More recent work has reassessed the complexity of its structure rather than treating it as an awkward beginning. In this first Neapolitan tomb, Tino tested how virtues, effigy, saints, and architecture could be combined for a royal patron. Within two years, at Donnaregina, he would solve the same problem against a wall and on a far greater scale.

## Mary of Hungary: Queenship, Motherhood, and Sacred Continuity

The tomb of Mary of Hungary<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-mary-of-hungary" class="footnote"><sup>16</sup></a> in Santa Maria Donnaregina is generally regarded as the masterpiece of Tino’s Neapolitan years and among the most coherent statements of his mature art. Mary, widow of Charles II and mother of King Robert, died in 1323. The documents give the monument an unusually firm chronology. It was under way in February 1325, when Robert ordered his officials in Rome to help master Gagliardo Primario acquire the necessary marble. In May 1326, in accordance with the queen’s testament, final payment was made to Gagliardo and to Tino, whose name the Neapolitan scribe wrote as *Dyno* and *Dino*. Other local documents call him *Cino* or *Gino*, a small reminder of how a Sienese name sounded at the Angevin court.

The partnership with Gagliardo Primario, known chiefly as an architect, has been read in two ways. It may reflect genuine creative collaboration, which would help explain the architectural unity of the monument. It may instead record a technical role in procuring materials and solving practical problems. The documents name both men; they do not divide the design between them.

The importance of the tomb lies in the coordination of several identities within one monument. Mary appears as a deceased woman, a queen, a mother within a ruling dynasty, and a Christian petitioner. These identities are distributed through the monument’s architecture, allowing the viewer to understand them in relation to one another. Unlike the suspended Tuscan tombs, the monument rises from the floor beneath a great Gothic arch enriched with polychrome elements.

The basic arrangement establishes a movement from support to commemoration and from commemoration to intercession. Figures identified as virtues carry the funerary structure. The sarcophagus presents members of the dynasty. Above it, the queen’s body lies within an opened setting, while the upper part directs attention toward sacred figures and her devotional identity. The church’s own account identifies the virtues and the family members represented on the tomb.

The supporting figures turn an ethical claim into an architectural fact. Virtue appears to sustain the monument physically, and the structure’s stability becomes inseparable from the qualities attributed to the queen and her dynasty. Whether every observer could name each virtue matters less than the relation of support the arrangement makes immediately legible.

The family imagery on the sarcophagus gives motherhood a public dimension. Mary’s significance is expressed through the descendants associated with her, and maternity becomes part of the visible continuity of rule. The presence of a saintly son, Louis of Toulouse, within the dynastic sequence allows genealogy and sanctity to intersect. The family is presented through a carefully organized image of continuity rather than a neutral record of biological descent.

The recumbent effigy introduces a quieter register. Whatever the scale of the surrounding claims, the queen’s body remains still. Curtains and attendants frame it as a focus of care and ceremonial attention. The monument does not erase death beneath political symbolism; it places the dead body within an ordered environment that interprets the loss.

The upper devotional imagery develops another aspect of Mary’s identity. The royal person appears in relation to the Virgin and to acts of religious offering, placing benefaction and humility within the same monument that proclaims dynastic importance. The kneeling posture gives submission a visible form. A queen’s status is not denied; it is reoriented toward a higher authority.

These different bodily conditions create a complex account of power. The virtues sustain, the descendants continue, the dead queen is attended, and the petitioner approaches the sacred. The architectural enclosure gives the whole a controlled rhythm: the upward movement is punctuated by horizontal zones, and the individual figures keep their weight within the elaborate Gothic frame. The tomb commemorates one woman while proposing how her life should be understood within the history of a dynasty and the expectations of salvation.

The photograph shows the monument as it stands today in the church of Santa Maria Donnaregina Vecchia, for which the queen had provided. Unlike the suspended Tuscan tombs, the monument rises from the floor beneath a great Gothic canopy. Its piers and arch are inlaid with red and blue glass mosaic, as at the tomb of Catherine of Austria. The structure reads as a sequence of levels. At the bottom, four winged figures, the virtues, carry the sarcophagus. The tomb became the model for the Angevin monuments that followed. The tombs of Charles of Calabria and Marie of Valois in Santa Chiara repeat its scheme of virtues, dynastic chest, effigy, and Virgin, though with increasing workshop participation. At Donnaregina the scheme appears in its most coherent and refined form. The canopy unifies the ensemble, the levels are clearly distinguished, and each figure keeps its weight within the elaborate Gothic frame. It is one of the most complete statements of how a medieval tomb could join personal remembrance, dynastic argument, and the hope of salvation.

## Charles of Calabria and the Problem of an Interrupted Future

The tomb of Charles of Calabria in Santa Chiara addresses a different dynastic situation. Charles was the son and intended successor of King Robert of Anjou, but he died in November 1328, before his father. His commemoration therefore involved an interrupted future. The person remembered had occupied a position defined partly by what he was expected to become. A monument could preserve his dignity, but it could not undo the disruption that his death introduced into the succession.

The documents again begin with stone. On 12 April 1329 Robert ordered the purchase of the necessary marble in Rome and at Terracina. The actual execution is often placed later, between 1333 and 1335. The design follows the tomb of Mary of Hungary but with more emphatic solutions, and here, for the first time, the extensive participation of workshop assistants is clearly visible. In the same church Tino is also credited with what remains of the tomb of an infant daughter of Charles who died in 1328, probably made alongside her father’s monument.

Santa Chiara’s institutional history identifies Tino as the author of the tombs of Charles of Calabria and Marie of Valois, while distinguishing them from the later monument to Robert made by the Bertini brothers. This distinction is essential. The presence of related forms within the church does not make all its royal tombs works by Tino, nor does it establish that their present arrangement reproduces a single original plan.

Charles’s monument gives lasting form to a status whose political fulfilment had been prevented. The funerary image maintains rank through clothing, posture, heraldry, and architectural display. Yet the central fact remains death before succession. The tomb remembers the past while preserving traces of an anticipated future that did not occur.

The broader political importance of Charles’s death is discussed by <a href="/scholars.html#lucherini-vinni">Vinni Lucherini</a> in her study of relations between the Neapolitan and Hungarian branches of the Angevin dynasty. She connects the succession problem with subsequent negotiations and with the commemorative politics of the court. Her analysis demonstrates why royal tombs need to be read alongside documentary evidence concerning family claims and political circumstances. It does not justify assigning every later dynastic decision to the sculptural programme of Charles’s tomb.

The distinction matters because a monument does not explain historical events by itself. Its imagery selects and orders aspects of identity. A tomb may present coherence at precisely the moment when political reality has become uncertain, and that clarity is itself historically meaningful. It reveals an effort to preserve a stable image of the ruling family despite the vulnerability exposed by premature death.

Tino’s established vocabulary suited such an effort. Supporting virtues, a prominent effigy, and a sacred upper register organized the deceased’s identity across earthly and spiritual dimensions, and their reuse connected Charles’s memory to other members of the family. Similarity between monuments made continuity visible where biological succession had been broken. A family had lost a son and husband, the court an heir, and a religious community received the responsibility of commemoration. Sculpture could not resolve those losses, but it gave them an enduring public form.

## Marie of Valois and the Political Visibility of Women

The tomb of Marie of Valois in Santa Chiara extends the question of dynastic continuity through a female figure. As the second wife of Charles of Calabria and the mother of his daughters, among them the future Queen Joanna I, her position connected marriage, descent, and succession. She died in 1331. Her monument belongs to the same commemorative world as the tomb of Mary of Hungary, but the relationships it articulated were different. Similar sculptural structures should not lead to the assumption that the two women’s identities were interchangeable.

The monument is usually dated to 1335–1336, at the very end of Tino’s life. It repeats in simplified form the model of the Donnaregina tomb and shows extensive workshop participation. A document of June 1339 adds a revealing epilogue: the balance owed for the tomb was paid to Landa, Tino’s widow, described as the widow of a *marmorarius*. The monument’s financial history therefore outlasted its maker. Whether its final carving and installation were also completed after his death cannot be determined from the payment alone, but the case illustrates why the history of an artwork is not always reducible to one date.

Female commemoration in these monuments deserves more than a passing reference to family relationship. Dynastic systems depended upon women as transmitters of claims, participants in marriage alliances, patrons, and mothers of potential successors. In Mary’s case the point was acute: with no surviving son, the succession passed through her daughter. Her tomb could therefore give visible form to political relationships that were not identical to the exercise of kingship, with the female body at the centre of a monument whose imagery reached outward toward the continuity of the ruling house.

At the same time, the language of virtue and devotion presented these identities within an accepted religious framework. A royal woman could be honoured through her connection to a dynasty and through her relation to sacred models of humility, charity, and intercession. These dimensions were not mutually exclusive; their conjunction was part of the monument’s meaning.

The comparison with Tino’s Marian sculpture is revealing. The Virgin and Child offer a sacred image in which maternity is joined to authority. Royal female commemoration could draw upon a visual world already accustomed to understanding a mother’s relation to her child as the bearer of larger significance. This does not make every royal mother an explicit imitation of the Virgin, but it establishes a field of associations within which maternal identity could possess considerable dignity.

Tino’s restrained treatment of gesture keeps these meanings from becoming merely emblematic. A recumbent woman remains a body whose life has ended, even when the surrounding imagery asserts genealogical importance. The women of his Neapolitan tombs thus emerge as more than subsidiary figures in a narrative of male rule. Their monuments show how dynastic identity was constructed through marriage, motherhood, benefaction, and devotion, and they gave female commemoration a prominent place in the public religious landscape.

Marie of Valois, sister of King Philip VI of France, was the second wife of Charles, Duke of Calabria, heir to the throne of Naples, and the mother of his daughters, among them the future Queen Joanna I. After Charles’s death in 1328, the succession passed through her daughter. Mary died in 1331. Her tomb in the Franciscan church of Santa Chiara stands near that of her husband. It was among the last works of Tino di Camaino, who designed it but did not live to see it completed.

The documents give the tomb an unusual epilogue. The monument is usually dated to 1335–1336, at the very end of Tino’s life. In June 1339, two or three years after his death, the balance owed for the tomb was paid to Landa, his widow, described as the widow of a *marmorarius*. The monument’s financial history outlasted its maker, and its completion is generally attributed to the workshop. The extensive participation of assistants, already visible in the tomb of Charles of Calabria, is even more evident here.

The structure repeats, in simplified form, the model created for Mary of Hungary at Donnaregina. Three standing female figures, the virtues, support the tomb from below. The central one, a woman nursing a child, is Charity; the two at the sides, crowned and holding attributes, are presumably two other virtues. Above them, the chest is carved with a court scene: a crowned figure enthroned at the centre, flanked on one side by laymen in the short tunics and cloaks of the period and on the other by friars and veiled women. Higher still, two angels draw back curtains to reveal the effigy of the duchess lying on her bier, with a row of standing figures behind her. On top, the Virgin and Child stand between two female saints. The whole is enclosed by a Gothic canopy with a crocketed gable, carried on spiral columns resting on lions.

Each level states a different aspect of the duchess’s identity. The virtues present her moral qualities as the foundation of the monument. The court scene on the chest places her within the ruling house and its circle, and the friars and veiled women recall the Franciscan community of Santa Chiara, where the tomb stands. The effigy, framed by curtain-bearing angels, preserves her bodily presence in death. The Virgin and saints at the summit place her within the order of salvation. The structure is that of Mary of Hungary’s tomb, but its meaning has been adjusted to a different woman: not a queen mother at the head of a dynasty, but a duchess whose importance lay in the daughter through whom the succession now passed.

The tomb also shows how far the workshop could carry Tino’s formula. The figures are slenderer, the drapery more linear, and the faces more generalized than in the master’s own works, but the architectural scheme and the iconographic programme remain his. The monument is thus a document of continuity. A design conceived by the master was completed by his assistants after his death, and the Angevin funerary tradition he had created continued without him.

The church of Santa Chiara was severely damaged by bombing and fire in 1943 and rebuilt afterwards. The royal tombs suffered with it. The present condition of the monument, including the dark colour of the effigy, may reflect that damage and the subsequent restoration. As with Tino’s other monuments, the surviving work should be read as the product both of its original design and of its later history.

## The Sculptor as Architect: San Martino, Sant’Elmo, and the Royal Works

In Naples Tino was employed as much as an architect and site director as a sculptor. This side of his career is easily overlooked because almost nothing of what he built survives in recognizable form. It is nonetheless documented more densely than many of his tombs, and it completes the picture of a master trained, like his father, in the management of great building sites.

The principal enterprise was the Carthusian monastery of San Martino on the hill above the city, founded in 1325 by Charles of Calabria. Tino directed the works together with the Neapolitan Francesco de Vico, as documents of 1325–1326, 1331, and 1335 attest. In 1329 he was also entrusted with the works of the royal residence on the same summit, the fortress that became Castel Sant’Elmo. Documents of 1334–1335 connect him with the fabric of Castel Nuovo and with works in the arsenal and port of Naples, although they are unclear about the extent and nature of his role.

The last documents of his life belong to these sites. On 8 July 1336 Tino received a substantial payment for his work at San Martino over the preceding two years, seven months, and twenty-eight days. Three days later, on 11 July, two acts transferred the direction of San Martino and Sant’Elmo to Attanasio Primario of Naples and referred to Tino as *quondam*. The precision of the payment, counted almost to the day, suggests the settlement of a completed service, and the sudden replacement suggests that death had intervened. It is the strongest evidence for the date of 1336.

The surviving fabric tells little of Tino’s architecture. San Martino was completed only in 1368 and transformed between the late sixteenth and the eighteenth centuries into one of the great monuments of the Neapolitan Baroque. Of the Gothic monastery only the vaulted substructures and traces in the church remain. Castel Sant’Elmo was completed in 1343 under Queen Joanna I and rebuilt in the sixteenth century on its star-shaped plan. Two claims repeated in popular accounts should therefore be corrected. Tino did not direct work at Sant’Elmo “until his death in 1343”: 1343 is the date of completion, several years after his death. Nor can the Certosa as it stands be called his design; the documents establish his direction of the early works, not the authorship of the complex visitors see today.

The significance of this activity lies elsewhere. It shows that the Angevin court valued Tino as a master of large organized enterprises, able to direct workforces, coordinate supplies, and supervise construction across several sites at once. The same capacities underlie his monumental tombs, whose architectural frames, assembled from many parts and installed within existing churches, were themselves small buildings. For Tino, as for his father, sculpture and architecture were not separate professions but two applications of the same expertise.

## Small Devotional Sculpture and the Intimacy of Sacred Encounter

Tino’s smaller devotional works reveal capacities that the great tombs can overshadow. Monumental scale is not essential to the gravity of his figures. A compact relief or statuette preserves the same concern with bodily weight, controlled gesture, and the relation between sacred presence and human approach. The reduction in size changes the conditions of viewing, encouraging closer attention to the contact between figures and to the organization of shallow space.

During the Neapolitan years, and especially toward their end, Tino produced a substantial group of small marble reliefs of exceptional refinement, possibly intended for the royal family. They seem to compete deliberately with Sienese and Florentine panel painting, transposing the portable altarpiece into stone. The group includes a rare double-sided marble triptych, now divided: the face with the *Virgin and Child between Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint John the Baptist* belongs to the collection of the Monte dei Paschi di Siena, while the face with *Christ as Man of Sorrows between the Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist* is in the Salini collection at Asciano. Other reliefs of the *Virgin and Child* are at Galatina and in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the latter with replicas probably from the workshop. A small triptych has been reassembled from pieces now at Castrovillari, in a private American collection, and at Massa Lubrense.

The marble relief of the *Virgin and Child with Queen Sancia, Saints, and Angels* in the National Gallery of Art in Washington is a particularly useful example. Dated by the museum to around 1335, it brings a royal petitioner into the presence of the Virgin and Child within a small, densely organized composition. Its height is only slightly more than half a metre, yet its arrangement retains an unmistakably ceremonial character.

The composition establishes hierarchy through scale and position. The sacred figures occupy the centre, while the petitioner approaches from below. Yet the difference in status is crossed by gesture. The Virgin’s movement toward the queen and the Child’s blessing create a connection across the spatial hierarchy. Authority becomes accessible through a carefully controlled act of response. Angels and saints frame the exchange, giving the scene a communal rather than purely private character.

The work’s small scale should not lead to certainty about its original use or ownership beyond what the evidence supports. A royal figure represented in a devotional image is not, by itself, proof that the object occupied a particular private chamber or served a particular ritual. The secure point is visual: the relief concentrates a royal devotional relationship into a form suited to close attention.

Independent statuettes of the *Virgin and Child* complete the picture. Examples in the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford, at Fondi, and in the Detroit Institute of Arts probably date from the Neapolitan years; Detroit dates its marble to about 1330–1337 and records a height below fifty centimetres. In such works the relationship between mother and child is built on the practical realities of support. The adult body carries the smaller body; the child’s movement modifies the mother’s pose. Physical dependence gives theological meaning a human basis, and the Incarnation becomes visible through an intelligible relation between bodies.

The smaller sculptures broaden our understanding of Tino’s emotional range. His art could address the public memory of rulers, but it could also organize an intimate sacred encounter. Scale changes, while the conviction that meaning emerges through embodied relation remains.

This small relief is one of the finest of the marble devotional images that Tino produced in his last Neapolitan years, probably for the royal family. Dated by the museum to about 1335, it represents Queen Sancia of Majorca, wife of King Robert of Anjou, in the presence of the Virgin and Child. It was first attributed to Tino in 1923 and is now among the best-known works of his late career. Its height of just over half a metre and its fine finish suggest an object meant for close viewing, set in a frame like a painted panel.

The shape is that of a small altarpiece, a rectangle crowned by a triangular gable, framed by a plain moulding. At the centre the Virgin sits crowned on a throne whose base is decorated with a row of small arches. Behind her, two angels with spread wings hold up a cloth of honour that falls in folds behind her shoulders. Two more angels stand at the sides with their hands crossed on their chests in reverence. The Child stands on his mother’s left knee, his curly head turned toward her, holding the edge of her garment.

The queen kneels at the lower left, veiled, her hands joined in prayer. Behind her stands a female saint, identified as Saint Clare<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-chiara-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>17</sup></a>, who lays a hand on the queen’s shoulder and presents her to the Virgin. On the right stands Saint Francis in the habit of his order, bearded and tonsured, holding a small cross and a book. The choice of saints reflects Sancia’s celebrated devotion to the Franciscans. With Robert, she founded the great Clarissan convent of Santa Chiara in Naples, the church in which Tino carved the tombs of Charles of Calabria and Marie of Valois.

The decisive gesture belongs to the Virgin. She reaches down with her right hand and touches the head of the kneeling queen. This is not a conventional gesture of blessing but a direct physical contact, crossing the distance between the sacred figures and the human petitioner. The hierarchy of scale and position remains clear, with the Virgin enthroned and the queen small and kneeling, yet it is bridged by a touch of personal protection. The relief makes the queen’s devotion visible and represents its response.

The kneeling figure wears a veil rather than a crown, and her dress resembles a religious habit. This may express Sancia’s well-known attachment to the Franciscan ideal of poverty, which led her, after Robert’s death, to enter a convent of Poor Clares. It should not be read as evidence that she had already taken vows by about 1335. The image presents the queen as a humble penitent rather than a sovereign, in keeping with the devotional purpose of the relief.

The relief shows Tino’s late Neapolitan style at its most refined. The figures are slender and graceful, the drapery falls in long, fluid folds, and the faces of the angels and the Virgin have an elegant, softened beauty close to contemporary Sienese painting. The composition is densely organized but clear: the vertical axis of the Virgin and the cloth of honour, the symmetrical angels, and the two flanking groups form a stable frame for the central exchange of gestures. The relief transposes into marble the format and the intimacy of a small painted panel, a type Tino seems to have developed in deliberate competition with the painters of his time.

The work also illustrates the role of such objects at the Angevin court. A royal figure represented in a devotional image is not, by itself, proof of its original use or setting. What the relief does show is how the queen wished her piety to be seen: kneeling, veiled, presented by the founder of the order she favoured, and personally touched by the Virgin. Like the royal tombs, it makes devotion and dynastic identity visible in the same image, but on an intimate scale suited to private contemplation.

### A Marble Triptych for Private Devotion: The Virgin and Child between Saint Catherine and Saint John the Baptist

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-24.jpg" alt="A white marble slab carved as a small triptych, with three round-arched compartments beneath crocketed pointed gables. Each gable holds a carved foliate rosette, and the arches rest on small corbels carved with heads. In the centre, the crowned and veiled Virgin is shown at half length, holding on her left arm the curly-haired Christ Child, who raises his right hand in blessing and holds a book in his left.   <figcaption>Virgin and Child between Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint John the Baptist, front face of a double-sided marble triptych, 1329–1332. Marble. Siena, Collection of the Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena; formerly Borletti collection.</figcaption>
</figure>

This relief is one face of a rare double-sided marble triptych from Tino’s late Neapolitan years. Its other face, showing *Christ as Man of Sorrows between the Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist*, is now in the Salini collection at Asciano. The two faces, today separated, once formed a single slab carved on both sides. Such an object could be placed on an altar and seen from front and back, or turned in the hand during devotion. Gert Kreytenberg has studied the reconstructed triptych and dated it to about 1334, at the very end of Tino’s career. The date given here, 1329–1332, is the one used by the collection. Either way, the work belongs to the small reliefs of exceptional refinement that Tino made in his last years, probably for the royal family or its circle.

The format reproduces in marble the shape of a painted triptych. A single slab is divided into three compartments, each with a round arch beneath a pointed gable decorated with crockets and a foliate rosette. The central compartment is higher and wider, as in painted altarpieces, and the arches rest on small corbels carved with heads. The architecture is crisp and precise, and the figures fill the arched fields at half length, close to the viewer.

The Virgin, crowned and veiled, holds the Child on her left arm and inclines her head gently toward him. The Child, with tight curls and a round, serious face, raises his right hand in blessing and holds a closed book in his left, the Word of God made flesh. His bare foot rests in his mother’s hand. The forms are full and softly modelled, and the drapery falls in long, fluid folds. The image is grave and calm, without the playful exchanges of the London and Glens Falls reliefs, as befits the central panel of an altarpiece.

On the left, Saint Catherine of Alexandria wears a crown, as befits a royal virgin martyr, and holds the palm of martyrdom and the spiked wheel on which she was tortured. On the right, Saint John the Baptist, long-haired and bearded, wears his camel-hair garment beneath a mantle and holds an unrolled scroll, which would once have carried the words of his announcement of Christ. The two saints frame the Virgin and Child as the figures of a painted altarpiece frame its central image. Their choice may reflect the devotion of the owner, perhaps a member of the Angevin court.

The double-sided format gives the object its meaning. On one face, the Virgin presents the Child, the Incarnation and promise of salvation. On the other, the Man of Sorrows shows the Passion through which that promise was fulfilled. The two faces together set the beginning and the end of Christ’s earthly life against each other, one visible while the other was hidden. Their separation, probably when the slab was sawn apart for the art market, broke this relation. Reunited in reconstruction, they show how carefully Tino, at the end of his career, translated the forms and the theology of panel painting into marble.

### The Madonna of the Pomegranate: A Panel from a Dispersed Triptych at Castrovillari

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-25.jpg" alt="A white marble panel shaped like a small altarpiece, seen against a black background. The pointed gable is edged with carved rosettes. Below, under a round arch, the crowned Virgin is seen to the knees, her veil falling over her shoulders and her mantle gathered in heavy folds over her lap. In her right hand she holds a pomegranate. </figure>

This panel belongs to the group of small marble devotional images that Tino produced in his last Neapolitan years. It has been recognized as the central panel of a small portable triptych, now dispersed. The other surviving parts are a *Saint John the Baptist* in a private American collection and a *Saint Peter* in the church of the same name at Monticchio, near Massa Lubrense on the Sorrento peninsula. The reconstruction places the work in the region around Naples, where Tino’s late activity has gradually been recovered in recent decades. How the panel reached Castrovillari, in northern Calabria, is not documented.

The format imitates a small painted altarpiece. A rectangular field is crowned by a triangular gable edged with carved rosettes, and inside, a round arch frames the main image. In the gable, a bearded saint appears at half length, holding keys in one hand, the attribute of Saint Peter. Below, under the arch, the crowned Virgin is seen to the knees, her veil falling over her shoulders and her mantle gathered across her lap in heavy, deeply cut folds.

The Virgin holds a pomegranate, the attribute that gives the panel its local name. The Child sits on her left arm and reaches with his right hand to touch the fruit. The pomegranate, with its many seeds within a single fruit, was read in medieval devotion as an image of the Church gathering many members in one body, and also as a sign of the Resurrection. The Child’s gesture makes the symbol the subject of an exchange between mother and son rather than a fixed attribute, in keeping with Tino’s habit of expressing theology through bodily relation.

The figures show the qualities of Tino’s late style. The Virgin’s face is broad, gentle, and slightly inclined; the Child is round-headed and curly-haired, like the children of the Washington relief and the Bartolomeo di Capua fragment. The relief is fairly deep, so that the figures stand out strongly from the background, and the drapery, though rich, is organized in a few large movements. The whole is calm and intimate, conceived for close viewing.

The presence of Saint Peter in the gable raises a question about the triptych’s original programme. A separate *Saint Peter* has been proposed as one of its wings. If both identifications are right, the saint appeared twice, in the gable of the central panel and in a lateral panel, which is unusual. Either the gable figure belongs to a different programme, or the identification of the Monticchio panel needs to be reconsidered. The measurements, frames, and carving of the three surviving panels have not been compared.

The edges where the panel once joined the wings are not visible in the photograph. Cracks run across the arch and the gable. Even in this condition, the work preserves the refinement of the small reliefs with which Tino, at the end of his life, brought the intimacy of the painted devotional panel into marble.

### The Virgin and Child in London: The Prototype of a Neapolitan Devotional Relief

  <img src="/Images/Tino-di-Camaino/tino-di-camaino-26.jpg" alt="A small marble relief with a pointed, house-shaped frame of stepped mouldings, its lower corners cut off diagonally.   <figcaption>Virgin and Child, c. 1330, probably made in Naples. Marble. London, Victoria and Albert Museum, A.31-1964.</figcaption>
</figure>

This relief is the prototype of a small group of nearly identical marble images of the Virgin and Child from Tino’s late years. The museum dates it to about 1330 and suggests that it was probably made in Naples. Replicas of the same composition survive in the Hyde Collection at Glens Falls, New York, and in two private collections. They are generally considered later versions, possibly by Tino’s workshop. Set beside the replicas, the London relief shows the quality of the original invention and the refinement of its execution.

The format is that of a small gabled altarpiece. A rectangular field is crowned by a triangle and framed by a broad stepped moulding, with the lower corners cut off diagonally, as if the relief were made to fit a niche or a frame. The relief imitates the shape of a painted panel, a deliberate choice in a group of works that seem to compete with the painted devotional images of Siena and Florence. Joins in the left side of the frame indicate old breaks and repairs.

The Virgin is shown at half length, crowned with an open crown over a veil that falls in soft folds to her shoulders. Her head is inclined toward the Child, and her face is long and gentle, with heavy-lidded eyes and a small, full mouth. In her right hand she holds a small cluster, perhaps flowers or fruit, an attribute the Child does not reach for. His attention is turned elsewhere.

The Child sits on his mother’s left arm, wearing a tunic fastened at the neck with a small brooch. He turns his head to look at her while holding a small bird, whose beak touches his fingers as if it were pecking at them. One bare foot appears below, against the heavy folds of the mantle. The motif of the bird, frequent in Tino’s late reliefs, combines the playfulness of a child with an allusion to the Passion, since goldfinches and other small birds were commonly read as signs of Christ’s future suffering. Here the playful aspect prevails. The bird’s peck and the Child’s backward glance at his mother create a lively, almost anecdotal moment.

The carving is of very high quality. The relief is fairly deep, the figures stand out strongly from the plain ground, and the surfaces are smooth and softly modelled. The drapery falls in broad, fluid folds; the Child’s tight curls and the details of the crown are cut with precision. The exchange of glances between mother and son, the inclination of the Virgin’s head, and the gestures of the Child with the bird give the image the warmth and intimacy that characterize Tino’s last works.

Comparison with the Glens Falls replica shows what made the London relief a model worth repeating. The composition is simple and easily legible, the relationship between mother and child immediate, and the format suited to private devotion. Repeated by the workshop for other patrons, it became one of the standard images of Tino’s late production, a sign of the demand for such objects at the Angevin court and in its circle.

This relief belongs to a small group of nearly identical marble images of the Virgin and Child associated with Tino’s late years in Naples. They are generally considered replicas, possibly made by Tino’s workshop. The relation between original and replicas has been examined in detail, and the attribution is left open between Tino and his workshop.

The existence of replicas is itself significant. It suggests that these small devotional reliefs were in demand at the Angevin court and among its circle, and that the workshop could repeat a successful composition for several patrons. Such repetition fits what is known of Tino’s Neapolitan workshop, which in these years also produced a series of related royal tombs. A replica is not necessarily a lesser work. The marble has a warm, honey-coloured tone, probably the result of patination or of later surface treatment. The surface is smooth and finely finished, and the relief is fairly deep, so that the figures stand out strongly against the plain ground. The crown, the veil, and the folds of the mantle are carved with care. The whole is conceived for close viewing, in keeping with the private devotional use for which such reliefs were probably made.

This statuette belongs to the small marble Madonnas of Tino’s last Neapolitan years. The museum dates it to about 1330–1337 and attributes it to Tino himself, in distinction from the two other works in this group, which it gives to the workshop and to a follower. At less than fifty centimetres high, it was conceived for close viewing, perhaps in a private chapel or on a domestic altar.

The type goes back to Tino’s early Madonnas in Turin and Lucca. The Virgin stands with the Child on her left arm and lifts the hem of her mantle with her right hand, and the two exchange glances. The compact volume and the intimate exchange between mother and child remain. The figures, however, have become more graceful and the surfaces more refined, in keeping with the courtly taste of Angevin Naples. The brownish stains on the marble are probably surface deposits rather than remains of colour; this has not been verified against the museum’s conservation record.

## The Tomb as a Setting for Prayer and Repeated Looking

A medieval tomb was encountered within an active environment. Its significance did not depend only on the moment of installation or on the intentions of its patron. People returned to the church, approached nearby altars, attended ceremonies, and passed through spaces in which the monument remained visible. Several of Tino’s tombs stood directly above altars, probably at Pisa and certainly at Siena, so that the liturgy was celebrated beneath the effigy. The work’s meaning developed through these repeated encounters, and its capacity to preserve memory depended on being seen again.

Tino’s monuments are well suited to such repetition because they offer several levels of attention. From a distance, the architectural outline establishes a clear presence. At a nearer range, the effigy identifies the commemorative centre. Closer still, reliefs and smaller figures invite examination. The monument operates both as a public marker and as an object of sustained contemplation.

This layered visibility also shapes the relation between image and prayer. The effigy gives the deceased a recognizable bodily form, while sacred figures place that person within a devotional framework. The viewer can move mentally between remembrance and intercession. A tomb does not need to narrate the entire life of its occupant to support this movement; a few decisive relationships suffice.

Curtain-bearing angels are particularly effective in creating such an encounter. The opened curtain implies an act of revelation: something previously enclosed has become visible. In a funerary setting, this gives the display of the effigy a ceremonial quality. The deceased is presented for attention rather than merely exposed, and the gesture acknowledges a boundary even as it opens it.

The angels also complicate the monument’s temporality. Their action is permanently suspended. They continue to reveal the deceased whenever a viewer approaches, making a single ceremonial gesture available across repeated occasions. Sculpture turns an event into an enduring condition. The same is true of kneeling petitioners and intercessory figures: their actions remain present even though the individuals and circumstances they concern belong to the past.

Such effects explain why the tomb’s architectural setting is inseparable from its meaning. Height, distance, and proximity to worship influence what can be seen and how the work is approached. A detached angel in a museum still communicates graceful movement, but its action becomes less specific when the effigy it once revealed is absent. The fragment retains aesthetic force while losing part of its relational meaning. Tino’s funerary art should therefore be understood as an organization of attention, directing the viewer from material presence toward remembered identity and from remembered identity toward religious expectation.

## Workshop Practice, Repetition, and the Question of Authorship

The scale and number of Tino’s commissions make collaboration an essential part of his achievement. A monumental tomb required a range of operations that could not be performed by one person. The documents confirm this from the start: at Pisa Tino drew weekly pay with which he evidently paid his collaborators, and at Siena he appears in the accounts alongside his father and more than twenty other masters. The modern desire to identify the master’s hand in every surface can obscure the importance of design, supervision, and coordination. Authorship in such circumstances includes the conception of relationships between parts, not only the execution of individual details.

This does not make distinctions of quality or handling irrelevant. Differences between figures may reveal varying degrees of participation, changes in production, or later intervention. They must, however, be interpreted within the conditions of workshop practice. A more summary passage is not automatically evidence of artistic decline, and a polished detail does not by itself establish personal execution by the named master.

The Neapolitan monuments make these questions especially pressing. The tomb of Charles of Calabria is the first in which the extensive participation of assistants is clearly visible, and the tomb of Marie of Valois, which repeats the Donnaregina model in simplified form, shows it even more. Shared architectural patterns and figure types allowed the workshop to answer repeated commissions while maintaining a recognizable identity. Such continuity was valuable to patrons who wished their monuments to share a common dynastic language. The workshop’s ability to repeat was part of its usefulness, and the same organization that served the tombs also served the royal building sites Tino directed.

Repetition could also make differences more meaningful. When the basic structure remains familiar, changes in the central effigy, family imagery, or devotional scene become easier to perceive. A series of tombs can communicate collective belonging while preserving the distinct identity of each deceased person.

Museum classifications illustrate the need for precision. The Detroit Institute of Arts distinguishes a *Virgin and Child* attributed to Tino from a separate *Virgin and Child with Saints* assigned to a follower. Such terminology expresses different levels or kinds of relationship. “By,” “workshop of,” and “follower of” should not be silently treated as equivalent.

The workshop’s significance extended beyond the master’s lifetime. When Tino died, Attanasio Primario took over his building sites, and the Neapolitan tombs of the following decades continued to adapt his types. Designs and procedures could pass through assistants or reach artists who knew the completed works, so influence need not depend on a documented apprenticeship. A sculptural solution installed in a prominent church became a public model. Tino’s distinctive contribution lies in the coherence of his solutions to recurring problems: how to give a figure weight, how to make a gesture legible, how to organize a tomb vertically, and how to relate the deceased to a sacred order. The workshop made those solutions materially possible on an ambitious scale, and recognizing it enlarges rather than diminishes his achievement.

## Fragmentation, Reconstruction, and the Limits of Knowledge

The history of Tino’s sculpture did not end when his monuments were completed. Later generations moved tombs, altered churches, collected fragments, and reconstructed ensembles according to changing expectations. The surviving corpus is therefore not a transparent record of the fourteenth century. It is the result of a long process in which preservation and loss have remained closely connected.

The pattern runs through every phase of his career. The Pisan font was wrecked in the cathedral fire of 1595. Henry VII’s tomb was dismantled in 1494 and recomposed in 1921. The Petroni monument lost its canopy and altar between 1484 and 1591 and reached its present chapel only in 1951. The d’Orso tomb was taken down within the fourteenth century itself, and the baptistery groups were removed in the early sixteenth. In Florence not a single tomb by Tino survives complete.

The tomb of Henry VII offers the clearest example of what follows from such a history. Once dismantled, its parts acquired separate histories, some remaining in the cathedral, others entering the Camposanto, museums, a villa, and a London collection. Modern displays make the fragments accessible, but accessibility should not be mistaken for certainty about their original relationships. A convincing display may still embody a modern hypothesis.

The d’Orso monument presents a similar problem, and the cathedral’s own account describes the current assembly as hypothetical. This acknowledgment matters because it prevents the authority of the present setting from concealing the uncertainty of reconstruction. A visitor sees a coherent monument; the historian must consider which parts are secure, which relationships are probable, and which elements have been lost.

Fragmentation also changes the apparent character of a sculpture. A figure once seen from below may now stand at eye level. A face belonging to a distant architectural group may appear unusually simplified when examined closely. An angel separated from its curtain or tomb may seem more autonomous than it was intended to be. These changes affect judgments about style as well as function.

Reconstruction is nevertheless indispensable. Without attempts to relate fragments, much of the original ambition of Tino’s work would remain invisible. The problem is not reconstruction itself but the failure to distinguish its evidential levels. Measurements, attachment points, material correspondence, provenance, documents, and iconographic compatibility support different kinds of argument. Visual resemblance alone rarely resolves every question.

The Neapolitan monuments require particular care because their settings include major destruction and restoration. Santa Chiara was severely damaged in the bombing of 1943 and subsequently rebuilt, and its institutional history records that rupture. Lucherini’s work on the royal tombs further challenges the assumption that Santa Chiara was conceived from the outset as a fully planned dynastic burial church. Her argument emphasizes documentary and archaeological evidence and the consequences of later restoration, encouraging an understanding of the ensemble as something that developed rather than the realization of a single initial programme.

Uncertainty is therefore not an obstacle external to the study of Tino. It belongs to the history of his works. A responsible interpretation preserves what the fragments make possible while resisting the temptation to complete every gap.

## Historical Significance and the Continuing Force of Tino’s Art

Tino di Camaino’s importance cannot be measured only by the number of surviving works or by his place between more familiar names. His sculpture gave a particularly coherent form to problems central to fourteenth-century visual culture. How could a dead person remain socially present? How could an image preserve authority while acknowledging mortality? How could a material object direct attention toward a future beyond ordinary sight? His monuments respond to these questions through the organization of bodies in space.

The relationship between sculpture and architecture is central to that response, and it was grounded in his professional life. Tino was twice a cathedral *capomaestro* and later the director of royal building sites; he thought as a builder as well as a carver. His figures do not merely occupy decorative positions within a structure. They carry, reveal, attend, petition, and preside. A supporting virtue converts moral worth into visible stability; an angel opening a curtain turns display into ceremony; a kneeling petitioner establishes the direction of dependence; an effigy makes absence physically present.

His treatment of the human body gives these relationships credibility. The figures have sufficient weight to seem subject to gravity, yet their controlled gestures allow them to express meanings beyond immediate physical action. The body becomes a meeting point between earthly experience and religious expectation, nowhere more clearly than in the tombs, where the stillness of the deceased is surrounded by images of continuing attention.

The diversity of his patrons demonstrates the adaptability of this language. A cathedral administration, a Ghibelline commune, a cardinal’s commemoration, a Florentine guild, a bishop’s executors, and a royal house each required different forms of distinction. His career also shows how exposed such work was to politics: the sculptor of an emperor’s tomb fought against the city that commissioned it and lost his office as a result. Through these changes Tino preserved a consistent sculptural intelligence, not a uniform treatment of every subject but a recognizable approach to the problem of making identity endure.

His Neapolitan production shows how an individual artistic language could become part of a broader commemorative tradition. Later monuments adopted forms his work had helped establish, although each requires its own attribution and historical explanation. The tomb of Robert of Anjou, made by other sculptors, belongs to this wider history. At the same time, his smaller devotional works prevent his achievement from being confined to public grandeur. His art can be intimate without becoming slight, just as it can be monumental without losing the human body at its centre.

The most enduring quality of his sculpture may be its balance between solidity and exposure. Marble promises permanence, but the figures repeatedly disclose dependence. Rulers require remembrance; the dead require prayer; children require support; petitioners require a response. Authority is made visible alongside vulnerability.

Tino’s art remains compelling because it gives loss a form that is neither abstract nor merely descriptive. The deceased do not return to life, yet they continue to occupy space within a network of human and sacred relationships. Sculpture preserves their presence while acknowledging their absence. In that difficult conjunction, Tino di Camaino found the basis of an artistic language capable of serving memory, devotion, and power without allowing any one of them to exhaust the meaning of the work.

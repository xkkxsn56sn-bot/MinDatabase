## Prefatory note on method and evidence

Any life of the *Maestro di San Ponziano* must begin with a frank acknowledgement of what such a "life" can and cannot be. The Maestro di San Ponziano is not a documented historical person. No contract, no payment record, no notarial deed, no obituary and no signature preserves his name; we do not know where he was born, when he died, whom he trained under or whom he trained. The territory in which he worked — Spoleto and the Valnerina, the south-eastern quadrant of Umbria — is, as [Andrea De Marchi](/scholars.html#de-marchi-andrea) has observed, almost wholly devoid of archives and documents earlier than the very end of the fourteenth century, the record having been thinned by time and by the region's recurrent earthquakes. The consequence is that virtually the entire population of artists active at Spoleto in the Trecento survives only as a set of anonymous "Masters of convenience" (*maestri di comodo*), critical constructs assembled around a nucleus of surviving works and christened, by convention, after the place or object that best identifies them.

The Maestro di San Ponziano is precisely such a construct. His conventional name derives from the frescoes in the crypt of the abbey church of San Ponziano, just outside the walls of Spoleto, and specifically from the oldest paintings in the central apse of that crypt. To write his biography is therefore to write the biography of a hand: to reconstruct, from the mute evidence of paint and carved wood, an artistic personality — its formation, its stylistic profile, its chronology, its place within a school — and, no less importantly, to write the biography of the critical idea itself, tracing how the figure was assembled, named, disputed and enriched by successive generations of scholars from [Roberto Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto) to [Alessandro Delpriori](/scholars.html#delpriori-alessandro).

This essay proceeds accordingly. It is grounded in the specialist Italian-language literature on the Spoletan Trecento, above all Alessandro Delpriori's monograph *La scuola di Spoleto. Immagini dipinte e scolpite nel Trecento tra Valle Umbra e Valnerina* (Quattroemme, 2015), which contains the first synthetic chapter devoted to the master, together with Andrea De Marchi's substantial introductory essay to that volume; the catalogue of the exhibition *Capolavori del Trecento. Il cantiere di Giotto, Spoleto e l'Appennino* (2018), in which works by the master were shown; and the foundational territorial and philological studies of [Giovanni Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni), [Bruno Toscano](/scholars.html#toscano-bruno) and [Corrado Fratini](/scholars.html#fratini-corrado). Throughout, I have tried to observe strict scholarly hygiene: to distinguish the eponymous nucleus from securely associated works, from locally or devotionally reported attributions, and from proposals advanced in the art trade; to name the scholar responsible for each disputed claim; and to flag uncertainty where it exists rather than to resolve it artificially. Where the popular and devotional literature on the abbey of San Ponziano preserves attributions that are chronologically incoherent, I say so.

## The place and its name

The abbey of San Ponziano stands outside the medieval walls of Spoleto, along the road toward Colle Ciciano, amid woods and olive groves and close to the city cemetery and the palaeochristian church of San Salvatore. The site is one of the most ancient sacred places of the city. Tradition holds it to be the spot where Ponziano — a young Spoletan of good family, martyred, according to the *passio*, in 175 under Marcus Aurelius — was buried after his decapitation, or where his severed head is said to have rolled to rest. In historical terms the area was certainly an early-Christian cemetery; an inscription in the crypt commemorates the matron Procula, a *spectabilis foemina* who, probably in the sixth century, is said to have rediscovered the place where the martyr's remains had been laid by the matron Sincleta, and to have raised an altar there.

By the central Middle Ages the site had become a flourishing Benedictine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedictine-order" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> nunnery. Its existence is documented before the year 1000 and securely attested by 1078, from the obituary note of the abbess Gerlenda contained in the great Atlantic Bible of San Ponziano — the so-called Bibbia atlantica, now preserved in the Biblioteca Guarneriana at San Daniele del Friuli. On 28 January 1080 the bishop Rodulfus consecrated three altars of the church. The community held extensive properties and dependencies at Spoleto, Perugia and Montefalco. The church in its present Romanesque form was raised between the end of the twelfth and the beginning of the thirteenth century; papal privileges accumulate through the thirteenth (Innocent IV<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-innocent-iv" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, 1251–52; Nicholas IV<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-nicholas-iv" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, 1290) and into the fourteenth century (the indulgences granted in 1301 by Cardinal Napoleone Orsini). In 1392, during the civil strife that convulsed Spoleto, the Benedictine nuns considered abandoning their peripheral site for the safety of the walls but ultimately remained; in 1521 the Benedictines were succeeded by Poor Clares<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-chiara-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, prepared for the transition by Clarisses from Santa Maria di Monteluce at Perugia, and the community passed under the spiritual care of the Observant Friars Minor<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>. The adoption of strict papal enclosure led the nuns to reserve the choir and the crypt for their own exclusive use, walling up and interdicting the crypt's access to the laity — a fact of considerable importance for the survival and the invisibility of its medieval frescoes.

It is this crypt — the twelfth-century substructure of the church, divided into short aisles ending in terminal apses and covered with cross-vaults borne on columns of reused antique material — that gives the master his name. The crypt was decorated with frescoes over the course of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and its walls today constitute a palimpsest of many hands. Among the oldest of these paintings, in the central apse, is a *Crucifixion with the two Marys and the animula carried to heaven*, a work of a fourteenth-century Umbrian painter to whom, by convention, the surrounding critical construct has been attached. That painter is surely the Maestro di San Ponziano.

## The historiographical horizon: how a Spoletan Trecento came to be seen

To understand the Maestro di San Ponziano one must understand the scholarly enterprise that made his recognition possible, for he is a comparatively late arrival in a slowly assembled panorama. The medieval art of Spoleto had long attracted attention for its Romanesque and Duecento phases — the region is exceptionally rich in twelfth- and thirteenth-century survivals — but the art of the *later* Middle Ages, the Trecento and Quattrocento, remained for a long time under-studied, lacking the systematic treatment that would knit its scattered evidence into a coherent context.

The decisive impulse came from Roberto Longhi. In a celebrated monographic course delivered at the University of Florence in the academic year 1953–1954 — later published posthumously and foundational for all subsequent research — Longhi placed the "Passione degli umbri," the expressive, sorrowful mode of Umbrian painting, at the centre of critical attention, and trained a generation of pupils who would carry the inquiry forward: among them [Carlo Volpe](/scholars.html#volpe-carlo), [Ferdinando Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando), [Mina Gregori](/scholars.html#gregori-mina), Giovanni Previtali and Bruno Toscano. Two of these pupils, working from the same Longhian premise but by almost opposite methods, would define the field.

Bruno Toscano approached the territory from above, so to speak: his method was geographical and contextual, attentive always to what has been *lost* as much as to what survives, insisting that we work on a minimal percentage of what once existed and that today's administrative geography cannot be mapped onto the cultural and historical geography of the Middle Ages. Toscano's fundamental essays on the fortunes of the "primitives," published in the 1960s, and his editorship of the two indispensable volumes of the *Manuali del territorio* — on the Valnerina, the Nursino and the Casciano (1977) and on Spoleto (1978) — established both a body of data and a way of reading the territory that remains in use. For Toscano there exists, at least for the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a culture that it is right to call *Spoletan*: possessed of its own rules, its own defined style, its own peculiar tradition, to be studied in relation to the major contemporary experiences of Italian art without being subordinated to their categories.

Giovanni Previtali, by contrast, worked from below, through the finest philological attention to individual objects. His pioneering studies of the 1960s onward — beginning with the *Maestro della Santa Caterina Gualino,* a wooden Madonna encountered at a Roman antiques fair — brought into existence, for the first time, the very consciousness that an Umbrian Trecento *sculpture* existed at all, opening a new field just as Longhi had reopened that of painting. Two of Previtali's insights are decisive for the Maestro di San Ponziano. The first, formulated at the beginning of the 1980s in his "lessons on Umbrian Trecento sculpture," is the cultural division of Umbria into two great zones separated by the Tiber: to the right, the Umbria of Perugia and Orvieto, more strongly Tuscan and Sienese in its sculpture, oriented toward the workshop of the Orvieto cathedral; to the left — the Umbria of Foligno, Spoleto and the Valnerina, reaching across the Apennine to embrace parts of Abruzzo and the Marche — a zone whose sculpture was less "Tuscan," far more expressive, and characterized by an intimate bond between carving and polychromy. The second insight follows from the first: in this left-bank Umbria the wooden simulacra were, in Previtali's provocative phrase, "sculptures only in a manner of speaking," works that a Florentine or a Perugian would have refused to call sculpture at all, because they were not self-sufficient after the carver's work but required the colour that not merely decorated their surface but, in substance, defined their forms.

Onto these two methods a third scholar grafted the interpretive thesis that most directly shapes our understanding of the master. Corrado Fratini, in a series of contributions in the later 1990s (notably 1997, 1998, 2000), proposed that in the Spoletan Ducato the workshop of the wooden carver and that of the painter *coincided* — that one and the same artist was responsible for both the intaglio and its polychromy — a practice contrary to the specialization well attested elsewhere, at Siena or Venice. Fratini's argument took its point of departure from the inscription at the foot of the wooden Madonna delle Concanelle at Bugnara (now in the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo, L'Aquila), signed in 1262 by the Spoletan painter Machilone with his son, in which the verb employed (*depinserunt*) and the fact that the signature consumes the available space without room for a separate carver's name together suggest that painter and sculptor were one.

It is against this triple background — Toscano's geography of the lost, Previtali's left-bank Umbria and symbiosis of carving and colour, Fratini's identity of painter and sculptor — that the systematic study of the individual Spoletan personalities finally took shape in Alessandro Delpriori's *La scuola di Spoleto* (2015), the printed outgrowth of his 2010 Florentine doctoral thesis, *Produzione figurativa a Spoleto e in Valnerina nel XIV secolo*. Delpriori's book was the first to attempt a synthetic, richly illustrated and diachronic account of the school, integrating painting and wood-carving throughout, and it is there that the Maestro di San Ponziano receives, for the first time, a chapter of his own (chapter VI), set between the chapter on the Maestro di Cesi and the first Master of the Beata Chiara da Montefalco (chapter V) and the chapters on the Maestro della Croce di Trevi and the Maestro di Fossa (chapters VII–VIII). The introduction to that volume, by Andrea De Marchi — "Spoleto 1300: contesti, tipi e linguaggi di un paesaggio artistico" — provides the interpretive frame within which the master's works are read, and is drawn upon repeatedly below.

One further, cautionary chapter of this historiography deserves mention, because it bears on how the master's corpus should be handled. [Filippo Todini](/scholars.html#todini-filippo)'s vast repertory of Umbrian painting (1989) — which did devote a section to the Spoletan painters of the Trecento, calling them among the most original and fascinating of all Umbrian Trecento artists — was severely judged by De Marchi, who saw in its atomizing, list-making, Berensonian format a decontextualizing extremism that stripped the works of their historical dimension and, by reaction, provoked an equally damaging attributional carelessness. The lesson for a biography of the Maestro di San Ponziano is that a bare list of attributed panels, detached from the contexts that give them meaning, misrepresents the very nature of Spoletan production; the works must be read within their sites and their devotional functions.

## The Spoletan "school": tradition, the shadow of Assisi, and the *fauve* reception of Giotto

The Maestro di San Ponziano belongs to a strongly characterized local tradition, and his individuality is legible only against it. De Marchi's introduction sketches that tradition with unusual force, and its main lines may be summarized as follows.

Spoleto had been a capital — of the Lombard duchy — and the long wave of that splendid early-medieval season reverberates through the twelfth century, when a pictorial school developed there that was not reducible to a mere appendix of the Roman, but possessed lively and autonomous characters, cultivating at an early date the new type of the painted Cross in emulation of north-western Tuscany. It remained a capital in the Duecento, when exquisite painters — and probably sculptors too — such as *Simeone and Machilone* flourished. These identity-traits reached a last, very high expression between the Duecento and the Trecento, under the electrifying influence of the pictorial workshops housed in the nearby basilica of San Francesco at Assisi.

Assisi is, indeed, the true point of reference for the whole school. The great international building-site of San Francesco — with its stained glass, mural painting, goldsmithery and sculpture, and its succession of foreign and cosmopolitan presences between roughly 1260 and 1320 — is what produced, in De Marchi's reading, the "magic moment" of Spoletan art. From the workshop of Cimabue emerged the Maestro delle Palazze, capable of receiving directly the novelties of the transept paintings of the Upper Basilica; in his wake came the *Maestro di Sant'Alò*, attentive to the Master of the Capture (*Maestro della Cattura*) and to the first experiments of Giotto; and on the scaffolding of the Franciscan Stories, perhaps literally alongside Giotto around 1290, De Marchi places the Maestro di Cesi, the first of the Spoletan Giotteschi.

Yet — and this is the crux — the Spoletan painters received the Giottesque revolution *critically*, bending it to their own tradition rather than being homogenized by it. Where at Rimini the Giottesque acculturation was an almost sudden explosion, at Spoleto it sedimented upon an already robust tradition, yielding highly original results. The reception of the new figurative ideals was folded into a chromatic and material *fauve*-ism, an exasperated mimicry oscillating between cries of pain and heart-rending tenderness, a nostalgic and inoxidizable high expressive temperature that binds the two centuries together. Subtle analogies may relate Spoletans and Riminese — the Maestro di Cesi of 1308 and Giuliano da Rimini of 1307 — in the name of a common Giottesque denominator and a common archaizing residue, but the expressive temperature is opposite, and the types and genres are entirely different, tied to a different artistic corography and to different stagings of the sacred.

Those types and genres are themselves distinctive, and they form the material world within which the Maestro di San Ponziano operated. In this part of Italy, in the age of the compartmented Gothic polyptych, the Gothic polyptych is conspicuously *absent*; in its place proliferate altarpieces of a quite different make — monumental tabernacles with a carved statue (more rarely a painted image) at the centre and lateral wings that could be closed, normally narrating the story of the figure to whom the altar or church was dedicated. Alongside these stand painted and carved Crosses set *in medio Ecclesiae*, and reliquary anconas, antependia (*paliotti*), and the occasional rare dossal. This refractoriness toward the imported Gothic polyptych, De Marchi insists, should not be read as mere archaizing resistance but as the proud assertion of an alterity, whose ancient roots constitute the strength of an identity that dialogues — strongly — with Assisi and with Giotto, yet does not conform.

Two structural facts of this world condition everything the Maestro di San Ponziano did. The first is the inseparability, already noted, of the work of the painter from that of the wood-carver: statues were conceived in an apparently schematic way, with smoothed transitions and evasive projections, precisely because they invoked gesso ground and living polychromy as their indispensable complement; and the pictorial intervention on panel was in turn subordinated to the integration of the sculptural artifacts, in the special form of the winged tabernacle. The second is the destination of much of this production for female monastic contexts — the enclosed choirs of nunneries — with all that this implies for the intimacy of viewing, the close-range reading of small painted narratives on the wings of tabernacles, and the theatricalization of devotion around Cross and tabernacle. Both facts are exemplified, as we shall see, precisely by the crypt of San Ponziano, a nuns' crypt whose imagery was designed for a privileged, enclosed viewing.

## The eponymous works: the crypt of San Ponziano

The core of the master's identity, and the origin of his name, lies in the frescoes of the central apse of the crypt. Here is painted a *Crucifixion with the two Marys and the animula carried to heaven*, described by all observers as the work of a fourteenth-century Umbrian painter and taken as the eponymous nucleus of the construct.

### The central apse: the *Crucifixion with the animula*

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-San-Ponziano/image 2.jpg" alt="Crucifixion with the two Marys and the animula carried to heaven">
  <figcaption>Crucifixion with the two Marys and the animula carried to heaven, 1325-35, fresco, crypt of San Ponziano, Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The scene occupies the small semicircular apse at the east end of the crypt's central aisle, a space whose ribbed pointed vault and narrow splayed window are integral to the composition. The painter has treated the entire architectural cell as a single decorative field: the ribs and the arch soffit carry bands of imitation *cosmatesco* ornament — chequered lozenges, four-petalled rosettes set in circles, stellate motifs and interlocking triangles in red ochre, yellow ochre, white and black — while a knotted guilloche frames the apse opening. This kind of painted marble-and-mosaic simulation is a Spoletan/Umbrian habit of the first half of the Trecento and here serves to dignify a very modest architectural setting.

In the conch itself, Christ hangs against a ground now reading as a mottled red-and-blue field. The apparent patchiness is almost certainly a conservation phenomenon rather than a design choice: the blue is likely azurite laid *a secco* over a red-earth *morellone* preparation, with extensive loss of the upper layer exposing the underpaint. The *titulus* is inscribed on a rectangular tablet above the head.

Christ is shown dead — head fallen onto the right shoulder, eyes closed, the torso sagging with a marked lateral curve, arms drawn into a shallow V, knees slightly flexed, wearing a white *perizoma* knotted at the hip with a loose fall of cloth. The modelling is pale and rather graphic, with contour drawing carrying more of the expressive weight than chiaroscuro. Notably, local descriptions record the cross itself as Y-shaped, and read this as a device calculated for the oblique viewpoint of someone approaching from the crypt entrance rather than as the German *Gabelkreuz* type. The cross is described as "strangely drawn in a Y," possibly for perspectival reasons, and the animula carried heavenward by two angels is thought to be probably the only depiction of St Pontianus in the crypt.

To Christ's left (viewer's right), in the upper register of the conch, a group of haloed angels — three heads are legible, with rose-coloured wings — supports a white-shrouded figure carried in a cloth. This is the *animula*: the soul rendered as a small human form borne to heaven in a *sudarium*, one of the most persistent Late Antique survivals in medieval funerary iconography. The frontal angel presses its hands together in prayer while the flanking pair take the weight of the cloth.

Below the conch, on the drum wall flanking the window, two half-length female figures survive against a white ground with a red dado. On the left, a veiled figure in white and red, her gaze directed downward; on the right, a haloed figure in a green mantle over a red tunic, hands extended forward and downward. The lower portions of both are lost to plaster damage and later whitewash.

A significant contextual observation from the local literature: two holes in the wall have prompted the hypothesis that a bracket here supported the wooden statue of St Pontianus — by the same master who frescoed the apsidiole — which would explain the Magdalene gazing tenderly at the martyr and the Virgin extending her hands toward Pontianus's severed head. If correct, this transforms the reading of the ensemble: the two women are not simply mourners of Christ but are gesturing toward a sculpted martyr physically present in the apse, making this a mixed painting-and-sculpture installation.

### The wooden statue of St Pontianus

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-San-Ponziano/image 4.jpg" alt="The wooden statue of St Pontianus">
  <figcaption>The statue of St Pontianus, carved and painted wood, 1325–1330, from the crypt of San Ponziano; Museo Nazionale del Ducato di Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The figure is a full-length recumbent martyr, laid out fully supine, the arms brought across the body and the hands placed one over the other upon the breast. The gesture is that of a corpse composed for burial rather than that of a northern *gisant*, which conventionally presents the deceased as though asleep and vertically conceived; here there is no such ambiguity, and the body is unequivocally dead. A long robe of martyr's red covers the figure, carved in deep, regular, near-parallel ridges that run the length of the torso and legs, with a whitish or gilt lining turning back at the shoulder and again at the hem. The fold system is graphic and repetitive rather than naturalistic: the carver thinks in incised channels rather than in the weight and fall of cloth, a linear conception wholly consistent with a sculptor who was also, and perhaps primarily, a painter. At the far end the shod feet project, retaining traces of green polychromy.

The head, however, is the decisive element. It was carved as a separate piece and is displayed detached, lying beyond the shoulders, and — as the local literature is careful to insist — this separation is not damage but original: the statue was sculpted with the head severed from the body from the outset.

That single fact reorganises the entire object. What is at issue is not a mutilated statue of a saint but a deliberate representation of the decapitated corpse, an unusually literal and almost forensic image for central Italy in the 1320s and 1330s, and one that answers directly to local hagiography: Pontianus was beheaded on the Tessino bridge, thereafter known as the Ponte Sanguinario, and his head is said to have leapt a considerable distance uphill to the site on which the church dedicated to him was subsequently raised.

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-San-Ponziano/image 5.jpg" alt="The head of St Pontianus">
  <figcaption>The head of St Pontianus, 1325–30, carved and painted wood, from the crypt of San Ponziano; Museo Nazionale del Ducato di Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The severed head may further be understood, in functional terms, as a sculptural double of the relic. The monastery preserves the skull of St Pontianus, which is still carried in procession each 14 January and displayed until the following Sunday for the celebration of the martyr-patron's feast. A carved head resting apart from a carved body, in the very crypt where the cult was localised, would have rendered the reliquary skull legible to pilgrims in both its presence and its absence: treat it as just an interpretation offered here as a hypothesis.

### The integrated program: the *gisant*, the false catafalque, and the reading of the space

It is here that the most significant piece of recent scholarship on the master enters, and it belongs to Delpriori, glossed by De Marchi in his introduction. De Marchi singles out the study of the crypt of San Ponziano as an exemplary reading of the *use of space*: a re-reading of the oldest frescoes of the central apse — the very paintings that give the anonymous master his name — together with their anomalous iconography, in relation to the original placement of the wooden statue of the mounted knight-martyr, now in the Museo Nazionale del Ducato di Spoleto.

The reconstruction runs as follows. The wooden statue of Saint Ponziano is a *gisant* — a recumbent effigy — and, remarkably, a *decapitated* one: the head is severed from the body not by damage but by design, carved that way deliberately. In its original disposition in the crypt this recumbent, headless simulacrum was integrated with a painted false catafalque (*finto catafalco*) on the wall beneath a Crucifixion "studied by art" for the privileged vision of whoever entered from the left side — the side of the monastery, that is, the nuns' side. The whole was so arranged as to propose the icastic parallel already mentioned, between the *animula* of the martyr carried to heaven by angels and the sacrifice of Christ, and to establish a direct nexus with the plaintive gesture of the Virgin, who lowers and extends her arms toward the body of Ponziano.

The associated fresco fragments of *Mary Magdalene and Mary* belong to this same logic. In them, as the topographical description notes, the Magdalene seems to observe something below and Mary seems to extend her hands, likewise downward; two holes in the wall have suggested that here, on a shelf or bracket, the wooden statue of Saint Ponziano lay extended. Only in this light do the attitudes of the figures make sense — the Magdalene gazing lovingly upon the martyr, the Virgin extending her hands pitifully toward the severed head of Ponziano. The painted figures, in other words, were designed to interact with the carved, recumbent body of the saint physically present below them.

Two conclusions of importance for the master's biography follow. First, this staging exemplifies precisely the *joint responsibility* for polychrome wooden apparatus and pictorial decoration — mural and on panel — as the coordinated care of a single workshop; the crypt of San Ponziano is thus a demonstration-piece of the Fratini–Previtali–Delpriori thesis of the painter-sculptor. Second, the "particular spectacularization" of the ensemble — a mode paralleled in the false sepulchres, probably functioning as cenotaphs in connection with ground burials, in San Domenico at Spoleto and in Santa Maria at Vallo di Nera — is congenial to an art deeply marked by the *visualized and recited religiosity* of the *laude* and the sacred representations. The Maestro di San Ponziano, on this reading, is not a maker of isolated devotional images but the author of an integrated, quasi-theatrical devotional environment, tuned to the enclosed liturgical life of a community of nuns.

### The crypt's other frescoes: a palimpsest, and a caution about attributions

The crypt is, however, a palimpsest, and here scholarly caution is essential, because the devotional and touristic literature attaches the name "Maestro di San Ponziano" to works in the crypt that cannot belong to him. The clearest instance concerns a *Madonna enthroned with the Child*. One much-consulted local source (the topographical survey by Silvio Sorcini on *I luoghi del silenzio*) singles out one of the crypt's several enthroned Madonnas as an anonymous Umbrian painter of Giottesque inspiration, conventionally called the Maestro di San Ponziano, of uncommon quality, praised for the secure structure of the figures and the studied architecture of the throne.

The discrepancy is instructive and must be handled honestly: the crypt contains multiple enthroned Madonnas of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries by different hands, variously assigned in the local literature to Paolo da Visso, to Bartolomeo da Miranda (a *Madonna in trono* dated 1478), and to the Maestro di Eggi, alongside frescoes of the later Quattrocento by Bernardino Campilio da Spoleto (including the two representations of Simonino da Trento of 1479–1481) and a large *Angel with two donors* attributed to the Maestro di Fossa.

A further work reported in the abbey — a panel **Crucifix**, formerly in the refectory and now near the presbytery, attributed by the same devotional literature to the Maestro di San Ponziano, with the saint on horseback painted on its predella — should likewise be treated with reserve. It is a plausible local tradition, consistent with the master's association with the site, but it rests (in the sources available here) on the topographical guide rather than on the peer-reviewed corpus, and it is listed below among reported rather than secure attributions.

### Madonna enthroned with the Child

The field is bounded above by a broad horizontal band carrying a chequered ornament of opposed triangles in black and white, interrupted at the centre by a lozenge and terminating at either end in a chevron — precisely the repertoire of simulated inlay used on the apse ribs and soffit — and a related band of lozenges and triangles closes the composition at the base. A vertical border with a meander in green survives at the left edge, marking the boundary with the adjoining field.

Within this frame the picture is set against a deep blue ground, now much darkened by the deterioration of the azurite, against which the whole apparatus of the throne is silhouetted. The throne is conceived not as a seat but as a full architectural tabernacle: two slender marble colonnettes with foliate capitals and moulded bases carry a segmental gabled arch, cusped at the intrados, whose underside is turned toward the viewer so that the structure is understood as seen from slightly below. Behind the Virgin's shoulders the back panel is filled with a dense diaper of black and gold triangular motifs, simulating either a figured textile or inlay work. The seat itself is a marble bench with a polychrome geometric intarsia along its edge, and a red patterned cushion is visible at the left, its foreshortening handled with some confidence.

Two winged figures stand on the entablature above the flanking piers, painted in pale ochre and white monochrome so as to read as carved stone statues surmounting the tabernacle. The device — feigned sculpture incorporated into a painted architecture — is a distinctly Giottesque conceit, and its deployment here indicates a painter with first-hand knowledge of the Assisi workshops rather than a provincial working from hearsay. A letter, apparently an **S**, is visible on the die of the right-hand pier and may belong to a longer inscription worth examining at the wall.

The Virgin is seated frontally, her head turned very slightly and her gaze directed outward past the viewer. The face is broad and long-jawed, built on firm contours, with heavy-lidded eyes, a straight nose and a small mouth, and modelled with a warm flush at the cheek. A cream veil edged with gold falls from the crown over both shoulders; beneath it she wears a red-brown gown with a gilt neckband, and over it a deep blue mantle that descends across the body and pools at the lower edge of the composition, where a red lining shows through. The halo is of applied gold leaf, incised with dense radiating striations, and is the only genuinely gilded passage in the painting — laid *a secco* with a mordant over the finished fresco surface.

The Child sits on the Virgin's left knee, his body turned toward her and his head tilted upward so that his gaze meets her face rather than the beholder's. He wears a long tunic of gold brocade patterned in a lozenge repeat, with bare feet and legs; his hair is a mass of reddish-blond curls and his halo, also gilded, is marked with the cross. The Virgin's hands close around his wrist and his side. This upward-turning, mutually absorbed exchange between mother and child is the affective heart of the picture and belongs unmistakably to the post-Giottesque generation, when the hieratic frontality of the Duecento *Maestà* was giving way to a humanised maternal relation.

The lower third has suffered: heavy abrasion and whitish calcareous deposit have consumed much of the drapery below the knees, and the pavement on which the throne stands is now only faintly legible.

### Madonna and Child

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-San-Ponziano/image 6.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, 1325–30, detached fresco (halos originally gilded, gilding lost), from the church of the Trinità, Spoleto; Museo Nazionale del Ducato di Spoleto, Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fragment preserves a half-length devotional group within an irregular field whose upper contour rises to a broad, slightly asymmetrical arch — the surviving outline of the original painted compartment rather than a cut imposed at the moment of detachment. It is now mounted on a neutral modern support, visible as a pale speckled surround, and the losses characteristic of a detached wall painting are everywhere apparent: hundreds of small white lacunae scattered across the surface, densest in the lower half, where the ground has parted from the paint film in a fine, granular pattern.

The Virgin is shown to just below the waist, her head inclined toward the Child at her left. The face is a long, narrow oval built on firm, economical contours: an arched brow, a straight nose of considerable length, large dark eyes set slightly asymmetrically and glancing away from the axis of the head, and a small mouth of deep red set in a faint, closed smile. The modelling proceeds from a greenish underpaint through warm flesh tones to concentrated pink at the cheek, a technique wholly conventional in Umbrian fresco of the period and here handled with restraint. A long veil of cream-white, edged along its fall, covers the head and descends over both shoulders.

The halo is not gilded but survives as a broad pale disc, incised with dense radiating striations and bordered by a band of pseudo-epigraphic ornament in relief. What now reads as bone-coloured plaster is almost certainly the exposed preparation of a lost gold layer, applied *a secco* over the finished fresco and long since detached — a very common pattern of loss, and one worth stating explicitly in any caption, since the present tonality misrepresents the original effect.

The mantle is the most striking passage. A deep blue-black cloak, its blue much altered by the deterioration of azurite, is strewn across its whole surface with eight-pointed stars in white, varying in scale and irregularly disposed. This is a decorative amplification of the Byzantine *maphorion*, on which three stars — at the brow and both shoulders — signify the Virgin's threefold virginity; here the motif has been multiplied into an all-over pattern, generating the celestial reading that gives images of this class their vernacular name, *Madonna delle Stelle*. Beneath the mantle a red gown appears at the throat.

The Child, at the right, is held upright against his mother's body and turns his head sharply upward to meet her gaze. His right hand reaches toward her chin in the gesture of the *Eleousa*, the Virgin of Tenderness, which by the fourteenth century had been thoroughly naturalised in central Italian painting as a vehicle for maternal intimacy. He wears a tunic of gold brocade, patterned with a fine repeating ornament and drawn tight across the arm, beneath a rose-pink mantle that falls in broad, softly modelled folds. His halo, likewise stripped of its gilding, carries incised rays and the cross.

The ground behind the figures is a warm brick red, worked with a large-scale incised or painted pattern suggesting a brocaded hanging; a horizontal red band and a darker zone survive at the upper right, remnants of a framing element or of the architecture within which the image was originally set. Along the bottom runs a band of overlapping green leaves — a painted garland or hedge which closes the composition and, in the devotional logic of such images, situates the group in a paradisal enclosure.

## The Maestro di San Ponziano as sculptor: the Madonna di Belfiore and the market

### Madonna di Belfiore (attributed)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-di-San-Ponziano/image 7.jpg" alt="Madonna di Belfiore">
  <figcaption>Madonna di Belfiore, c. 1310, carved and painted wood, from the parish church of San Niccolò at Belfiore di Foligno; Museo Nazionale del Ducato di Spoleto.</figcaption>
</figure>

The proposal that the master was also a carver of statues — not merely the polychromist of others' carvings but the intagliatore himself — is the boldest and most consequential claim of the recent literature, and it deserves careful statement.

The group is carved almost fully in the round at the front and set against a flat rectangular backboard whose surface is painted rather than modelled — and this disjunction between three-dimensional figure and two-dimensional ground is the first thing that should be said about the object, because it is not an aesthetic choice but the residue of a lost structure. The backboard is the rear wall of the tabernacle housing; the painted bands that flank the throne at the base, carrying a cosmatesco repeat of lozenges and chequered squares in red, black and white, are the interior faces of its side walls. What is now displayed as a free-standing sculpture was conceived as the carved centre of a painted box, visible only when the shutters were opened.

The painted ground behind the Virgin's shoulders and head consists of a dense lattice of interlocking lozenges in pale green and orange-red, each compartment enclosing a small quatrefoil rosette — a simulated figured textile of the kind commonly used as a cloth of honour, here executed in tempera directly on the wood. Along the upper edge a fringed valance is indicated, complete with pendent tassels, so that the whole is understood as a hanging suspended behind the throne. The throne's arms are visible at either side as pale members with inlaid decoration.

The Virgin sits erect and rigorously frontal. She wears a low open crown, its lobed elements picked out in red and gold, set directly over a veil that falls in a broad plane down both sides of the head and onto the shoulders. The face is a wide oval built on large, emphatic forms: strongly arched dark brows, very large eyes with heavily drawn upper lids and a fixed outward gaze, a long straight nose, a small mouth painted deep red, and cheeks flushed with a concentrated pink. The effect is one of solemn, almost startled presence rather than of interior life — the carver is working within a hieratic conception in which the image confronts the worshipper rather than communing with the Child.

Her gown shows at the breast in red beneath a decorative yoke of banded embroidery in white and dark blue, and a red sleeve with a cuff appears at the right wrist. Over this falls a mantle whose surface, now a warm ochre-brown, is covered by a fine, close craquelure across its entire extent: this is the exposed bolo and gesso preparation of a lost gold leaf, and the original effect would have been of a figure enveloped in burnished gold. The drapery of the lap and legs is handled in broad, simple, almost tubular volumes, with a small number of deep vertical channels and a single strong diagonal fold across the knees; there is no attempt at the calligraphic complication that a painter would bring to the same passage. At the base the feet emerge, shod in red.

The right hand is raised at chest height and holds a small object, now damaged, which on the surviving contour is most plausibly a flower or fruit — a stem is discernible below the fingers. The left hand passes beneath the Child and supports him at the hip.

The Child sits on the Virgin's left knee, likewise frontal, his head turned very slightly. His skull is close-shaven or bald, an archaising trait, and the face is round and full, with wide eyes and a small mouth. He wears a long tunic of pale silvery blue and holds against his body a small rectangular object with a red cover, certainly a book. The disparity of scale between the two figures is considerable, and the Child is proportioned as a small adult rather than an infant.

A note about dimensions: it is described in the church inventory drawn up by Angelo Bolognini in 1727, placed in the last altar on the left, as **Immagine di rilievo, detta Madonna del Rosario, la qual si porta in processione dal priore ogni prima domenica del mese, post vesperos, di vari colori assai galante e di altezza palmi 3 e mezzo circa** (A relief image, called the Madonna of the Rosary, which is carried in procession by the prior every first Sunday of the month, after vespers, of various very gallant colours and approximately three and a half palms high.)

The condition is that of a much-handled cult object: gilding almost entirely lost, extensive abrasion at the projecting surfaces, damage to the Virgin's nose and to the fingers of both hands, and the paint of the backboard worn thin toward the right.

The proposal has, moreover, migrated into the art market, which is itself a datum worth recording with appropriate caution. At the 2023 Assisi antiques fair (*Mostra Arte e Antiquariato*, Bastia Umbra) a dealer (Alice Fine Art) exhibited a *Madonna and Child* in carved, gilt, silvered and painted wood, about 118 cm high, dated to the first decades of the fourteenth century and given an Umbrian origin, under the name "Maestro di San Ponziano." Such a market attribution carries none of the authority of a museum catalogue or a peer-reviewed study, and it should be treated as a commercial ascription reflecting — and trading upon — the corpus-expansion proposed by Delpriori; but it is symptomatic of how quickly the reconstructed sculptural personality of the master has become a working category, and it belongs in any honest census of works circulating under his name, clearly labelled as a trade attribution.

The dating of the sculptural works (the Belfiore Madonna at c. 1310) somewhat earlier than the crypt frescoes and the Trinità Madonna (c. 1325–1330) is worth noting, though it should not be over-interpreted: if all these ascriptions hold, they would imply an activity spanning roughly the first three decades of the century, with the sculpture standing at the earlier end. But the chronology of the sculptural attributions is softer than that of the frescoes, and I flag it as such.

#### The devotional rationale for a painter who carves

It is worth pausing on *why* the identity of painter and sculptor should have been possible — even natural — in the Spoleto of the Maestro di San Ponziano, since this is the interpretive foundation on which his reconstructed double activity rests. De Marchi and Delpriori adduce a striking textual justification. In a passage of the *Compilatio Assisiensis*, Saint Francis, explaining to the bishop of Terni how images should be regarded in prayer, is reported to have treated painted panels and polychrome wooden sculptures as equivalent — *sicut in pictura Domini et beate Virginis in ligno depicta honoratur Deus et beata Virgo* — the terms *pictura* and *ligno depicta* denoting, respectively, a painted panel and a polychrome wooden statue, held to be devotionally interchangeable. If, for the devout, a painted Virgin and a carved one were the same kind of image with the same function, then the artist who supplied both need not be two persons; and in a region where the carving was, in Previtali's phrase, incomplete until the colour defined its very forms, the logic pressed toward a single directing hand.

To this Delpriori adds a suggestive body of evidence drawn from the hagiography of the Beata Chiara da Montefalco — a central figure of Spoletan spirituality at the very moment of the master's activity — as reported by her biographer Berengario di Donadio da Saint-Affrique<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-berengario-di-donadio-da-saint-affrique" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>: her reluctance to pray before painted images ("I have the Cross of Christ in my heart"), set beside the report that in ecstasy she remained rigid "like a wooden statue." The wooden simulacrum, on this reading, could function almost as a substitute body, a quasi-relic, exempt from any devotional reservation about images — which helps to explain both the fortune of polychrome wooden sculpture in this milieu and the peculiar intensity of a work like the recumbent, headless *gisant* of Saint Ponziano, a carved body standing in for the martyr's own.

The argument is strengthened by the fact that the devotional object in question was not an autonomous image but an instrument of ritualized attention. In a nunnery crypt, where the eye moved from painted narrative to carved body within a narrow, enclosed space, the distinction between panel, wall and wood was less important than the cumulative effect of presence, gesture and proximity. A painted image and a polychrome wooden figure could thus answer the same devotional need by different means.

That equivalence also aligns with the material conditions of the region. The Spoletan tradition did not habitually separate carving from colour in the way that later, more academic orthodoxies would have preferred; the carved surface was often conceived as incomplete until it received its painted skin, and the painted image could be made to function as though it were sculptural. In such a context, the same hand could plausibly move from one medium to the other without violating the expectations of patrons or worshippers.

The crypt of San Ponziano makes this logic visible. The recumbent saint, placed beneath the painted Crucifixion, does not simply echo the scene above it; it turns the chapel into a theatre of mediation in which martyrdom, burial and salvation are joined in a single spatial argument. The viewer's experience is therefore not that of looking at isolated objects but of moving through a devotional apparatus whose parts were designed to reinforce one another.

Seen in this light, the master's supposed double practice is less a curiosity of connoisseurship than a reflection of the cultural economy of sacred image-making in early Trecento Umbria. It speaks to a world in which images were made to be touched by belief, to be read at close range, and to serve the needs of a local cult whose force depended on the material immediacy of relic, body and representation. The painter who carved, then, is not an anomaly but an index of the social and devotional logic of the place.

The importance of this point is heightened by the fact that the saint himself was a martyr whose body and head were both objects of veneration. In such a setting, the distinction between a devotional image and a surrogate relic was never absolute. A painted Christ on the wall, a carved saint on the ground, and the preserved skull of the martyr in the monastery's custody could all participate in a single economy of presence, each intensifying the others rather than competing with them.

It also helps explain why the master's art is so often most persuasive in the very places where the evidence is most fragmentary. The crypt, the altar, the tabernacle and the processional image all belonged to a habitual environment in which visual form, ritual use and local devotion were tightly interwoven. To recover the artist's intentions, one must therefore think not only in terms of style but in terms of placement, movement and the choreography of looking.

For that reason, the reconstruction of the Maestro di San Ponziano cannot be reduced to a list of works or a sequence of attributions. It depends upon an understanding of how sacred images were staged within a lived religious world, where the body of the saint, the painted narrative and the viewer's own movement through space formed one coherent devotional experience. The master, on this reading, was not simply a painter or a carver but a maker of environments.

#### Why so little survives: the dispersion of the Spoletan patrimony

The thinness of the master's corpus is not an accident of his own limited productivity but a symptom of a general catastrophe of survival, and it conditions everything we can say about him. Bruno Toscano's insistence that we work always on a minimal fraction of what once existed is nowhere more apposite than here. The nunnery of San Ponziano suffered the Napoleonic suppression of 1810 and the demanial confiscations that followed Italian unification (1860), in the course of which numerous works of art and sacred furnishings belonging to the monastery were alienated; the monumental winged tabernacles that were the school's characteristic altarpieces were, as De Marchi laments, almost always dismembered and destroyed, drastically diminishing our perception of a strongly characterized type of which Spoleto was a principal centre of production.

That the master's wooden statue and the Trinità Madonna survive at all — the former reintegrated into the Museo Nazionale del Ducato, the latter detached from its church and likewise museumized, the Belfiore Madonna recovered by the State only in 2006 — is testimony less to the completeness of the record than to the vagaries of loss and recovery. The enclosure of the crypt after 1521, which walled it away from the laity, is paradoxically the reason its eponymous frescoes were preserved. Any biography of the Maestro di San Ponziano is, in this sense, written in the negative space of an overwhelmingly dispersed patrimony.

The consequences of this dispersion are not merely archival. They alter the very terms in which the artist can be known. When a work has been detached from its original setting, dismembered, transferred, or simply lost, the evidence available to the modern scholar is no longer a stable object but a fragment of a former system of meaning. The surviving pieces therefore do not speak in isolation; they only become legible when restored to the networks of use, devotion and display that once gave them coherence.

This is especially true of the Spoletan production of the Trecento, where the most characteristic forms — tabernacles, crosses, reliquary structures and processional images — were designed to function as part of a larger devotional apparatus rather than as self-contained masterpieces. Once that apparatus was broken apart, the modern observer is left with scattered remains that can easily be mistaken for a random assortment of images rather than the remains of a once-integrated sacred environment.

For the Maestro di San Ponziano, then, the problem of survival is inseparable from the problem of interpretation. To speak of his work is always to reconstruct, in some measure, the world from which it emerged. And because so much of that world has vanished, the artist's biography becomes a history of absence as much as of presence, a record of what has been preserved, what has been displaced, and what can still be inferred from the traces that remain.

## Style and artistic personality

What, then, can be said of the *manner* of the Maestro di San Ponziano — of the qualities that allow a hand to be recognized across fresco, panel and wood?

The characterizations available in the literature, while brief, are consistent. The eponymous frescoes are praised for the *secure structure of the figures* and the *studied architecture of the throne* — that is, for a firm, constructive sense of figural mass and of built space, an evident inheritance from the Giottesque revolution absorbed at Assisi. At the same time the master belongs unmistakably to the expressive, "left-bank" Spoletan current: his origins in the ambit of the Espressionista di Santa Chiara (Palmerino di Guido) place him within the heightened emotional register — the mimicry oscillating between grief and tenderness — that De Marchi identifies as the constant of the school. The *icastic essentiality* of the painted figuration, marked by strong profiling contours, which De Marchi describes as a general feature of the Spoletan idiom and as a corollary of the joint painting-carving practice, is the formal signature we should expect to find in his work: figures defined by firm outline, forms conceived so as to read powerfully at devotional close range and in the raking light of a crypt.

The iconographic dimension is equally telling. The master's most characteristic surviving invention — the Crucifixion of the crypt with its Y-shaped Cross, its downward-reaching Virgin, and its *animula* rising to heaven — displays exactly the "iconographic autonomy" that De Marchi and Delpriori identify as a hallmark of the Spoletan school, its refusal to submit to the most common iconographic rules, its readiness to bend the standard schemes to a particular site, function and audience. The Y-cross calibrated to a single viewing position, the integration of painted grief with a carved recumbent body, the fusion of martyr's *animula* and Christ's sacrifice into a single visual argument: these are the marks not of a provincial follower mechanically repeating models, but of an inventive intelligence working with real command of the expressive and spatial means of his tradition.

It is precisely this quality that earns the master the epithet, in the local literature, of a painter "of uncommon quality" (*di qualità non comune*), and that justifies the space De Marchi and Delpriori give him within the school. He is a minor master by the measure of surviving quantity and of documentary presence; he is not a minor master by the measure of pictorial intelligence.

## Relations to contemporaries: the Maestro di Cesi, the Maestro di Fossa, and the problem of confusion

No anonymous master exists in isolation, and the Maestro di San Ponziano is best located by triangulation against the other reconstructed personalities of the Spoletan Trecento.

The most important — and most delicate — relationship is with the Maestro di Cesi, the first of the Spoletan Giotteschi, author of the great *Coronation of the Virgin* triptych of 1308 (now in the Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris) painted, in pendant with a *Christus triumphans* Cross (now in the Museo del Ducato), for the monastery of Santa Maria della Stella at Spoleto, and of the mural decoration of the chapel of the Cross in Santa Chiara at Montefalco (commissioned in 1333). The literature is explicit that the Maestro di San Ponziano is a *contemporary* of the Maestro di Cesi, and that he is "confused" with him owing to their great stylistic proximity (*con lui si confonde per la grande vicinanza stilistica*), as the presentation of the 2018 exhibition put it. This closeness is both an opportunity and a danger for the critic.

It is an opportunity because it fixes the master's chronological and cultural position with some precision: he moves in the same orbit, absorbs the same Assisan and Giottesque lessons, works to the same devotional ends. It is a danger because it means that individual attributions oscillating between the two hands must be argued with care, and that the boundary of the San Ponziano corpus is, at its edges, genuinely uncertain. The prudent position is the one implied by Delpriori's decision to give the master a chapter of his own while placing it immediately after the Maestro di Cesi: the two are distinct personalities within a shared idiom, and the distinction rests on the specific nucleus of works — above all the integrated crypt ensemble — that can be gathered around the San Ponziano hand.

With the Maestro di Fossa — for De Marchi and Delpriori the greatest of the Spoletan Trecento artists, a painter who moves in the wake of the Giotto of the Lower Basilica but is grafted with the grace of Simone Martini and works in parallel with Puccio Capanna, and whose influence endures into the Quattrocento — the relationship is one of shared school rather than of confusion. The Maestro di Fossa and his associated carver, the *Maestro della Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto*, form the mature culmination of the painter-sculptor practice that the Maestro di San Ponziano exemplifies at an earlier, less monumental stage. Likewise the Maestro della Croce di Trevi (named from the great shaped Cross at Trevi of c. 1317, and formerly known also as the Master of the Cini diptych) and his associated carver the *Maestro della Croce di Visso* stand as parallel cases of the same coordinated painting-carving enterprise. The Maestro di San Ponziano is thus one node in a network of painter-sculptor pairings (or painter-sculptor identities) that De Marchi enumerates: the Maestro di Cesi working always with the sculptor of the Santa Cristina di Caso; the Maestro della Croce di Trevi with the Maestro della Croce di Visso; the Maestro di Fossa with the Maestro della Madonna del Duomo di Spoleto — and the Maestro di San Ponziano, on the recent proposal, uniting the two roles in his own person.

Behind all of them stands the great matrix of the Duecento tradition — Alberto Sotio (or, more prudently, the Master of the Cross of the Duomo of Spoleto), Petrus, Simeone and Machilone, the Maestro di San Felice di Giano, Rinaldetto di Ranuccio — and, ahead of them, the long shadow of the school reaching to Bartolo di Spoleto (the so-called Maestro dei Calvari) and into the Quattrocento with the Maestro di Eggi (a painter admired, De Marchi notes, by [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico)) and Giovanni di Corraduccio.

## Chronology and dating

The chronology of the master, in the present state of knowledge, may be summarized as follows, always with the caveat that no date is documentary and all rest on style and on museum cataloguing.

- The **Madonna di Belfiore** (polychrome wood, Museo del Ducato) is dated by the museum to *c. 1310*. If the attribution holds, this is the earliest point of the reconstructed activity.
- The **crypt frescoes** of San Ponziano (the eponymous Crucifixion with the *animula* and the Magdalene-and-Mary fragments) and the **wooden statue of Saint Ponziano** are placed in the *first third of the fourteenth century*, with the statue dated by the museum to *c. 1325–1330*.
- The detached **Madonna and Child** from the church of the Trinità (Museo del Ducato) is dated to *c. 1325–1330*.

The overall span implied is therefore roughly **c. 1310 to c. 1330**, i.e. the first three decades of the Trecento, contemporary with the Maestro di Cesi (documented 1308, active into the 1330s). This is consistent with the master's formation in relation to Palmerino di Guido / the Espressionista di Santa Chiara, a current active around and after 1300, and with the general pattern of the Spoletan Giotteschi absorbing the Assisan lessons of c. 1290–1320.

I note one point of possible tension for future study: the sculptural attributions (Belfiore, c. 1310) sit a little earlier than the securely eponymous frescoes (c. 1325–1330). This is not in itself problematic for a career spanning three decades, but the sculptural end of the corpus is more lightly argued than the pictorial end, and the internal chronology of the carved works should be regarded as provisional.

## X. Open questions and directions for research

A rigorous biography must end by naming what remains unresolved. For the Maestro di San Ponziano the principal open questions are these.

1. **The boundary with the Maestro di Cesi.** Given the acknowledged confusability of the two hands, the precise extent of the San Ponziano corpus at its margins is uncertain. Which panels or fresco fragments now floating between the two names belong to which artist is a question that only close comparative analysis — ideally with the works assembled, as they partly were in 2018 — can settle. Any expansion of the corpus by stylistic analogy should be argued against the Maestro di Cesi as the nearest alternative.

2. **The reality and extent of the sculptural activity.** The proposal that the master carved as well as painted rests on the coherence of the Spoletan painter-sculptor thesis and on specific attributions (above all the Belfiore Madonna). It is persuasive but not documented, and — as Dario Mor and others have cautioned in the broader debate on Umbrian and Abruzzese wooden sculpture — the mere fact that painter and polychromy are attested for a given workshop does not by itself prove that the same person wielded the chisel. The attribution of specific carvings to the master should be held open.

3. **The status of the local, devotional attributions.** As shown above, guides to the abbey attach the master's name to works that cannot be his (the enthroned Madonna dated 1478) and to works that may be his on grounds of tradition rather than analysis (the refectory Crucifix). These need to be sifted against the scholarly corpus; the discrepancies in the popular literature are a caution against building any argument on the touristic sources alone.

4. **The original disposition of the crypt ensemble.** Delpriori's reconstruction of the integration of the *gisant* statue, the false catafalque and the apse Crucifixion is the most valuable single contribution to the master's study, but it remains a reconstruction, dependent on the reading of wall-holes, sight-lines and the anomalous Y-cross. Further technical investigation of the crypt — of the plaster stratigraphy, the fixing-points, the original access — could confirm or refine it.

5. **The identification of Palmerino di Guido.** The master's origins are tied to the "Espressionista di Santa Chiara," itself only *probably* to be identified with Palmerino di Guido. The security of the San Ponziano master's formation is therefore only as strong as that underlying identification, which belongs to the wider and still-debated problem of the Assisan expressionist current around Giotto.

None of these uncertainties is a reason to doubt the reality of the artistic personality. They are, rather, the normal condition of an anonymous master reconstructed from a fragmentary and dispersed patrimony — the condition that Toscano taught us to expect when we work, always, on a minimal fraction of what once existed.

## Conclusion

The Maestro di San Ponziano remains an elusive figure, but he is no longer an imaginary one. He emerges from this study not as a documented historical personality, still less as a fully recoverable biographical subject, but as a vivid critical construct anchored in a real cluster of works, sites and devotional practices. What can be said with confidence is that the name designates a distinctive artistic hand, active in the first decades of the Trecento and shaped by the peculiar conditions of Spoleto, its local traditions and its intense engagement with the lessons of Assisi and Giotto.

His importance lies less in the quantity of what survives than in the quality of the insight that survives with it. The crypt frescoes of San Ponziano, the recumbent statue of Saint Ponziano and the related devotional apparatus together suggest an artist of uncommon intelligence, capable of thinking across media and of building an image-world that was at once pictorial, sculptural and liturgical. The very fact that his works are dispersed, damaged and often only indirectly legible makes his achievement all the more remarkable: he appears not as a master of easy visibility, but as one whose force is most clearly felt in the complexity of the environments he helped to create.

The argument for a painter-sculptor, or at least for a workshop in which painting and carving were inseparably joined, is therefore more than a technical hypothesis. It is a way of understanding how sacred images functioned in this part of Umbria, where the boundaries between panel, wall, wood and relic were not rigidly maintained. In that setting, the Maestro di San Ponziano becomes a figure of cultural mediation: a maker of images whose work belongs to the devotional life of a community as much as to the history of style.

At the same time, the case also demonstrates the limits of attribution. The history of the master's corpus is full of contamination, confusion and contamination by later tradition. Some works are securely associated with the hand, others are plausible but uncertain, and still others have been attached to his name by local devotion or by the modern art market rather than by rigorous analysis. This is not a weakness in the reconstruction so much as a reminder that the study of anonymous masters is necessarily a study of evidence, method and caution.

What is most persuasive, then, is not the completeness of the picture but its coherence. The Maestro di San Ponziano may never be recovered as a fully documented historical individual, yet the ensemble of works and ideas gathered around him forms a meaningful artistic personality. He belongs to a Spoletan world that was neither provincial nor derivative, but intensely local, highly inventive and deeply engaged with the devotional demands of its own setting.

His art is marked by a striking balance between expressive intensity and formal discipline, between emotional directness and architectural control. The Y-shaped Crucifixion, the downward-reaching Virgin, the recumbent martyr and the integrated crypt program all point to an artist who could transform inherited forms into something unmistakably his own. The result is not merely a local variant of Giotto or an echo of Assisi, but a specific and compelling artistic voice within a wider Umbrian and Italian context.

The study of the Maestro di San Ponziano therefore matters for more than one name in the history of art. It matters because it shows how artistic individuality can be reconstructed from fragments, how place and devotion shape style, and how the history of a region can be written through the surviving traces of a single hand. Even if the full biography remains incomplete, the figure of the master continues to illuminate the cultural life of medieval Spoleto with unusual clarity.

In the end, the Maestro di San Ponziano is best understood as a threshold figure: a witness to the ways in which medieval art was made, displayed and experienced in a world where image, ritual and relic were inseparable. His history is therefore not simply the history of an anonymous artist, but a history of how a community remembered, honoured and visually staged its sacred past.

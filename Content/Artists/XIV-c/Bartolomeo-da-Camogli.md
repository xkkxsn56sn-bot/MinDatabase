## Introduction: a name, a panel and a problem

Few fourteenth-century painters are known so precisely and yet so thinly as *Bartolomeo Pellerano da Camogli*. His name survives on one painted surface and in a few Genoese notarial acts. Despite that, he has a place in the history of European devotional imagery that much better-documented painters lack. The reason is a single panel in Palermo. It shows the Virgin seated on a cushion on the ground and nursing the Child, and an inscription on it names the subject as Our Lady of Humility and gives the year 1346. Since that inscription became known to international scholarship, the panel has been the fixed point from which the history of the Madonna of Humility is written. It is the earliest securely dated surviving example of the type. It is also the earliest painting in which the title, *de humilitate*, is attached to the image by the image itself. It is not, however, the earliest image of the type, and no published document places the painter in Palermo at any date.

Several layers of knowledge have built up around this painter and his panel, and they need to be kept apart. Some of what is said about Bartolomeo is documented in the strict sense: notarial instruments published from the Genoese archives in the nineteenth century, and the inscription on the panel. Some is securely inferred from those documents, such as his membership of the Pellerano family of Camogli and his death by 1348. Much more is hypothesis, ranging from his training under a putative father to the route by which Avignonese models reached him. A further layer is plain error, repeated in reference works and sometimes given the authority of a museum label or an encyclopaedia entry.

The modern study of Bartolomeo rests on three bodies of work. The first is the iconographic investigation by <a href="/scholars.html#meiss-millard">Millard Meiss</a>, whose article "The Madonna of Humility" appeared in *The Art Bulletin* in 1936 and was reprinted in his *Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death*. It opens with the Palermo panel. The second is the monograph and later encyclopaedic entries of Anna De Floriani, beginning with her *Bartolomeo da Camogli* (Genoa, 1979). Together they remain the fullest synthesis of the documents, the critical fortunes of the work and the Ligurian context. The third is the work of <a href="/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando">Ferdinando Bologna</a> on the Angevin and Mediterranean dimensions of Trecento painting. In studies from 1955 to 1994 he did most to move the problem of the prototype from Naples or Siena to Avignon. He also proposed the hypothesis about Bartolomeo's family and training that later Genoese scholarship has had to engage with.

## The documentary Bartolomeo: Genoa, 1339–1348

Everything securely known of Bartolomeo's life comes from Genoese notarial acts. A Genoese antiquarian collection of 1870, *Notizie dei professori del disegno in Liguria dalle origini al secolo XVI*, first gathered and published them. Two Treccani entries have summarised them since: De Floriani's in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* (1998) and a later entry in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (2015). The documented chronology is short, and it is worth setting out in full, because almost every loose claim about the painter's biography comes from blurring it.

The earliest act is dated 2 February 1339. On that day Bartolomeo took on an apprentice, Simonino Smeraldo da Rapallo. According to the 1998 encyclopaedia entry, the deed was drawn up in his own workshop, located near the palace of the *Abate del popolo* in Genoa. The document matters for several reasons. It shows that by early 1339 Bartolomeo was an independent master with his own shop, able to take on and train a *garzone*. That implies he was already an adult with a settled professional status. Hence the usual statement that he was probably born in the early years of the fourteenth century, which is a reasonable inference and not a recorded fact. The deed also places the workshop in the political centre of the city, near the seat of the popular magistracy, which says something about his standing and clientele. Finally, the apprentice came from Rapallo, on the same stretch of the Riviera di Levante as Camogli. Recruitment of this kind was common in the Genoese workshop economy, and it fits the regional connections that the painter's toponymic suggests.

The second act is dated 30 March 1346. On that day Bartolomeo was commissioned to paint an *ancona*, or altarpiece, for the Genoese church of San Siro. The 2015 biographical entry names the commissioner as Raffo di Tommaso. The work is lost. The document deserves emphasis because it falls in the same year as the Palermo panel. In 1346, then, Bartolomeo was working in Genoa and taking on a substantial ecclesiastical commission there. This makes any claim that he was "active in Palermo" in that year at least questionable. It is entirely possible that the Palermo panel was painted in Genoa and shipped south, as so many panels were in the Tyrrhenian trade. Nothing in the documents excludes a journey to Sicily either. But the only documented place of work is Genoa.

The last group of documents concerns his death. The two encyclopaedia entries describe the evidence slightly differently. The 1998 entry says that Bartolomeo died by October 1348, when the painter Giovanni Re da Rapallo succeeded him in the lease of a house on the piazza del Comune. The 2015 entry is more precise. It cites two documents of 1349 in which Bartolomeo's minor children, acting through a guardian, leased his workshop to Giovanni Re da Rapallo, who had been paying rent since November 1348. Both accounts agree on the essential point: Bartolomeo was dead by late 1348. Whether he died of the Black Death, which reached Genoa in that year, is a natural and frequent speculation. But no document gives a cause of death. The plague should therefore be mentioned only as a context, never as a fact. A further document, dated 18 August 1358, names the artist as long dead. The 2015 entry treats it as the document confirming that he belonged to the Pellerano family.

The death date of "1358" found in some authority records comes from a misreading of this posthumous mention as a death record, and has no basis. Equally baseless is the birthplace "Genoa" in some authority files. The documents call him *de Camulio*, "of Camogli", and the encyclopaedia entries call him *originario di Camogli*. Birth in Genoa itself is not documented. Camogli is a toponymic of origin, and it does not prove where he was born either, but it is the only geographical indication that the sources give.

## The Camogli connection and the Pellerano family

The name *de Camulio* links Bartolomeo to the fishing and seafaring town of Camogli on the eastern Ligurian coast, near Recco and the Portofino promontory. Medieval Genoese usage often attached such toponymics to families who had moved to the city from the Riviera, and they could persist for generations. The 1358 document links the toponymic to the surname Pellerano. That is why the modern literature uses the double form "Bartolomeo Pellerano da Camogli", while older and foreign literature often prefers the Latinised "Bartolomeo Camulio" or "Bartolommeo da Camogli". The forms are equivalent. They do not designate different individuals.

Two other painters of the same family appear in the Genoese documents. Opizzino Pellerano da Camogli is documented in Genoa from 1302 to 1325 and was dead before 1361. Following the 1870 publication, part of the literature has proposed that he was Bartolomeo's father. The 2015 entry presents this as an opinion held by "some", and the 1998 entry says only that Bartolomeo was "probably" his son. Antonio Pellerano, a painter documented in 1341, has been proposed as Bartolomeo's brother, again with a "probably". These kinships are reasonable hypotheses built on a shared name and trade. No surviving document actually states either relationship. Any account that calls Opizzino simply "his father" has turned a hypothesis into a fact.

The hypothesis becomes art-historically important because of a further proposal by Ferdinando Bologna in 1994: that Opizzino should be identified with the anonymous painter known as the Master of Santa Maria di Castello, also called the Master of the Croce della Consolazione. Bologna described this master's formation as developed in contact with Sienese experiences and above all with pre-Giottesque, Cimabuesque Assisi. That culture was represented in Genoa in 1292–93 by the Pistoian painter Manfredino d'Alberto. If the identification holds, Bartolomeo would have grown up in a workshop already oriented towards central Italy, and his Sienese and Martinian leanings would have a domestic root. Later Genoese scholarship, including a 2011 survey of Ligurian medieval painting, has taken the Master of Santa Maria di Castello as an important point of reference for Bartolomeo's style. The 2015 entry describes the identification with Opizzino with a cautious "forse". The chain therefore runs as follows. Opizzino is probably Bartolomeo's father. Opizzino may be the Master of Santa Maria di Castello. The Master of Santa Maria di Castello is stylistically relevant to Bartolomeo. Only the third link rests on visual analysis of surviving works. The first two rest on reasonable but unproven inference.

## The "1339 Palermo document" that is not there

One persistent claim about Bartolomeo is that a Palermo document of 1339 attests his presence in Sicily. It is sometimes elaborated into a commission for a processional banner, or *gonfalone*, for the Compagnia dei Disciplinati<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-disciplinati" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> di San Nicolò lo Reale. If it were true, it would change the picture considerably. A 1339 Palermo commission would make Bartolomeo a painter who worked in Sicily, or at least for Sicilian patrons, years before 1346. It would also tie the later panel to a patron relationship already documented.

Neither of the authoritative modern syntheses of Bartolomeo's documents mentions any such document. Both the 1998 encyclopaedia entry and the 2015 biographical dictionary entry list the Genoese acts summarised above and nothing else. The 2015 entry adds that the Palermo panel was recorded only from the seventeenth century, in the cloister of San Francesco. Neither the scholarship on the confraternity of San Nicolò lo Reale nor the work on its statutes and its 1388 panel by Antonio Veneziano, records a banner by Bartolomeo. The date 1339 does belong to Bartolomeo, but in Genoa. It is the date of the apprenticeship contract of 2 February 1339, and it explains the formula "active from 1339" found in reference works. It is likely that an unclear phrase such as "documented from 1339", placed next to the Palermo panel and its confraternal predella, has been turned into "documented in Palermo in 1339".

A second possible source of confusion lies in Palermitan institutional history. A Palermo foundation deed dated 12 November 1339 does exist. Recent work on Palermitan welfare institutions identifies it as the act establishing the hospital and oratory of Santa Maria La Nuova, and it survives in a fifteenth-century transcript. This document has nothing to do with Bartolomeo or with San Nicolò lo Reale. But a Palermitan document dated 1339 and connected with lay devotional foundations could easily have drifted into accounts of a painter whose career also "begins" in 1339. On present evidence the "1339 Palermo document" for Bartolomeo da Camogli is unverified, and very probably a conflation.

The confraternity itself, on the other hand, is well documented. It belongs to the discussion of the panel's possible destination, below.

## The Palermo panel: a description

Meiss opened his 1936 article with a description of the panel that remains accurate. The Virgin sits on a small cushion placed on the ground. She is turned obliquely to the picture plane, her head inclined gently to the left, her further leg raised higher than the nearer one. She holds the Child close to her body. The Child draws her breast into his mouth while turning his head away to look out at the viewer. The combination of the suckling Child with an outward gaze is the panel's principal psychological device. It turns an intimate scene of nursing into a direct address to the beholder. The image sits within a painted architectural setting with a trilobed arch. Writers on the panel's decoration have noted that its spandrels contain the Annunciation, with the angel on one side and the Virgin Annunciate on the other. A frieze of fictive cosmatesque inlay runs around three sides of the picture field and fills the spandrels. An essay on painted cosmatesque ornament recalled that a 1953 reading of the panel had already praised this decoration for an elegance worthy of Simone Martini's culture.

The presence of the Annunciation matters iconographically. It links the seated, nursing Virgin to the moment of the Incarnation, when, in the medieval exegesis of Luke, Mary's humility made her the vessel of the Word. Recent studies of the theme have stressed that Annunciation references are frequent in the earliest Madonnas of Humility. They have treated the Palermo panel as a key example of this association between humility and Incarnation.

Below the main field is a predella with a subject unusual for the date. In the centre are the symbols of the Passion, the *arma Christi*. Two groups kneel before them, men on one side and women on the other, each led by two members of the confraternity in the hooded habits of the *disciplinati*. The 2015 entry describes them as "due gruppi di oranti (laici e flagellanti) inginocchiati davanti ai simboli della Passione", a formula that does not record the division between men and women. The 1998 entry links this choice to Neapolitan production of the mid-fourteenth century, notably the work of Roberto d'Oderisio. That link was first drawn in Bologna's 1969 study of painters at the Angevin court of Naples. The predella is the internal evidence that ties the panel to a penitential confraternity.

The "marble aedicule on twisted columns" sometimes mentioned is the painted architecture, not a carved frame. No published technical study has established whether any part of a frame is original; the panel is now displayed without an outer frame.

## The inscription: what it says and how it has been misquoted

The inscription is the panel's principal document, and its exact wording has often been transmitted carelessly. The diplomatic transcription in the 2015 *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* is the most careful accessible version. It marks abbreviations with square brackets, interpuncts with mid-dots and line breaks with slashes:

> «† N[OST]RA · D[OMI]NA · DE HVMILITATE · / M CCC·XXXX·VI · HOC · / OPVS · PINSIT· MAG/ISTER B[AR]TOLOMEV[S] · DE · / CAM·VLIO PINTOR»

The 1998 encyclopaedia entry gives a normalised reading of the same text: "Nostra Domina de Humilitate / MCCCXXXXVI hoc / opus pinsit mag / ister Bartolomeus de / Camulio pintor". The two agree in substance. The text is a title, *Nostra Domina de Humilitate*, followed by a dated signature: in 1346 master Bartolomeo of Camogli, painter, painted this work.

Several points follow, each correcting something commonly repeated. First, the signature does not read "BARTHOLOMEUS DE CAMULIO PINXIT". That formula, which circulates widely, is a classicising normalisation. The verb as transcribed is *pinsit*, not the classical *pinxit*. The name is *B[ar]tolomeu[s]*, without the *h* of the learned spelling *Bartholomeus*. The signature also adds two terms the formula drops: the title *magister* and the vernacularising professional designation *pintor*. These are not trivial details. *Pinsit* and *pintor* show the vernacular pressure on the painter's Latin. They are exactly the kind of evidence that tells us how a Genoese workshop of the 1340s composed its inscriptions. Quotations should reproduce them.

Second, the date is written with the unusual sequence M CCC XXXX VI, using four Xs rather than the subtractive XL. It reads unambiguously as 1346. The dates 1340, 1347, "1346–47" and "1339–1346" that circulate have no basis in the inscription. The date 1340 goes back to a nineteenth-century dictionary of painters, and "1339–1346" merges the start of the documented career with the date of the panel.

Third, the title has been transmitted in two forms. The *Dizionario Biografico* and the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* expand the abbreviation as *D[OMI]NA*, giving the Latin *Nostra Domina de Humilitate*. Meiss's English-language successors more often report it as *Nostra Donna de Humilitate*, and a 2009 monograph is quoted in review as reading *N[OST]RA D[ON]NA DE HUMILITATE*. The surviving letters are an abbreviated form, and either expansion is palaeographically possible. *Domina* fits the Latin of the rest of the formula. *Donna* fits its vernacular features (*pinsit*, *pintor*). The panel itself shows the abbreviated form DNA with a mark of abbreviation, which does not decide between the two. The two principal Italian reference works agree on *Domina*, but neither expansion can be presented as certain. What is not in doubt is the significance of the phrase. As the 2009 monograph emphasised, the Palermo panel shows that the composition was associated with the concept of humility by 1346, while many other early examples carry no title at all. The modern art-historical label "Madonna of Humility" is therefore, as a 2000 study in *RACAR* put it, "a legitimate historical label", taken from this inscription.

## Provenance: San Francesco, the Disciplinati and the question of destination

The panel's documented history begins in the seventeenth century, not the fourteenth. The 2015 entry cites three early modern sources. One is the manuscript *Religionis Christianae Panormi libri sex* of the seventeenth-century Palermitan historian Pietro Cannizzaro, Biblioteca Comunale di Palermo, Qq.E.36, fol. 416r–v. Another is the manuscript of the eighteenth-century canon who wrote *Le chiese e le case dei regolari di Palermo*, Biblioteca Comunale di Palermo, Qq.E.5, fol. 545r–v. The third is the same author's printed *Palermo divoto di Maria Vergine* (Palermo, 1719), vol. I, pp. 209–210. These sources record the panel in the cloister of the Franciscan convent of San Francesco d'Assisi in Palermo, on the wall adjoining the church. From there it passed to the Museo Nazionale of Palermo, as photographs in the Fondazione Zeri archive still label it, and later to the Galleria Regionale della Sicilia at Palazzo Abatellis. The gallery opened in 1954 in Carlo Scarpa's installation.

The 2015 entry judges that the panel probably reached Sicily *ab antiquo*, citing De Floriani's monograph, a 1980 essay on Genoese painters in Sicily and a 1983 study of the Pisan chapels in San Francesco. That is, it arrived in or near the fourteenth century and not through modern collecting. This is the scholarly consensus, and it is reasonable. But between 1346 and the seventeenth century the panel's location is not documented, and the 1998 entry warns explicitly that the cloister "potrebbe non costituire l'originaria destinazione della tavola". The cloister location is the earliest recorded location, not necessarily the original one.

The most plausible original context, though not a documented one, is the confraternity of disciplinati known as San Nicolò lo Reale. For the confraternity the documentation is better. According to the scholarship on Palermitan confraternities, the company met in a chapel attached to the convent of San Francesco, and was later incorporated into it. Its statutes, the *Capitoli*, were drawn up in 1343. An exhibition catalogue of the Regione Siciliana dates them precisely to 6 January 1343, and they survive in later copies, the oldest of the fifteenth century. The *Capitoli* themselves state that they were modelled on two earlier texts: the statutes of the Florentine disciplinati and those of the company of San Domenico in Genoa. The date 1306 sometimes given as the confraternity's foundation, and repeated by the Palermo diocese, probably refers instead to the Genoese San Domenico statutes used as a model. This interpretation, advanced by earlier philological scholarship and taken up in a 2010 study, implies that the claim of a 1306 foundation is a later misreading. At least four other Palermitan confraternities also claim 1306. The confraternity's association with King Frederick III of Aragon, who appears among its deceased members, is said to explain its title "lo Reale". In 1388 it commissioned from Antonio Veneziano the large panel of the roll of deceased brothers, tempera on panel measuring 168 × 125 cm according to its catalogue record, now in the Museo Diocesano of Palermo.

The predella of Bartolomeo's panel, with its kneeling flagellants, makes a disciplinati destination likely. The Franciscan setting of San Nicolò lo Reale and the panel's later appearance in the cloister of the same convent make that confraternity the obvious candidate. The 2009 monograph is summarised in review as stating without hesitation that the 1346 image "was made for a flagellant confraternity". A 2014 study of painted cosmatesque ornament speaks more carefully of the San Nicolò company as the one to which the Madonna "perhaps" belonged. A recent English-language study of flagellant confraternities in Italian art says frankly that the panel belonged to a company in Palermo, "but we are not sure which one". Those three formulations mark exactly the boundary between what is documented and what is inferred. That the panel was made for a confraternity of disciplinati is a strong inference from its imagery. That it was made for San Nicolò lo Reale is a plausible hypothesis. That it was commissioned by the confraternity in a documented act is not established.

There is, however, a circumstantial link that deserves attention. The San Nicolò *Capitoli* of 1343 were modelled on a Genoese disciplinati statute. Three years later a Genoese painter signed a Madonna whose predella shows disciplinati at prayer. That does not prove a commission, but it does point to a channel of confraternal contact between Genoa and Palermo. That channel would explain how a Palermitan company came to own the work of a painter who, as far as the documents show, worked in Genoa.

A different and widely repeated claim says the panel was painted "for the chapel of the Genoese" in San Francesco, or for "San Francesco dei Genovesi". San Francesco in Palermo did house chapels of foreign merchant "nations". The Pisan chapels have their own literature, and a Genoese presence is entirely plausible. But no source documents the panel's placement in a Genoese chapel. The claim seems to come from combining the painter's Genoese origin with the general knowledge that merchant nations had chapels in the church. It is a conjecture. Likewise, the link sometimes drawn between the predella flagellants and the plague-time flagellant movement of 1346–49 is anachronistic. The Black Death reached Sicily only in the autumn of 1347. The Palermitan disciplinati were already an established institution with statutes by 1343. The flagellants in the predella are members of an organised civic confraternity, not participants in an epidemic penitential outbreak.

## Material evidence: support, frame, gilding and the limits of what is published

The published record on the panel's technique, carpentry, punchwork and conservation is thin.

The panel is painted in tempera and gold on wood. A recent study of flagellant confraternities in Italian art reproduces it with the dimensions 164.5 × 116.5 cm. That height is consistent with the panel being an independent altarpiece or confraternal image rather than a small object for private devotion, and it is comparable to the San Nicolò roll of 1388. The museum's own catalogue does not publish the measurement or an inventory number in an accessible form. The Fondazione Zeri photographic archive holds a print of the panel under the title "Bartolomeo Pellerano da Camogli – Madonna dell'Umiltà. Palermo – Museo Nazionale". Its record classifies the subject as "Madonna con Bambino, Simboli della Passione — scomparto di polittico". That classification belongs to the archive's descriptive system. It is not evidence that the panel was once the central compartment of a dismembered polyptych, a hypothesis that no study has argued.

The most pressing questions are whether the frame, the trilobed arch and the predella are original and integral with the main panel, and what the pattern of the punched gold ornament is. No published technical examination has answered them. The fictive cosmatesque frieze and the Annunciation spandrels are painted on surfaces that read as part of the original design. Writers since 1953 have discussed them as integral to the work's Martinian elegance, and nothing in the literature reviewed suggests that they are later additions. But the joinery between the main field and the predella, the construction of the arch and the state of the gilding are not described in the accessible literature. A punch-mark analysis of the kind now routine for Sienese and Florentine panels would be especially valuable in this case. It could test directly how close Bartolomeo's tooling is to the Martinian workshop in Avignon, which has so far been argued only on stylistic and iconographic grounds. Its absence is a real gap in the scholarship.

On conservation history, the same caution applies. The panel was shown in two exhibitions of the early 1950s that revived interest in the painter: the Bordeaux exhibition *Les primitifs méditerranéens* of 1952, catalogue no. 42, and the Messina exhibition *Antonello da Messina e la pittura del '400 in Sicilia* of 1953, catalogue no. 1. It was then installed in Scarpa's gallery, which opened in 1954. Palazzo Abatellis was closed for restoration from February 2008 to November 2009, with Scarpa's installation largely preserved. Whether the panel itself was treated on any of these occasions, by whom and with what findings, is not documented in the published literature.

## Critical fortunes: from Sicilian anonym to Mediterranean protagonist

The modern critical history of the panel shows clearly how a work's meaning depends on the frame of reference brought to it. The great nineteenth-century compiler of documents on Sicilian art published the panel in his history of the fine arts in Sicily from the Normans to the end of the fourteenth century, attributing it to a Sicilian artist. The two Treccani entries cite the relevant volume with different dates, 1859 and 1862, a bibliographic discrepancy that remains unresolved. As early as 1870, Genoese antiquarians recognised the signature *de Camulio* and claimed the panel for the Ligurian painter whose documents they were publishing. These were the author of the *Notizie dei professori del disegno in Liguria* and the author of a contemporary set of *Appunti artistici sopra Levanto*.

For the next eighty years, however, the panel was generally judged a provincial work of Sienese or more broadly Tuscan derivation. That is how the standard surveys of Italian painting from the late nineteenth century to the 1920s characterised it. An English-language encyclopaedia article still calls it "of a somewhat mediocre quality", a judgement that was overturned in the 1950s. The same period saw the first attempts to enlarge Bartolomeo's catalogue, discussed below, all of which have since failed.

The turning point was the 1953 essay "Frammento siciliano" in *Paragone* by <a href="/scholars.html#longhi-roberto">Roberto Longhi</a>, written after the two exhibitions. It read the Palermo panel not as a provincial echo of Siena but as an important document of the culture formed at Avignon under Simone Martini, and so as a key piece in what came to be called the "Mediterranean" circulation of Gothic painting. Both Treccani entries date the recognition of Bartolomeo's role in the formation of *arte mediterranea* to this essay. Subsequent scholarship, above all Bologna's studies of 1955, 1961 and 1969 and the monograph on Matteo Giovannetti and painting in Provence (1962, revised 1991), developed the Avignonese reading. It linked Bartolomeo to the Martinian legacy in the papal city, and through the predella and its Neapolitan parallels to Catalan and Angevin painting. De Floriani's monograph of 1979 then gathered the documents, the bibliography and the Ligurian context. It added two important suggestions: that Bartolomeo may also have known Simone's frescoes at Assisi, and that his work should be related to surviving Genoese illumination and panel painting. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry for Sicily, written in the late 1990s, sums up the result. Before the mid-fourteenth century, the Palermo Madonna and the reworked Crucifix of Cefalù Cathedral showed that contact with Genoa gave the major Sicilian centres access to the main developments along the Siena–Avignon axis.

## Meiss and the lost prototype

Meiss's article of 1936 is the classic study of the Madonna of Humility. Its argument still shapes the field, even among those who reject it. Its core is simple. The Madonna of Humility is defined by "the single fact that she was seated on the ground", and the etymological relation between *humilitas* and *humus*, which medieval writers from Isidore onward made explicit, gives the image its meaning. The surviving early examples are the Palermo panel of 1346, a panel in Berlin from the circle of Simone Martini, the related Neapolitan works and Simone's Avignon fresco. Their similarities, Meiss argued, are best explained as derivations from a lost prototype by Simone Martini himself. He presented the intimate, nursing variant as essentially Sienese in sensibility. He contrasted the more formal, frontal Child of the Avignon lunette with the *lactans* compositions of Palermo and Berlin, but traced both versions to a Martinian invention of roughly the same moment.

Where Meiss placed that lost prototype is reported inconsistently in the secondary literature. The 1998 encyclopaedia entry says that Meiss supposed a prototype painted by Simone during his Neapolitan stay of 1317. Other summaries, including a Courtauld Institute technical report and later reviews, say more generally that Meiss located the invention in Siena with a panel by Simone. Both summaries agree that Meiss credited Simone Martini with inventing the type in a lost work, and that the Palermo panel was for him a derivative, not an original, witness. They disagree on the city.

For Bartolomeo, the effect of Meiss's thesis was double. It made the Palermo panel internationally famous, as the earliest dated witness to a type that would spread from Italy to Catalonia, France, Bohemia and the Low Countries. But it also subordinated the panel to an absent original, so that its interest lay mainly in what it reflected. The later historiography of the type can be read as a slow recovery of the panel's own meaning.

## After Meiss: Avignon, manuscripts and the gradual descent

The first major revision came from Italian scholarship of the 1950s and 1960s, led by Bologna. It shifted the hypothetical prototype from Simone's pre-Avignon career to his Avignon years. According to the 1998 encyclopaedia entry, the Avignonese hypothesis proposed that the prototype dated from Simone's Avignon period and was made in parallel with the frescoed lunette over the portal of Notre-Dame-des-Doms. It also proposed that the prototype might be identified with a lost painting of 1346 over the door of the chapel of Saint-Martial in the Papal Palace. Simone died in Avignon in 1344, so a work of 1346 could not be by him. As reported, the suggestion may refer to work by his Avignonese successors, or the date may concern the record rather than the execution. The Avignonese hypothesis drew support from a further source. A 1964 study of fourteenth-century optics and aesthetics pointed out that the Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> theologian Agostino Trionfo (d. 1328), writing at Avignon, had given special emphasis to Mary's humility in two short treatises, *In salutationem et Annunciationem* and *In Canticum Deiparae Mariae Virginis*. The papal city therefore offered both the visual and the theological conditions for the type to crystallise.

The Avignon lunette itself is the principal fixed point in this debate. Simone Martini arrived in Avignon around the beginning of 1336; two sonnets by Petrarch<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-petrarch" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> provide the evidence. The portal frescoes of Notre-Dame-des-Doms, a Blessing Christ in the tympanum and a Madonna of Humility with the kneeling Cardinal Jacopo Stefaneschi in the lunette, are generally linked to the cardinal's commission. They are usually dated around 1341, the year of his death, although some French sources give a range of 1336–40 or 1336–44. The frescoes are badly damaged, but their sinopie survive and are displayed in the Palais des Papes. In this composition the Child is not nursing but holds a scroll inscribed *Ego sum lux mundi*. Whatever the exact date, the Avignon lunette is earlier than the Palermo panel. It is therefore the earliest surviving Madonna of Humility, while the Palermo panel is the earliest surviving *dated* one. The distinction is simple, but it is regularly lost.

A second revision came from Dutch and North American scholarship of the later twentieth century, which placed the type in a longer evolution. A 1969 study of Marian humility and glorification in Sienese painting by <a href="/scholars.html#hendrik-willem-van-os">Henk van Os</a> set the type within Sienese devotional imagery as a whole. A 2000 article in *RACAR* argued that the Virgin's "stepped descent" to the ground had been prepared over decades in Annunciations, Holy Families and Crucifixions. Examples include Giotto's kneeling Annunciate in the Arena Chapel, Taddeo Gaddi's seated Annunciate in the Baroncelli Chapel, Lorenzettian Holy Families seated on the floor and the many fainting Virgins of early Trecento Crucifixions. It also connected this development to Franciscan devotional literature, especially the *Meditationes vitae Christi*. The same article reported a relevant document, drawn to its author's attention by a specialist in Trecento painting. In the night of 15 August 1334 at Avignon, the papal scribe Opicinus de Canistris saw in a dream the Virgin seated on the ground with the Child in her lap, and recorded the vision in his autobiography. That vision is two years earlier than Simone's arrival and some seven years earlier than the lunette. It suggests, without proving, that an image of this kind was already circulating in Avignon before Simone painted his fresco.

The most far-reaching revision came in 2009 with a monograph published in Woodbridge in the series Bristol Studies in Medieval Cultures. It explicitly rejected the search for an "ur-image" as "a tired and unproductive discussion about the influence of a lost, unknown painting". It proposed instead that the type developed from the image of the Woman of the Apocalypse seated on the ground in French manuscripts, especially a group made in and around Metz. Nursing and Annunciation references were added in Italian and Avignonese adaptations. The book identifies four early examples from the 1340s and 1350s: Simone's Avignon fresco of about 1341, Roberto d'Oderisio's panel of about 1345 from San Domenico in Naples, the Berlin panel attributed to Lippo Memmi of about 1345–50, and Bartolomeo's Palermo panel of 1346. In a chapter on early appearances in Naples and Palermo, it traces the type's spread from Avignon to the South through the Angevin court's frequent contacts with the papal city. It reads each instance as a deliberate adaptation to its patrons' devotional needs. For the Palermo panel, the book emphasises the confraternal and commemorative context and the inscription's early association of the composition with humility.

Later scholarship has largely accepted the shift away from a single prototype. It still argues over whether the Metz manuscripts explain all the features of the earliest Italian examples. A 2021 study in *Religions* accepts the apocalyptic derivation but stresses that the earliest images refer to the Annunciation and Incarnation, and cites the Palermo panel and its spandrel Annunciation as a principal example. A 2012 essay on the "Avignon type" in Siena again presents Simone's Avignon fresco as the seminal work. What emerges from all this is not a single replacement for Meiss's thesis but a widened field. In that field the Palermo panel is no longer a reflection of a lost masterpiece. It is an early, local and highly deliberate version of a type still in the making.

## Primacy claims and their correction

This historiography makes it possible to correct the commonest error about the panel: that it is "the earliest known painting of this type", "the alpha of all paintings" of the theme, or "the first Madonna of Humility". Both formulas overstate. The Avignon lunette of about 1341 is earlier. Roberto d'Oderisio's Neapolitan panel is dated around 1345 by the 2009 monograph, which, if correct, would also place it before 1346. The Berlin panel may be close in date. The Opicinus vision of 1334 suggests that the image was being formulated earlier still. What can be said with complete security is what the 2015 *Dizionario Biografico* entry says, in formulations that can be quoted. The panel is "unanimemente riconosciuto il più antico esemplare superstite datato" of the type. In the words of the 1998 entry, it is "la più antica testimonianza di cronologia certa di questo tema iconografico". Its primacy is a primacy of *dated survival*, not of invention.

A second and subtler error is to treat the Palermo panel as the *source* of the type's spread through the Mediterranean. Bartolomeo was undoubtedly an important channel through which Avignonese ideas reached Genoa and Sicily, and possibly Catalonia. But the Neapolitan versions have their own direct Avignonese sources through the Angevin court. Bologna himself, discussing Roberto d'Oderisio's fresco in San Domenico, concluded that the affinities with Bartolomeo were "notevoli e insistenti". He judged it more likely, however, that Roberto took the idea directly from Avignon rather than by way of Genoa. On present knowledge, the Palermo panel is better described as one of several parallel early receptions of an Avignonese formula than as the start of a chain.

## The catalogue that shrank: attributions proposed and withdrawn

Bartolomeo's catalogue has a peculiar history. Every attempt to enlarge it has failed, so his secure oeuvre today is exactly what it was in 1870: the Palermo panel. Both Treccani entries say this plainly. The 2015 entry adds that the various attributional proposals made over time "non hanno avuto fortuna". The failed proposals are instructive in their own right, because they show how a single dated work can draw in anonymous material of similar character.

In 1921 a study in the *Bollettino d'arte*, the first monographic article on the painter, attributed to him the Madonna of Humility in the Pinacoteca Nazionale in Cagliari. In 1954 that panel was restored to the Portuguese painter Álvaro Pires de Évora in an article in *Paragone*, and the restitution has held. In 1925 the fifth volume of a standard English-language survey of the Italian schools proposed the Madonna of Santa Maria di Castello in Genoa, a work now discussed instead in connection with the Master of Santa Maria di Castello. In 1947 a study of Trecento painting in Naples proposed the Madonna of Humility in San Domenico Maggiore. That work was reassigned in 1969 by Bologna to the Neapolitan Master of the Tempere Francescane. In each case the proposal rested on shared iconography or a general Martinian character, and in each case closer stylistic analysis found a different hand.

Two further groups of material belong in a separate category: works related to Bartolomeo's art, not attributed to him. De Floriani's work of 1979 drew attention to the frontispiece of the Matricola of the Genoese porters' guild, the *Caravana*, dated 1340 and kept in the Archivio di Stato in Genoa. She also pointed to a polyptych of the Madonna and Child with scenes of the Life of the Virgin and the Passion, formerly in Savona and now in the cathedral of Albi. She presented both as evidence for the Ligurian culture in which Bartolomeo worked, and the encyclopaedia entries present them in the same way. They are context, not catalogue. A 1996 exhibition entry on the Master of the Croce dei Piani d'Invrea is also cited in the 2015 entry among the unsuccessful or related proposals.

The principal Sicilian proposal concerns the painted Crucifix in the cathedral of Cefalù. A 1981 synthesis of figurative production in Sicily from the late twelfth to the mid-fifteenth century, in the multivolume *Storia della Sicilia*, proposed that the cross had been begun on its recto by Bartolomeo. On this hypothesis the panel arrived in Sicily from Genoa and its other figures were completed there by Tommaso de Vigilia. That would make the cross a fourteenth-century work reworked in the fifteenth century. A specialist in Angevin and southern Italian painting reportedly followed this proposal, but it has been firmly rejected elsewhere. A 1992 study of painted crosses in western Sicily is cited among the dissenting voices, and the cathedral's own guidance materials, drawing on the scholarship, call the early dating not shareable. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry on Sicilian painting takes a middle position. It says the original layer of the reworked Cefalù Crucifix has been "comprehensibly" brought close to Bartolomeo and to early fourteenth-century Ligurian culture. It is a contested hypothesis about a heavily reworked object, not an attribution.

No work in Pisa, or of Pisan provenance, has been attributed to Bartolomeo. Pisa enters his story differently, through the Pisan colony in Palermo and the Pisan chapels in San Francesco, which make up part of the cultural setting of the panel's later location, and through the large Pisan-made panels exported to Sicily, among them, it has been argued, the San Nicolò roll by Antonio Veneziano. A "Pisan Bartolomeo" has no basis in the literature.

## Genoa, Pisa, Avignon, Palermo: Bartolomeo in Mediterranean exchange

With the documentary, iconographic and attributional ground cleared, Bartolomeo's historical place can be stated with some precision. He was a Genoese master of the second quarter of the fourteenth century. His formation was probably shaped by a local tradition already open to Siena and Assisi, whether or not that tradition was mediated by his putative father. His one surviving work shows close familiarity with the inventions of Simone Martini's Avignon period. It is earlier than any other dated example of a type that Avignon seems to have crystallised, and it was made, or at least ended up, in a Franciscan and confraternal setting in Palermo.

Each of the four cities named in the title of this section played a different part. Genoa was where Bartolomeo trained and worked, and its maritime networks connected him to every other point of the system. The city's commercial colonies stretched from the Provençal coast to Sicily and beyond. Its disciplinati statutes provided a model for the Palermitan company of San Nicolò. Its painters, as the later case of Barnaba da Modena "pinxit in Janua" would show, produced panels for export to Andalusia and Murcia. That a Genoese workshop should produce a large confraternal image for Palermo is unremarkable within this framework.

Avignon was the source of Bartolomeo's most up-to-date ideas. Exactly how they reached him is undocumented. He may have travelled to the papal city, or seen Martinian works brought to Genoa, or learnt the formula through drawings or portable panels. Nothing in the documents settles the question. Avignon was only a short sea voyage from Genoa by way of the Rhône delta, and the papal court drew Ligurian merchants, bankers and clergy. Contact of some kind is entirely plausible. The 1979 suggestion that Bartolomeo also knew Simone's Assisi frescoes widens the possible sources without making any one of them certain.

Pisa provides the comparative backdrop more than any direct link. Throughout the Trecento, Pisan workshops sent large panels to Sicily, and the Pisan community in Palermo shaped the patronage of San Francesco through its chapels. Within that market, a Genoese work such as Bartolomeo's panel stands out as an alternative channel to the Tuscan supply. It is Ligurian, Martinian and Avignonese, not Pisan.

Palermo, finally, is where the panel acquired its meaning for posterity. It probably hung there from the fourteenth century onward. Its image of the Virgin seated on the ground, recorded in the seventeenth-century cloister of San Francesco, became part of the city's Marian devotion, as the 1719 *Palermo divoto di Maria Vergine* attests. It has been suggested that later Palermitan images, among them the Virgin seated on the ground in the Nativity of the Oratory of San Lorenzo next to the convent, look back to it. The suggestion is a reading, not an established derivation. From Palermo too, through the exhibitions of 1952–53 and the 1953 essay that followed them, Bartolomeo entered modern scholarship as a protagonist of "Mediterranean" Gothic, alongside Catalan, Neapolitan and Provençal painting.

## Conclusion

What can be said of Bartolomeo Pellerano da Camogli rests on a few documents and one panel. He was a painter from Camogli, from the Pellerano family, with a workshop in Genoa near the palace of the Abate del popolo. He took an apprentice from Rapallo on 2 February 1339, was commissioned to paint an altarpiece for San Siro on 30 March 1346, and was dead by late 1348, leaving minor children. His relationships with the painters Opizzino and Antonio Pellerano are probable but not documented. The identification of Opizzino with the Master of Santa Maria di Castello is a scholarly hypothesis. No Palermo document of 1339 and no banner for the Disciplinati of San Nicolò lo Reale can be verified.

His surviving work is a single large panel, now in Palazzo Abatellis, signed and dated 1346 in an inscription that reads *pinsit* and *pintor*. It carries the title *Nostra D[omi]na de Humilitate*, which gave the Madonna of Humility its art-historical name. It is the earliest surviving *dated* example of the type, but not the earliest example. Its predella shows that it belonged to a penitential confraternity of disciplinati. Its later placement in the cloister of San Francesco makes the Franciscan company of San Nicolò lo Reale, whose 1343 statutes were modelled on a Genoese text, the most plausible owner, though not a documented one. Every attempt to attribute other works to him has failed, and the Cefalù Crucifix remains a contested hypothesis.

Meiss's thesis of a lost Martinian prototype, which made the panel famous, has been relocated to Avignon by Bologna and his successors. Later scholarship, which emphasises French manuscripts, Franciscan devotion and the Virgin's gradual descent to the ground, has broken it down further. Through all these revisions the Palermo panel has kept, and in some ways strengthened, its importance. It is no longer read as a reflection of something lost. It is valued as one of the few precisely dated points from which the early history of a major European devotional image can be reconstructed.

The panel is the only surviving work signed by Bartolomeo da Camogli and the earliest dated example of the Madonna of Humility, the image of the Virgin seated on the ground that its own inscription names *de humilitate*. It is recorded from the seventeenth century in the cloister of San Francesco in Palermo and is now in the Galleria Regionale della Sicilia at Palazzo Abatellis. It consists of a main field under a painted arch and a predella of three compartments, framed together by a painted border.

The Virgin sits low on the ground, turned three-quarters to the left, so that her body forms a broad triangle that fills the lower half of the field. Her blue mantle, edged with a gold border with a running pattern, covers her head and falls in wide folds over her knees to the ground, where its hem turns back to show a lining of red and gold. Under it she wears a red dress. The ground beneath her is painted dark brown and flecked with gold, perhaps to suggest earth or a flowered meadow.

Her head is inclined to the left and her eyes are lowered towards the Child, with an expression of calm attention. Thin gold rays spread from her head across the dark blue ground, which is scattered with small gold stars. The rays replace the usual disc of the halo and, together with the stars, give the Virgin a celestial character that contrasts with her humble position on the ground.

The Child lies across her lap, wrapped in an orange-yellow cloth that leaves his upper body and legs bare. He holds his mother's breast with both hands and sucks at it while turning his eyes outwards towards the viewer. The Virgin supports him with both hands, the right under his body, the left on his legs. The outward gaze of the nursing Child, which draws the viewer into the scene, is one of the most frequently discussed features of the panel.

The inscription is written in gold capitals in short lines on the dark ground at either side of the group, at the level of the Virgin's head and again at the level of her knees. The lines alternate between the left and right sides, so that the text must be read across the figures: the title, *Nostra Domina de Humilitate*, at the top, and the date and signature below, ending with *Bartolomeus de Camulio pintor*. The placing of the words in the field itself, rather than on the frame, makes the title part of the image.

The arch above the Virgin is trilobed and rests on two twisted columns with white capitals, painted to imitate marble. The spandrels hold the two figures of the Annunciation. A frieze of painted inlay with rosettes runs around three sides of the panel. The Annunciation links the nursing Virgin to the Incarnation, and the painted marble and inlay give the image an architectural setting of Martinian refinement.

The predella, divided into three compartments, shows the instruments of the Passion at the centre and two groups of kneeling devotees at the sides, led by members of a confraternity of *disciplinati* in white hooded habits. It places the patrons of the panel directly beneath the Virgin, as witnesses of the Passion and petitioners for her intercession.

The panel is in good condition. The blue of the mantle and the red and orange of the garments keep their brightness, under a fine network of cracks, and the gold of the rays and of the inscription is worn but legible. The panel is displayed without an outer frame, and the bare wood of the support shows around the painted border, with losses along the upper edge; the predella is more abraded than the main field. The image follows a precise programme: the Virgin of Humility, named by the inscription, the Annunciation above, and the Passion with the confraternity below, linking the Incarnation to the penitential devotion of the patrons.

The predella is integral to the design of the panel and is the main evidence for its connection with a confraternity of *disciplinati*.

The central compartment shows the *arma Christi* against a pale wall, without figures. At the left stands the column of the flagellation on a base, with a rope wound round it, and beside it a tall lance. At the centre a crossbar on a post carries two scourges and a dark vessel, perhaps the bucket of vinegar. At the right a ladder leans against the wall, the ladder of the Deposition.

Along the top of the compartment are smaller objects, painted in dark outline: further staffs or spear-points and, on the crossbar, a bird, probably the cock of Peter's denial. The objects are arranged with regularity, in an order that invites contemplation of each instrument in turn, as in devotional texts that list the instruments of the Passion.

In the left compartment a crowd of men kneels towards the centre. In the front row are two figures in white habits with tall pointed hoods that cover their faces, the costume of the *disciplinati*. Behind them the men wear long robes of white, grey and blue, and their faces, some bearded, are visible. They hold their hands joined or raised in prayer.

In the right compartment two hooded figures kneel in front with joined hands, followed by a crowd of women in white veils. Among them, one wears a black mantle and another a red mantle, which may indicate different conditions or ranks. The division between men and women on the two sides of the instruments of the Passion follows the usual arrangement of devotees in medieval images and in church.

The hooded figures at the head of both groups are the members of the confraternity, shown in the penitential habit they wore during processions and acts of discipline. The scourges at the centre are both instruments of Christ's Passion and the instruments of the brothers' own penance. The men and women behind them may be the lay members or associates of the company, or the wider community it represented.

The devotees kneel before the *arma Christi* rather than before a narrative scene of the Passion. The predella turns the Passion into an object of contemplation and places the confraternity in the presence of the instruments of salvation, beneath the Virgin whose intercession it seeks.

The predella is more worn than the main field. The paint surface is crossed by a dense network of cracks, and the white habits and veils have lost much of their modelling. The faces in the back rows are faint, and some areas of the lateral compartments are abraded to the preparation. The composition and the main figures nonetheless remain clearly legible.

The small panel in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin is one of the Sienese works regularly set beside the Palermo Madonna in the history of the Madonna of Humility. It came to Berlin in 1821 with the collection of the merchant Edward Solly. The museum catalogues it as the work of an anonymous painter of the circle of Lippo Memmi, dated around 1340; elsewhere in the literature it is attributed to Lippo Memmi himself and dated around 1345–1350. Its dimensions, 32.2 by 23.4 centimetres, show that it was made for private devotion.

The Virgin sits low, close to the ground, on a round cushion patterned in gold with a dark lattice, placed on a carpet that runs across the whole width of the panel. The carpet is painted with red, white and blue geometric motifs and a border of meanders. The cushion and carpet give the humble seat on the ground a precious setting.

Her deep blue mantle covers her head and falls in broad folds over her knees to the carpet, where its edge, lined in white, turns over in a series of curving folds at the right. The mantle is edged with a gold border of ornamental letters. Beneath it she wears a dress of gold brocade with a floral pattern on a dark ground, worked with great delicacy in gold over the paint.

The Virgin inclines her head towards the Child with an expression of gentle concentration. Her face, with long almond-shaped eyes, a long nose and a small mouth, is framed by the white lining of the mantle. Her halo is punched with a border of small motifs and with fine radiating lines, which also surround the head of the Child.

The Child lies across her lap, wrapped from the waist down in a pink cloth with a gold hem. He holds his mother's breast with both hands as he feeds and turns his eyes outwards towards the viewer, while she supports him with both hands. The combination of the nursing Child with an outward gaze is the same as in the Palermo panel and is the main reason the two works are compared.

The comparison also shows the differences. The Berlin panel is small, set against gold and turned towards the right; the Palermo panel is large, set against a dark starry ground under an architectural arch, and turned towards the left. In Berlin the Virgin wears precious brocade and sits on a cushion and carpet; in Palermo she wears a plainer dress and sits on a ground flecked with gold. The Berlin panel carries no inscription naming the subject.

These differences have been read in different ways. For Meiss the Berlin and Palermo panels were derivations from a lost prototype by Simone Martini; later scholarship has preferred to see them as parallel early versions of a type still in formation, of which the Avignon lunette of Simone Martini is the earliest surviving example. The Berlin panel represents the Sienese, precious and intimate form of the type, the Palermo panel its monumental, confraternal form.

The panel is in good condition. The blue of the mantle, the gold brocade and the pink of the Child's cloth are well preserved, and the punched decoration of the gold ground and haloes is intact. A few small losses and some abrasion are visible in the gold ground at the right. The gilded moulded frame surrounds the painted field closely.

The panel comes from the Dominican church of San Domenico Maggiore in Naples and is now in the Museo di Capodimonte. Once proposed as a work of Bartolomeo da Camogli and later given to the Master of the Tempere Francescane, it is attributed by more recent literature to Roberto d'Oderisio, a Neapolitan painter of the generation after Giotto's stay at the Angevin court, and dated around 1340–1345. It is one of the earliest surviving Madonnas of Humility and belongs to the Neapolitan current that the literature has connected with the predella of the Palermo panel.

The panel is shaped as a pointed arch, with a gold ground and a frame decorated with a running foliate ornament. The shape suggests that it was made as the upper part of an altarpiece or as a lunette above a door or an altar. The Virgin occupies the centre, and the flying angels fill the two sloping sides of the arch.

The Virgin sits low on a red cushion placed on a dark red marbled ground, which runs across the bottom of the panel. Her mantle, now very dark, is lined in green and edged with a gold border, and spreads in broad folds around her on the ground. Beneath it she wears a red dress with a gold border. A small star is painted on her right shoulder. Her head, turned slightly towards the viewer, is framed by a halo inscribed with the opening words of the angelic salutation, *gratia plena dominus tecum*, partly legible.

The Child sits on her lap in a red tunic, feeds at her breast and looks out of the picture with a serious expression. The Virgin holds him with both hands. As in the Palermo and Berlin panels, the nursing Child turns his eyes towards the viewer, and the gesture of the mother's hands and the position of the Child on her lap are close to those of the Palermo panel.

At either side two columns of six small angels fly towards the Virgin with outstretched arms. Their wings are painted in bands of red, white, blue and gold, and their haloes are punched. The repetition of the angels in diagonal rows creates a rhythmic frame for the central group and gives the composition a celestial setting that the Palermo panel expresses instead through the starry blue ground.

Two black labels in the gold ground, at the level of the Virgin's shoulders, read *Mat[er]* at the left and *Omnium* at the right, "Mother of all". Like the inscription of the Palermo panel, the labels attach a title to the image, but the title here stresses the Virgin's universal motherhood rather than her humility.

On the ground at the left are a shield with battlements and a red band and, beside it, a crescent moon; at the right are a shield with diagonal bands and a two-handled vase with white lilies. The shields are presumably those of the patrons. The crescent moon recalls the Woman of the Apocalypse, "clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet", whose image has been proposed as a source of the Madonna of Humility; the lilies are a sign of the Virgin's purity.

The panel is in good condition, with its original shape and much of its gilding. The blue of the Virgin's mantle has darkened to near-black, probably through alteration of the pigment, and the marbled ground is worn. The comparison with Palermo shows two early versions of the same type made in the 1340s for different audiences: a Neapolitan panel with heraldry and celestial angels, and a Genoese confraternal image with the Passion beneath the Virgin.

## Introduction: A Well-Documented Painter with an Uncertain Corpus

Few Sienese painters of the fourteenth century present the historian with so paradoxical a profile as *Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio*. On the one hand, he is among the best-documented artists of his generation: his name recurs across four decades of Sienese communal, cathedral, and hospital records, and he was repeatedly summoned to arbitrate the value of other men's work and to preside over the deliberations of his city's government. On the other hand, only two signed and dated paintings by his hand survive intact, so that almost the whole of his artistic personality has had to be reconstructed—slowly, contentiously, and with frequent reversals—by connoisseurship rather than by document. The result is a career in which the biographical armature is unusually firm while the catalogue of works remains, even now, provisional and disputed.

This essay attempts to hold those two dimensions together. It is organized thematically rather than chronologically, on the premise that the most illuminating questions about Jacopo are not "what did he do next?" but rather "what do we actually know, and how do we know it?" Throughout, I have tried to distinguish sharply among four epistemic registers: what is documented in primary sources; what commands broad scholarly consensus; what remains genuinely contested among specialists; and what is frank hypothesis. Jacopo is a painter about whom a good deal of confident-sounding but poorly grounded information circulates, and one of the aims of what follows is to separate the archival bedrock from the accretions of guidebook and encyclopedia repetition.

Three modern scholars are treated here in some depth, because the modern understanding of Jacopo is substantially their creation: [Roberto Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto), who in 1955 isolated the anonymous personality of the "Maestro degli Ordini"; [Luciano Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano), who in 1972 identified that master with Jacopo di Mino and thereby gave the documented name a body of work; and [Cristina De Benedictis](/scholars.html#de-benedictis-cristina), whose studies of Sienese painting between 1330 and 1370 provided the fullest critical framing of his development. Around these three revolve the nineteenth-century documentary foundations laid by [Gaetano Milanesi](/scholars.html#milanesi-gaetano) and a range of later contributions—by [Frederick Mason Perkins](/scholars.html#perkins-frederick-mason), [Millard Meiss](/scholars.html#meiss-millard), [Mojmír Frinta](/scholars.html#frinta-mojmir-svatopluk), and others—that will be cited more briefly.

## The Documentary Record: Life, Guild, and Civic Office

The documentary portrait of Jacopo di Mino rests overwhelmingly on a single monumental nineteenth-century publication: Gaetano Milanesi's *Documenti per la storia dell'arte senese*, whose first volume appeared in Siena in 1854. Milanesi, later famous for his monumental annotated edition of Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>'s *Vite*, was an archivist of extraordinary diligence, and his transcriptions from the Sienese archives remain the point of departure for all subsequent research on the artist. It is a sobering measure of how much scholarship still depends on him that the standard modern biographical entry cites Milanesi 1854, volume I, at pages 27, 40, 50, 263–264, 269–272, 275, 354, and 365 for the great bulk of its documentary claims.

What do these documents establish? First, the outlines of a life. Jacopo was born in Siena around 1315–1319, the son of a certain Mino, of the *popolo* (parish district) of Sant'Antonio. In 1344 he married Caterina di Cecco di Tura, a baker's daughter (*fornaio*); in 1366, in a second marriage, he wed Margherita d'Angelo di Tuccio. He had four children, whose names are recorded, Giovanna, Filippo, Agnolina, and Giacomo. Neither the place nor the date of his death is recorded, but a document establishes that he was already dead by 1396. This is a fuller familial record than survives for most of his contemporaries, and it is worth stressing that it is documentary fact, not inference.

Second, the documents establish his standing within the corporate structures of the Sienese painters' craft. He is recorded as inscribed in the *Breve dell'Arte dei pittori senesi*—the statute and matriculation roll of the painters' guild—of 1355, and again in guild records of 1363 (the *libro delle capitudini delle arti*) and 1389. The *Breve* of 1355 has a particular importance for the social history of Sienese painting: it is the first true matriculation list of the corporation, into which only Sienese painters could be enrolled.

A precise and telling detail emerges from the order of names in that list. As Cristina De Benedictis's account of Luca di Tommè in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* records, in the 1355 *Breve* Luca "figura al terzo posto, dopo Lippo Vanni e Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio"—that is, he stands third, after Lippo Vanni and Jacopo di Mino. The order of the first three names—Lippo Vanni, then Jacopo di Mino, then Luca di Tommè—is not a trivial curiosity; it is a rough contemporary index of seniority and standing within the profession at mid-century, and it places Jacopo, by 1355, among the two or three most eminent painters then active in the city. (One caveat should be entered: although the *Breve* bears the date 1355, some scholars have preferred to date the surviving redaction to 1356 on scribal grounds, and [Peleo Bacci](/scholars.html#bacci-peleo) once questioned the date altogether; the substance of Jacopo's high placement is unaffected.)

Third, and most strikingly, the documents establish Jacopo's role as a civic figure and as a professional arbiter. He was repeatedly paid by the Commune, by the Opera del Duomo, and by the great hospital of Santa Maria della Scala, both for works of his own and for expert assessments (*perizie*) of the work of others. In 1373 he judged the value of a panel executed by Luca di Tommè for the Palazzo Pubblico of Siena; in 1376 he assessed works for the Cappella di Piazza del Campo; and between 1388 and 1389 he evaluated the designs of Mariano Romanelli for the head-pieces and tabernacles (*testiere e tabernacoli*) of the choir in the cathedral. To be chosen as a valuer of other masters' work is, in the economy of a medieval guild, a mark of unusual respect: it presupposes both technical authority and the trust of patrons and colleagues.

His public standing extended beyond the craft into the government of the city itself. According to [Magister](/scholars.html#magister-sara)'s reconstruction from the Milanesi documents, Jacopo was called to preside over the sittings of the communal Consiglio three times—in 1361, 1377, and 1379—and in 1362 he served as *ufficiale del Sale* (an official of the salt administration) for the *terzo* of Camollia, one of the three administrative districts of Siena. This is a level of civic engagement almost unmatched among Sienese painters of the century, and it corroborates the impression, given by the *Breve*, of a man near the summit of his profession's social prestige. A frequently repeated formulation in popular sources—that he was summoned "three times in twenty years to preside over the council"—compresses these facts accurately enough, though it is worth noting that the correct years are 1361, 1377, and 1379.

Jacopo's reputation, moreover, reached beyond the walls of Siena. A Pistoiese document, datable to around 1347–1350 and first published by [Alessandro Chiappelli](/scholars.html#chiappelli-alessandro) in 1900, records a discussion about who was the finest artist "from Giotto onward"; in it Jacopo appears among the "maestri di Siena li quali ci sono dati per li migliori"—the Sienese masters given as the best. That a painter's name should surface in a Pistoiese debate about artistic excellence is compelling evidence that his fame was not merely local.

## The Vanished Œuvre: What the Documents Commissioned and Time Destroyed

One of the cruel ironies of Jacopo's career is the disproportion between the abundance of documentary evidence for his activity and the scarcity of surviving works to which those documents can be attached. The archives record a substantial body of commissions—many of them prestigious, several of them public—of which essentially nothing survives. Any honest account of the painter must therefore reckon with a large, documented, but physically lost œuvre.

Among the losses recorded in the documents are the following. In Siena, frescoes for the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala, executed in 1354–1355; works in the cathedral carried out in 1366 together with Paolo di maestro Neri; the vault of a chapel adjacent to that of Sant'Ansano, again in the Duomo, frescoed in 1367–1368 in collaboration with Bartolo di Fredi; a coperta di Biccherna—one of the painted covers for the account-books of the communal treasury, a characteristically Sienese art form—executed in 1369. A panel was commissioned on 12 April 1372 for the high altar of the monastery church of Passignano. And in 1374 he was paid for frescoes in the new Palazzo dei Priori at Montepulciano.

This last commission deserves emphasis because it is documented with precision and because it bears directly on Jacopo's activity outside Siena. A payment recorded in the Montepulciano communal archive (the Apodisse dei Priori for 1374–1375) to "magistro Jacobo de Senis pictore" concerns a now-lost fresco of a Maestà with various saints, among them Christopher, in the Palazzo Nuovo dei Priori—a datum brought to scholarly attention by [Ilio Calabresi](/scholars.html#calabresi-ilio) in a 1981 notice in La Nazione. The Montepulciano payment is important for two reasons: it confirms that Jacopo worked as a fresco-painter of civic subjects for a subject town of the Sienese state, and it anchors his documented presence in the very town that also preserves what many consider his finest surviving panel, the Coronation of the Virgin now in the Museo Civico.

The scale of these losses should temper any confident generalization about Jacopo's "development." A painter of whom we possess two signed panels and a reconstructed body of attributions, but whose documented frescoes for the Scala, the Duomo, the Biccherna, and the Montepulciano priors have all perished, is a painter whom we know, in a real sense, only in fragments. The stylistic narrative offered below is built on the surviving fraction, and it would be prudent to remember how unrepresentative that fraction may be.

## The Two Fixed Points: The Signed and Dated Panels

Against this background of loss, two works stand as the immovable anchors of the entire reconstruction, because both bear the painter's signature. They are the only *opere certe*—certain works—in the strict sense, and everything else in the catalogue is attached to them by the fallible instrument of stylistic analysis.

### Madonna and Child ("Madonna del cardellino")

</figure>

The first is a panel of the Madonna and Child at Sarteano, a town in the southern Sienese contado. Painted for the medieval church of San Martino in Foro — demolished in 1841 — it is today displayed in the nineteenth-century church of San Martino (also documented as Santi Martino e Vittoria), within the Sala d'arte "Domenico Beccafumi" that gathers the artistic patrimony of the town's churches. It bears the signature "Jacobus Mini de Senis" together with a painted date read as 1342. The signature is secure; the date, however, carries a genuine philological caveat that responsible scholarship has always acknowledged: the final characters are damaged, and some doubt about the exact reading persists, so that the panel is generally cited as of 1342 but occasionally proposed as of 1344. The prudent position is that it is signed and datable to the early-to-mid 1340s, with 1342 the most probable reading. This work, popularly called the Madonna del cardellino from the bird clasped in the Child's hand, is the foundation of our knowledge of Jacopo's early manner.

The panel presents the Virgin at half length beneath a gabled profile, her deep blue-black mantle drawn up over her head and falling in broad, unbroken planes over a crimson robe, so that the two figures emerge from the burnished gold ground with an almost heraldic clarity. She supports the Child on her left arm, her right hand pressed gently against his side in the tender, physically credible gesture that betrays the painter's Lorenzettian formation; her head inclines toward him, though her narrowed, elongated eyes turn pensively past him toward the viewer's space.

The Child, wrapped in a rose-pink cloth shot with delicate star ornament over his bare torso, twists in her arms with lively naturalism, one foot swinging free of the drapery. In his left hand he grasps a dark bird by its wings — the bird that gives the painting its popular name — while on his chest hangs a sprig of red coral, the protective amulet of Trecento childhood and, by long association, a prefiguration of the Passion.

The two motifs together give the image its quiet doctrinal charge: the bird and the blood-red coral fold the destiny of the Cross into a scene of maternal intimacy. The haloes are richly punched with rosette and foliate motifs, that of the Virgin ringed by a broad ornamental band, and the gold ground is further worked with a punched arcade following the gable — a decorative refinement consistent with the sophisticated punchwork culture of mid-Trecento Siena that Frinta's technical studies placed at the center of the attributional debate around Jacopo. Along the base runs the painted inscription in red capitals carrying the signature and the partly abraded date. The elaborate wrought-iron Gothic-revival mount enclosing the panel is a later devotional framing, not original to the work.

### Mystic Coronation of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, with Saints Mary Magdalene, Lucy, and Agnes, between Saints Anthony Abbot and Michael the Archangel

  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-02.jpg" alt="Mystic Coronation of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, with Saints Mary Magdalene, Lucy, and Agnes, between Saints Anthony Abbot and Michael the Archangel">
  <figcaption>Mystic Coronation of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, with Saints Mary Magdalene, Lucy, and Agnes, between Saints Anthony Abbot and Michael the Archangel, 1362, tempera and gold on panel, Siena, Pinacoteca Nazionale.</figcaption>
</figure>

The second fixed point is a triptych, signed and dated 1362, representing the mystic Coronation of Saint Catherine of Alexandria — the Child, from the Virgin's lap, places a crown upon the kneeling saint's head — accompanied in the central panel by Saints Mary Magdalene, Lucy, and Agnes, and flanked on the wings by Saints Anthony Abbot and Michael the Archangel. (The DBI describes the scene as the *Sposalizio*, the Mystic Marriage; the cataloguing tradition and the iconography itself favour the cognate scene of the Coronation.)

It bears the painter's signature and the date 1362:

> Jacobus Mini de Senis me pinxit MCCCLXII tempore presbiteri Mathei rectoris Sancti Antonius.

The panel came from the church of Sant'Antonio Abate in Fontebranda in Siena — a building demolished in 1940 — and is now in the Pinacoteca Nazionale di Siena (inv. no. 145). Between the two signed works lies a span of twenty years, and the stylistic distance between them is the principal evidence for the claim, discussed below, that Jacopo's manner changed markedly over his career.

The triptych retains its full carpentry of gabled panels and crocketed pinnacles, and it announces at once the neo-martinian phase of Jacopo's maturity. In the broad central panel the Virgin, monumental in her deep blue mantle over a gold-brocaded robe, is seated before a cloth of honour worked in a punched diaper pattern; the Child, twisting from her lap in violet drapery, leans outward to set a crown upon the bowed head of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, who kneels at the right in a sumptuously ornamented rose gown, her hands crossed on her breast in acceptance. The scene is the mystic *coronation* of the saint — the rarer pendant to her mystic marriage, and like it a scene of glorification: having accepted her as his bride, Christ crowns her.

Around this nucleus stand three further virgin saints and holy women, identified in the cataloguing tradition as Mary Magdalene, in the rose mantle at the lower left, and, behind, Lucy and Agnes, the latter recognizable by the lamb perched at her shoulder; pairs of angels' heads cluster in the cusps above. On the left wing Saint Anthony Abbot, patron of the church for which the work was made, stands gaunt and grey-bearded in the dark habit of the hermits, tau staff in one hand and a red-bound book in the other — a figure of austere Lorenzettian gravity amid the courtly refinement of the rest.

On the right wing the Archangel Michael appears in golden armour and flame-orange mantle, the scales of judgment in one hand, the defeated serpent writhing beneath his feet. In the pinnacles, Christ the Redeemer blesses from the central gable amid further half-length saints and prophets. The whole ensemble — the elongated, small-mouthed faces with their heavy-lidded eyes, the calligraphic play of hems and mantle-edges, the profusion of punched and chrysographed ornament — declares that deliberate recovery of Simone Martini's elegance which De Benedictis identified as Jacopo's decisive contribution to Sienese painting after mid-century, and which stands at the furthest remove from the sturdier naturalism of the Sarteano Madonna of twenty years earlier.

It is on the basis of these two panels and the numerous documents that the whole modern effort to reconstruct the artist's career has proceeded. The chain of scholarship—Perkins in 1930, [Van Marle](/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond) in 1934, [Carli](/scholars.html#carli-enzo) in 1955, and their successors—took these two anchors as its calibration points. The essential methodological fact to keep in view is this: everything beyond the two signed panels is attribution, and attribution in Trecento Siena is a notoriously unstable business.

Formation and Stylistic Orientation: Lorenzetti, Gaddi, and the Return to Simone Martini

The question of Jacopo's artistic formation is one on which the documents are silent and the scholarship is inferential. Nothing is recorded about his apprenticeship. What can be said proceeds entirely from analysis of the works, and it is complicated by the fact—stressed by every serious student of the painter—that his manner underwent pronounced changes across his career.

The consensus of modern scholarship, as crystallized in the entries of De Benedictis and Magister, runs roughly as follows. The early works are strongly marked by the style of the Lorenzetti, and of Ambrogio in particular, within whose ambit Jacopo may have formed. For this first phase a collaboration or at least a close proximity to Taddeo Gaddi has also been proposed—the Florentine master's manner, it is argued, remains detectable in Jacopo's work as late as the end of the 1350s. The works of the 1360s and after, by contrast, show a decisive reorientation toward the manner of Simone Martini (and of Lippo Memmi), from whom Jacopo took, and developed with some originality, a pronounced decorative sensibility.

This last point—the "return to Simone Martini"—is the single most important claim made for Jacopo's historical significance, and it must be stated with care, because it is the point at which documented fact, consensus, and interpretive hypothesis are most easily confused. As a description of Jacopo's later style, the martinian orientation commands broad agreement. As a claim about his historical role, it is an interpretation, and one associated especially with De Benedictis, who argued that Jacopo's importance lies essentially in the new direction he expressed and exercised over local painting in the second half of the Trecento: moving away from Lorenzettian models, he inaugurated—well before Francesco di Vannuccio, Niccolò di Buonaccorso, and Paolo di Giovanni Fei—the intentional, considered recovery of the art of Simone Martini that would characterize Sienese painting down to the early decades of the fifteenth century.

This is a substantial and attractive thesis, and it is worth registering both its force and its limits. Its force is that it gives Jacopo a genuine art-historical function: not merely a competent survivor of the pre-plague masters, but a hinge-figure who helped set the course of neo-martinian revival that runs through the later Trecento into the age of Taddeo di Bartolo and beyond. Its limit is that it rests on a stylistic reading of a reconstructed corpus, and its chronological priority ("well before" the younger neo-martinians) depends on dating attributed works whose dates are themselves inferred. It should be presented, therefore, as the leading scholarly interpretation of Jacopo's significance, not as an established fact of the same order as his guild inscriptions. The reader should also be wary of the flat guidebook formula that Jacopo was simply "a follower of Simone Martini." The truth advanced by the best scholarship is more interesting and more precise: his early work is Lorenzettian and Gaddesque, and the martinian element is a later, deliberate, and arguably programmatic recovery rather than a formation.

### The Pisan Keystone: The Founders of the Religious Orders in San Francesco at Pisa

No work is more consequential for the reconstruction of Jacopo di Mino's early career than the fresco cycle of the founders of the religious orders in the vault webs (vele) of the cappella maggiore—the choir chapel—of San Francesco at Pisa, and none illustrates more vividly the tangled paths by which Trecento attributions travel. This is the eponymous work of the "Maestro degli Ordini," the anonymous personality around which Roberto Longhi assembled a corpus in 1955; and yet for four centuries the frescoes circulated under another painter's name entirely, on the authority of the most influential art-historical text ever written. The chapel stood under the patronage of the Gambacorta, one of the great families of Trecento Pisa, and its decoration belongs to a campaign that the sources fix, with unusual precision, to the year 1342.

The authority in question is Vasari, who in the second edition of the Vite (1568) records that Taddeo Gaddi was brought to Pisa, where in San Francesco, for Gherardo and Buonaccorso Gambacorti, he frescoed the cappella maggiore with stories of Saints Francis, Andrew, and Nicholas, portraying himself in profile with a hood wound about his head; and Vasari transcribes the inscription he read at the foot of one scene:

> Magister Taddeus Gaddus de Florentia pinxit hanc historiam Sancti Francisci et Sancti Andreae et Sancti Nicolai, anno Domini MCCCXLII, de mense augusti

"Master Taddeo Gaddi of Florence painted this story of Saint Francis and Saint Andrew and Saint Nicholas, in the year of the Lord 1342, in the month of August".

The inscription itself is lost; Vasari's transcription is our only witness to it. It is worth pausing on what this document actually establishes: that Taddeo Gaddi signed and dated work in the chapel in August 1342—not that every painting now visible there is his.

Gaddi's presence in Pisa in that year is independently corroborated by a remarkable private document: an autograph letter from the painter to Tommaso degli Strozzi in Florence, preserved in the Archivio di Stato of Florence (Carte Strozzi-Uguccioni, ser. 3, Filza 103, c. 8). The letter bears only the day, 7 September, without a year; but weighed against Vasari's testimony, 1342 is by far the most plausible date.

In it Taddeo writes:

> Tomaso, Taddeo dipintore tuo, da Pisa. Renditi sicuro che solo per onore avese io voglio dipignere la tavola e renditi sicuro che così sarà. Onde maestro Paulo e voi e luj la fate fare di legname al nome di Dio. E io tosto avrò conpiuto il lauoro de' Ganbacortj e cosi dela tavola detta farò. In conclusione io farò ciò che il Maestro Paulo mi dirà e cosi di voj. Dio sia guardia di tuttj. Dì vij di settembre.

"Tommaso, your painter Taddeo, from Pisa. Be assured that only for honour's sake do I wish to paint the panel, and be assured it shall be so. So let master Paolo and you and him have the woodwork made, in God's name. And I shall soon have finished the work of the Gambacorta, and then shall do the same for the said panel. In conclusion, I shall do whatever master Paolo tells me, and you likewise. God keep you all. The 7th day of September."

The phrase "il lauoro de' Ganbacortj", the work of the Gambacorta, points directly to the family chapel in San Francesco, and shows the master, writing in his own hand, on the point of completing it.

The subsequent fate of the chapel's decoration explains why so celebrated a campaign is today almost invisible. The wall frescoes described by Vasari perished, largely under whitewash; Gaetano Milanesi, annotating his edition of the Vite in 1878, recorded the situation with his customary exactness: the paintings "are now covered with white, except however those of the vault, representing in beautiful and grandiose figures the first founders of the religious Orders."

Only the vault webs, then, survive, the Founders of the Orders that give the anonymous master his name, high, dimly lit, and to this day barely photographed, so that the cycle exists in the scholarly literature far more vividly than in any accessible reproduction. The reader who searches for images of it and finds almost nothing has not misunderstood the bibliography; the monument itself is elusive, and much of what circulates online about the church's Trecento frescoes remains filed, in the old Vasarian manner, under Taddeo Gaddi.

It was precisely this Vasarian shadow that the twentieth-century connoisseurship had to dispel. Longhi, in "Il Maestro degli Ordini" (1955), recognized that the surviving vault frescoes could not be reconciled with Taddeo Gaddi's manner and made them the calibration point of an anonymous personality; [Pier Paolo Donati](/scholars.html#donati-pier-paolo) enlarged the grouping in 1968; and Luciano Bellosi, in the decisive article of 1972 (p. 73), identified the Master with the documented Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio, an attribution accepted in the standard modern treatment of the Pisan frescoes by [Mariagiulia Burresi](/scholars.html#burresi-mariagiulia) and [Antonino Caleca](/scholars.html#caleca-antonino) in Affreschi medievali a Pisa (2003).

The resolution is elegant because it discards nothing: Gaddi truly was in Pisa in 1342, truly worked on the Gambacorta chapel, and truly signed there—the letter and the transcribed inscription stand; but the vele that survive are by another, younger hand, that of a Sienese painter working in the same chapel and, evidently, at close quarters with the Florentine master. Here lies the most economical explanation of the pronounced Gaddesque strain that all critics detect in Jacopo's early manner: not a distant echo, but the trace of a shared scaffolding.

The epistemic layering of the case should nevertheless be stated plainly, for it is the foundation on which the whole early corpus rests. What is documented is Gaddi's activity for the Gambacorta (the letter, whose year is itself an inference) and the existence, once, of his signed and dated inscription of August 1342 (known solely through Vasari). What is scholarly consensus is that the surviving vault frescoes are not Gaddi's but Jacopo's—Bellosi's attribution, now standard.

What remains inference is the extension of the 1342 date to the vele themselves: it is reasonable, since vault and walls plausibly belong to one campaign, but the date attaches, strictly speaking, to a lost inscription beneath a lost wall scene. The cycle thus occupies a peculiar and instructive position in Jacopo's catalogue: chronologically the firmest anchor of his early activity, and yet a work whose date is borrowed from another painter's signature—a fitting emblem for an artist whose entire œuvre has had to be recovered from beneath other men's names.

## The "Maestro degli Ordini": Longhi, Donati, and the Anonymous Personality

To understand how Jacopo's corpus was assembled, one must follow a detour through a famous piece of connoisseurial reconstruction that, for some seventeen years, ran on a separate track from the documented name.

From the Pisan vault frescoes just described, an anonymous artistic personality was constructed and christened the "Maestro degli Ordini" (Master of the Orders). The decisive step was taken by Roberto Longhi, whose essay "Il Maestro degli Ordini" appeared in Paragone in 1955. Longhi, the most influential Italian connoisseur of the twentieth century, gathered around the Pisan frescoes a nucleus of stylistically related works and defined the Master as one of the significant anonymous figures of central-Italian Trecento painting. Longhi's engagement with this material was longstanding: a drawing by his hand, faithfully reproducing the San Francesco figure from the choir (a work later transferred to the Museo Diocesano di San Miniato), may date as early as the 1920s, well before the published essay.

Longhi's nucleus was subsequently enlarged by Pier Paolo Donati, whose "Aggiunte al 'Maestro degli Ordini'" appeared, again in Paragone, in 1968. By the end of the 1960s, then, the "Maestro degli Ordini" existed as a reasonably well-defined connoisseurial construct—an anonymous but coherent artistic personality—entirely separate, in the literature, from the documented figure of Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio, who was known chiefly from Milanesi's documents and from the two signed panels.

The importance of the Longhi–Donati reconstruction for the present subject is twofold. First, it is a model instance of the method by which the study of Trecento painting proceeds when documents fail: an eponymous work (here the dated Pisan frescoes) becomes the calibration point for a stylistic grouping. Second, the Vasarian attribution to Taddeo Gaddi, examined in the previous section, remained the default of the literature for four centuries—a red herring that still governs much of what circulates about the church, and one that should be corrected wherever it recurs.

## Bellosi 1972: The Identification and Its Consequences

The reconstruction reached its decisive turning point in 1972, with Luciano Bellosi's article "Giacomo di Mino del Pellicciaio," published in the *Bollettino d'arte*. Bellosi—a scholar who would become one of the leading authorities on Sienese and Tuscan painting of the period—proposed that the anonymous "Maestro degli Ordini" of Longhi and Donati was none other than the documented Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio. In a single stroke, this identification married the connoisseurial construct to the archival name: the body of work that Longhi had assembled around the Pisan frescoes could now be understood as the early production of a painter about whose life and civic career a great deal was independently known.

The consequences of Bellosi's proposal were far-reaching, and it is fair to describe it as the foundational act of the modern understanding of the artist. It transformed Jacopo from a name attached to two panels into a painter with a substantial, if debated, early œuvre; it gave the 1342 Pisan frescoes—if the identification holds—a documented author; and it reframed the whole question of Jacopo's development, since the "Maestro degli Ordini" material represented precisely the Lorenzettian-Gaddesque early phase that the signed 1362 triptych, with its martinian orientation, seemed to have left behind. The identification was quickly registered by the scholarly community: [Alessandro Conti](/scholars.html#conti-alessandro) reviewed the article in the *Annali della Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa* the following year, a sign of the attention it commanded.

Bellosi's identification is now the reigning scholarly consensus, and it is adopted by the standard reference works. But it is important, in the spirit of epistemic honesty, to be clear about its status. It is a connoisseurial hypothesis of a high order, accepted by most specialists, but it is not a documented equation. The name "Maestro degli Ordini" survives in the literature precisely because it designates a stylistic construct whose identity with the documented Jacopo, however persuasive, rests on visual argument rather than on an archival tie between the Pisan frescoes and Jacopo's name. The prudent formulation—and the one adopted here—is that the identification is the accepted working hypothesis of the field, embraced by Bellosi's successors, rather than a fact on the same footing as the two signatures.

It was also Bellosi who advanced the estimate of Jacopo's quality that has become standard. As the Museo Civico–Pinacoteca Crociani at Montepulciano records, the *Coronation of the Virgin* is "ritenuta dal Bellosi l'opera più alta del pittore senese"—held by Bellosi to be the highest work of the Sienese painter. That judgment, too, is a matter of critical evaluation rather than documentary fact, but it has proved durable, and it is echoed in the museum's own presentation of the work.

## The Coronation of the Virgin at Montepulciano: A Case Study in Attributional History

### Coronation of the Virgin with Eleven Angels

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-03.jpg" alt="Coronation of the Virgin with Eleven Angels">
  <figcaption>Coronation of the Virgin with Eleven Angels, central compartment of a dismembered polyptych, tempera and punched gold leaf on panel, mid-fourteenth century, Montepulciano, Museo Civico–Pinacoteca Crociani (Palazzo Neri Orselli).</figcaption>
</figure>

No single work illustrates the vicissitudes of Jacopo's critical fortune better than the great panel of the Coronation of the Virgin now in the Museo Civico–Pinacoteca Crociani at Montepulciano. Its attributional history is a compressed history of Sienese connoisseurship itself, and it deserves detailed treatment.

The panel — the central compartment of what was once a larger polyptych, and admired for its retinue of music-making angels — came from the oratory of the Arciconfraternita della Misericordia at Montepulciano, where [Francesco Brogi](/scholars.html#brogi-francesco) recorded it in the Cappella della Concezione as a work in the manner of Simone Martini. It was documented in the salone of the Museo Civico, then housed in the Palazzo Comunale, from 1909, and is today displayed in the museum's present home in the Palazzo Neri Orselli. No documented date survives: the museum's catalogue places it in 1350–1360, while the Dizionario Biografico groups it with the martinian works of the painter's later decades — a divergence that itself bears on the disputed chronology of Jacopo's stylistic turn.

The panel's attributional journey is remarkable. It was assigned by [Bernard Berenson](/scholars.html#berenson-bernard) to Spinello Aretino, with the figures of the angels given to Luca di Tommè in collaboration. It was attributed by both Longhi and [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico) to the Lucchese painter Angelo Puccinelli — an opinion fossilized in the Soprintendenza's photographic archive, where prints of the panel remain captioned under Puccinelli's name. Only later was it "restored" to the Sienese Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio by Bellosi — reviving, as the museum's own catalogue notes, an attribution "ipotesi già da tempo avanzata dal Perkins e ripresa successivamente dal [Previtali](/scholars.html#previtali-giovanni)" (a hypothesis long before advanced by Frederick Mason Perkins and subsequently taken up by Giovanni Previtali). That a single panel could be attributed, by serious scholars, to a Florentine (Spinello), a Lucchese (Puccinelli), and a Sienese (Jacopo) — with a portion given to yet a fourth hand (Luca di Tommè) — is a vivid demonstration of how unstable attributions in this period can be, and of how much the "reconstruction" of a painter like Jacopo depends on the shifting judgments of connoisseurs.

Within its round-headed profile the panel stages the Coronation as a scene of consummate courtly ceremony. Christ and the Virgin are seated side by side on a long marble bench inlaid with cosmatesque ornament, before a cloth of honour whose gold-and-blue lozenge diaper fills the field with textile splendour; beneath them a polychrome tiled pavement recedes in confident perspective. Christ, in a vermilion robe crossed by richly ornamented bands and a dark mantle — now sadly abraded, with extensive losses that expose the ground — reaches across with both hands to set the jewelled crown upon the head of the Virgin, who bows beneath it, hands crossed on her breast, in a pale mantle shot with gold over a rose gown gathered by an embroidered sash.

The gravity of the two protagonists is enveloped by the retinue that has always been the panel's glory: seven angels form an arc around the upper register, three at the apex and two pairs in the spandrels, their heads inclined in adoring symmetry, while at the foot of the throne kneel the four music-making angels of the museum's delighted "complessino" — one with a portative organ crowned, remarkably, by miniature Gothic pinnacles, another bowing a vielle, the others with harp and psaltery.

The whole is executed with the profusion of punched gold, chrysography, and ornamental patterning that defines Jacopo's mature, neo-martinian manner: the elongated oval faces, heavy-lidded eyes, and calligraphic drapery rhythms consciously revive the elegance of Simone Martini, translated into the more densely decorated idiom of the post-plague decades. It is not difficult to see why Bellosi judged this the painter's highest achievement — nor why, before him, connoisseurs of the first rank could read its refinement in such divergent directions.

The current attribution to Jacopo commands wide acceptance, and the museum presents the work as the painter's masterpiece, a judgment inherited from Bellosi. But the history just sketched should induce caution. The panel is an attribution, not a signed work; its assignment to Jacopo is a scholarly consensus of relatively recent date, reached after more than half a century of disagreement; and its status as his "highest" achievement is an evaluative claim. All three registers — attribution, consensus, and evaluation — should be kept distinct. What the panel does demonstrate securely is the survival, in the local painting of the later Trecento, of the refined style of Simone Martini, and the role played by Jacopo in the diffusion of that neo-martinian idiom.

## The Madonna 'Belverde' and the Perils of Documentary Attribution

### Madonna 'Belverde' — Madonna and Child Enthroned between Two Angels

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-04.jpg" alt="Coronation of the Virgin with Eleven Angels">
  <figcaption>Taddeo di Bartolo (formerly attributed to Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio), Madonna 'Belverde' — Madonna and Child Enthroned between Two Angels, c. 1405, tempera on gold ground on panel, 150.7 × 65.7 cm, Siena, Basilica di Santa Maria dei Servi.</figcaption>
</figure>

If the Montepulciano *Coronation* illustrates the instability of purely stylistic attribution, the case of the so-called Madonna 'Belverde' in the Sienese church of Santa Maria dei Servi (the Basilica di San Clemente) illustrates an even subtler danger: the danger of over-reading a document. It is a case that repays close attention, because it has been the source of a persistent and instructive error.

The facts are these. The will of Francesco di Guglielmaccio Petroni, drawn up in 1363, refers to a panel

> quam facere debet magister Jacobus pictor

"which master Jacopo the painter is to make". On the strength of this document, and of a tradition going back to the early seventeenth century, the venerated Madonna 'Belverde' (also called 'Belvedere') in Santa Maria dei Servi was long attributed to Jacopo di Mino, and was cited as a third work securely his on documentary grounds.

The Restituzioni conservation campaign ([Solberg](/scholars.html#solberg-gail)'s scheda, Restituzioni 2018) recovered the full splendour of this surface, and its radiographs yielded a discovery of real art-historical consequence: traces of a Gothic arch above the Virgin's head, indicating that the panel — now cut to a rectangular, icon-like format — originally rose to a pointed profile and may have formed the centre of a polyptych comparable in structure to Jacopo di Mino's signed triptych in the Pinacoteca.

The restorers' second discovery was decisive for the attribution: at the lower right, the fragmentary remains of an inscription ending in "eus," logically the close of "Tadeus" — Taddeo di Bartolo's signature. The physiognomies confirm what the letters imply: the broad, softly modelled faces with their small mouths and level gazes, the sturdy blessing Child, the sweetened elegance of the whole belong to the generation after Jacopo, to the neo-martinian tradition as Taddeo received and transformed it around 1405. The image became an object of particular cult in the early sixteenth century, and copies were produced for private devotion down to the nineteenth — a fortuna that long shielded the traditional attribution from scrutiny. Therefore, the 1363 mandate to Jacopo, in other words, "evidently was not carried out."

Two further points worth having for the essay. First, the scheda adds a nuance our Belverde section can absorb: Perkins had already observed in 1930 that the present panel is a remaking or replacement of an older venerated image called the 'Belvedere' — hence the significance of the double name, 'Belverde' versus 'Belvedere' (the latter is Milanesi's form), which are not mere variants but may distinguish the c. 1405 panel from its lost predecessor. Second, a small discrepancy to be aware of: the Restituzioni web summary refers to Jacopo's Pinacoteca triptych as of "1363," where Treccani and the standard literature give 1362 — almost certainly a slip in the web text, but worth noting since we cite both.

The essay's Belverde section otherwise stands confirmed in every particular — the 1363 Petroni will, the post-1390 chapel, the c. 1405 dating, the "(Tad)eus" discovery, and Solberg's explicit description of Jacopo as Taddeo's master. With the dimensions now secured, I can update that section of the artifact and fold in the three corrected passages from our previous exchanges, if you'd like everything consolidated in one pass.

This case has three implications for the study of Jacopo. First, it removes from his secure catalogue a work that older literature confidently included, and it is precisely the kind of correction that a rigorous account must register. Second, it dramatizes the methodological lesson that a document commissioning a work is not the same as evidence that the work was executed, still less that a given surviving object is that work. Third, and here the case opens onto the larger question of Jacopo's legacy, the transfer of the Petroni commission from the older Jacopo to the younger Taddeo di Bartolo is entirely consistent with the master–pupil relationship that the scholarship posits between the two men, of which more below. The Treccani *Dizionario Biografico* is admirably candid on the point: the present state of the 'Belverde' panel is the product of heavy repainting at the end of the Trecento by a painter identifiable with, or close to, Taddeo di Bartolo, and it therefore no longer permits Jacopo's authorship to be recognized in it.

## The Battistero Drawing: Another Corrected Error

A second instructive correction concerns a supposed connection between Jacopo and the design of the façade of the baptistery of San Giovanni in Siena. In 1382 Jacopo was paid

> per uno disegniamento che diè a l'Uopera della facciata di S. Giovanni

"for a drawing that he gave to the Opera for the façade of San Giovanni". From this payment it was once inferred that Jacopo was the designer, or at least the draftsman, of the baptistery façade.

Modern scholarship rejects this inference. The surviving parchment design for the baptistery façade, now in the Museo dell'Opera della Metropolitana in Siena, is datable on stylistic grounds to the first half of the Trecento—around the 1330s–1340s—and is attributable to the circle of the Agostino (that is, the ambit of Agostino di Giovanni and related sculptor-architects). The consensus, articulated in the technical literature on the drawing and in the reference entries, is that Jacopo was probably not its author, nor the designer of the façade, but merely the person who possessed the older drawing and handed it over to the Opera in 1382. The 1382 payment is thus genuine documentary evidence of Jacopo's involvement in the cathedral works, but not evidence that he designed the baptistery façade. This is another instance in which a real document has been made to say more than it does, and in which careful scholarship has had to peel back an over-interpretation.

## The Early Corpus: Umbria, the Contado, and the Franciscan Milieu

Beyond the two signed panels and the disputed masterpieces, the reconstructed early corpus of Jacopo—largely the material once gathered under the "Maestro degli Ordini" and expanded since—clusters geographically in Pisa, the southern Sienese contado, and Umbria, and it is bound up conspicuously with the Franciscan order. This concentration is itself significant: it suggests a painter whose early career was oriented as much toward the towns of the Sienese state and the neighboring Umbrian centers as toward the metropolis, and whose patronage was substantially mendicant.

### Madonna and Child with Saints John the Baptist and John the Evangelist

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-05.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child with Saints John the Baptist and John the Evangelist">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child with Saints John the Baptist and John the Evangelist; in the crownings, the Annunciation, Paired Apostles, and Angels, c. 1340s, tempera and gold leaf on panel, church of San Francesco at Sarteano.</figcaption>
</figure>

The triptych presents itself as a compact aedicular ensemble: three gabled panels, each crowned by its own stepped entablature-like cornice — a carpentry whose somewhat anomalous, architectonic character suggests reworking or remounting at a later date, though only direct examination could confirm it. In the central panel the Virgin, in a deep blue mantle drawn over a white veil and crimson robe, holds the Child on her right arm; he reaches up with both hands to clutch the edge of her veil at the breast — a gesture of instinctive, almost grasping infantile intimacy that belongs squarely to the Lorenzettian repertory of maternal naturalism, and that links this panel closely to the signed *Madonna del cardellino* of 1342 painted for the same town.

Four angels' heads incline in adoration from the upper corners of the gold ground, beneath a punched arch. On the left wing stands John the Baptist, gaunt and dark-bearded in his camel-hair tunic and violet mantle, pointing across toward the central group while his unfurled scroll carries the Ecce Agnus Dei; on the right, his youthful counterpart John the Evangelist, round-faced and auburn-haired in blue and rose, holds a red-bound book. The pairing of the two Johns — Precursor and Beloved Disciple, penitential witness and contemplative theologian — flanking the Incarnation is given its narrative key in the compartment above the central panel, where the Annunciation unfolds under a triple arcade, Gabriel striding in from the left toward the seated Virgin, a red seraph's head glowing in the gable above.

The lateral crownings each carry a pair of half-length apostles under twin arches, with further angels' heads in the spandrels. Stylistically the ensemble speaks the language of Jacopo's first manner: the fuller, rounder facial types, the sturdy Child, the sober drapery construction, still close to Ambrogio Lorenzetti and to the Gaddesque strain of the Pisan frescoes, and at the furthest remove from the attenuated martinian elegance of the 1362 triptych. Its Franciscan destination places it within that dense cluster of mendicant commissions — Sarteano, Asciano, Cortona, Città della Pieve — that Bellosi recognized as the core of Jacopo's intense activity in the 1340s.

...

Two works in this early group warrant particular comment. The first is the frescoed *Ascent of Souls from Purgatory into Paradise* in the former church of San Francesco at Todi—an image often celebrated as among the earliest pictorial representations of Purgatory, and connected iconographically with the legend of Saint Patrick's Purgatory and with the intercession of Saint Philip Benizi, the Servite. This fresco, which bears the date 1346 (the last figures are lost to a fall of plaster, but archival evidence confirms the reading), was discovered only in 1975 during restoration work in what is now the choir of the Poor Clares, and it was attributed to Jacopo by Federico Zeri, a proposal reported and developed by [Grondona](/scholars.html#grondona-marco) in a 1986 study in the *Studi storici dell'Ordine dei servi di Maria*. Here the epistemic layering must be exact. The date (1346) is documented; the discovery (1975) is fact; the attribution to Jacopo is a proposal, associated with Zeri, and it is by no means unanimous. Other scholars have preferred the name of Andrea Vanni, and more recently the fresco has been associated with a local painter; the work is sometimes discussed under the provisional label "Maestro del Purgatorio di Todi." The responsible statement is that the Todi Purgatory is a securely dated Sienese-ambit fresco of 1346 whose attribution to Jacopo, though seriously advanced, remains genuinely open.

The second work meriting comment is the group of frescoes at Sarteano and Asciano associated with the Franciscans. At Sarteano, in addition to the signed 1342 *Madonna*, Jacopo (or his workshop) is credited with the San Francesco triptych and *Annunciation*; at Asciano, in the church of San Francesco, frescoes of Saints Bartholomew and Barbara are given to him. As Bellosi observed, Jacopo's activity was especially intense precisely in the 1340s, when the master was closely tied to the Franciscan milieu. The mendicant orientation of this early work is one of the more securely established features of his early career, even where individual attributions remain debatable.

### The Annunciation: The Archangel Gabriel and the Virgin Annunciate

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-06.jpg" alt="The Annunciation: The Archangel Gabriel and the Virgin Annunciate">
  <figcaption>The Annunciation: The Archangel Gabriel and the Virgin Annunciate, devotional diptych, undated, tempera and gold on panel, Fiesole, Museo Bandini.</figcaption>
</figure>

The two small panels, each in its engaged gilt moulding, form a portable diptych of the Annunciation divided across the physical gap between them — a format in which the very space between the leaves becomes the charged interval that the angelic salutation must cross. On the left leaf Gabriel alights on one knee in pure profile, his great wing still raised from flight, clad in a grey-blue patterned tunic beneath a mantle of orange-gold damask whose hem whips behind him with the momentum of his arrival; his right hand rises in address, while his left holds out a dark leafy branch that reads as the olive of Sienese usage — the emblem, since Simone Martini's Uffizi altarpiece of 1333, of the peace announced to Mary, and a deliberately Sienese alternative to the Florentine lily.

On the right leaf the Virgin receives the message seated on a bench ornamented with cosmatesque intarsia, before a gold-brocaded cloth of honour; a lectern at her side carries the open book of her interrupted reading, its lines of script minutely rendered, while from the upper corner the dove of the Holy Spirit plunges toward her on red-gold wings. Her response is the gesture of humble acceptance: head bowed beneath its punched halo, arms crossed upon her breast, the deep blue mantle pooling about her in long calligraphic folds. Everything in the pair — the profile elegance of the angel, the recoiling modesty of the Virgin, the sumptuous patterning of textiles and punched borders, the gold ground framed by continuous rosette bands — declares the neo-martinian idiom at its most concentrated: Simone's great public Annunciation translated into the intimate register of private devotion. It is exactly the kind of object through which the martinian revival that the scholarship credits to Jacopo circulated beyond the altarpiece into the household.

Here there is a genuine divergence worth registering. The label under which the photograph circulates gives "1342–50 ca.," but Magister's DBI entry places the diptych explicitly in Jacopo's later phase — the "settimo-nono decennio," the 1360s–1380s, the years of the martinian revival — and the style visible in your photograph (the attenuated profiles, the olive-branch iconography, the decorative saturation) sits far more comfortably there than beside the robust Sarteano Madonna of 1342.

- The attributional nugget

Perkins attributed the "piccolo dittico" to Niccolò di Buonaccorso in 1918, and it was Bellosi, in the same 1972 article that founded the modern Jacopo, who took it from Niccolò and restored it to Jacopo di Mino — with [Marcucci](/scholars.html#marcucci-luisa) (1965) having discussed the panels in between. So the Fiesole Annunciation joins Montepulciano, Perugia, and the Pinacoteca polyptych in the gallery of works that reached Jacopo's catalogue only by displacing another master's name — and, pleasingly, the displaced master here is Niccolò di Buonaccorso, one of the very neo-martinians whom De Benedictis says Jacopo *anticipated*. The confusion between them is itself indirect evidence of the thesis.

### Coronation of the Virgin with Music-Making Angels; below, Scenes of the Martyrdom of a Female Saint (Victoria?)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-07.jpg" alt="Coronation of the Virgin with Music-Making Angels; below, Scenes of the Martyrdom of a Female Saint (Victoria?)">
  <figcaption>Coronation of the Virgin with Music-Making Angels; below, Scenes of the Martyrdom of a Female Saint (Victoria?), fresco ensemble from the ruined pieve of Santa Vittoria (?), Sarteano, church of San Francesco.</figcaption>
</figure>

Within a walled-up arch whose exposed brickwork bears witness to the wartime injuries and subsequent consolidations of the building, the lunette preserves a Coronation of the Virgin of pronounced courtly refinement. Christ, in a pale blue mantle sown with stars over a gold-banded tunic, turns to set the crown upon the Virgin, who inclines toward him with crossed hands, wrapped in a white mantle worked throughout with a delicate dark palmette brocade; behind the pair stretches a cloth of honour of flaming vermilion damask, and beneath their feet runs a pavement of cosmatesque inlay. Six angels attend the ceremony in symmetrical wings — at the left one bows a vielle while two clasp their hands in prayer; at the right two adore and one sounds a lute — their haloes forming twin constellations against the red ground.

An ornamental frieze of rosettes and lozenges separates the lunette from the lower register, of which three fragments survive amid the masonry: at the left a crowned ruler surrounded by soldiers whose spears bristle against the sky — the persecuting king of the passio; at the centre the martyr herself, her golden, rayed nimbus intact, while two angels swoop down bearing the crown and palms of martyrdom and a hand extends toward her the white disc of the Host — the miraculous viaticum of the legend; at the right, within an arched architectural setting, a further crowd of onlookers. The ensemble, for all its lacunae, speaks the decorative language of the later Trecento — the textile splendour, the elongated courtly figures, the music-making retinue that recalls, in fresco, the Montepulciano *Coronation*.

### Maestà (Madonna and Child Enthroned)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Jacopo-del-Pellicciaio/pellicciaio-08.jpg" alt="Maestà (Madonna and Child Enthroned)">
  <figcaption>Maestà (Madonna and Child Enthroned), detached fresco from the cloister of the convent of San Francesco, before 1396, Siena, Basilica di San Francesco, first transept chapel.</figcaption>
</figure>

Set within the black-and-white striped marble revetment of the basilica, the fresco presents the Virgin enthroned in strict frontality upon a throne of astonishing architectural ambition: a polychrome Gothic aedicule in white and red, its tall crocketed pinnacles and gabled canopy rising against a deep blue ground strewn with gilt stars, its arms and base inlaid with cosmatesque ornament and crowned by slender finials — less a seat than a miniature cathedral, translating into paint the language of Sienese marble furniture.

The Virgin's halo, gilded and incised with radiating lines, frames a face of grave, elongated elegance; her mantle, drawn up over her head and falling in a single monumental mass, now reads as a nearly unbroken dark field — the combined result, one presumes, of pigment alteration and of the heavy repainting that the scholarly literature explicitly records. On her left knee sits the Child in a pale rose patterned tunic, one hand raised toward his mouth, the other resting near a small rounded object — fruit or bird — now difficult to read with confidence.

The wide ornamental border that encloses the whole, with its candelabra of acanthus and vine on a red ground punctuated by cosmatesque lozenges, belongs plausibly to the re-presentation of the image after its transfer, in the spirit of the basilica's late nineteenth-century neo-Gothic restoration; it frames the medieval fragment like a relic. Even through the veil of its restorations, the conception retains real grandeur — the star-spangled ground, the architectural throne, the hieratic frontality — and its original setting in the conventual cloister places it within that Franciscan orbit which the documents and the reconstructed corpus alike identify as the constant of Jacopo's career, from the Pisan vele of 1342 to this, chronologically the open end of his catalogue.

The polyptych survives with its full carpentry — five gabled compartments under crocketed cusps, slender pinnacles, twisted colonnettes, and a continuous moulded base — so that it still reads as the miniature golden façade its makers intended. At the centre the Virgin, in a deep blue mantle open over a red robe, holds the Child wrapped in a gold-brocaded mantle; he clasps a small bird to his chest with one hand and with the other displays an unfurled cartellino inscribed

> Ego sum via, veritas et vita

"I am the way, the truth, and the life", so that the infant himself pronounces the Johannine text that the flanking saints embody. Above the central arch two pairs of adoring angels in blue lean inward against the gold. On the wings, an eloquently balanced mendicant program: at the far left Saint Clare, austere in her dark habit and veil, raises the gilded pyx of her Eucharistic legend; beside her the Baptist, gaunt in camel-hair beneath a blue-and-rose mantle, points across to the Child while his scroll carries the Ecce Agnus Dei — the gesture and the text converging on the "Lamb" whom the central cartellino answers. To the right of the Virgin stands Augustine in full episcopal splendour — jewelled cope, mitre, crozier, a morse worked with a holy face — and, at the far right, Francis, hooded, the stigmata legible on hands and side, a red-bound book in his grasp.

The pinnacles complete the theology of the whole: over the centre rises the Crucifixion, with the Virgin and the Evangelist flanking the cross and the Magdalene embracing its foot in a flame of red drapery; over the wings, paired half-length saints under smaller gables — Paul with his sword recognizable among them — with angel-heads in the spandrel medallions. The physiognomies, with their level, wide-set eyes and firm oval faces, and the sober monumentality of the standing figures give the ensemble a gravity that sits somewhere between the painter's Lorenzettian beginnings and his later decorative refinement — which is precisely, as we shall see, where the scholarship hesitates.

The polyptych in the Siena Pinacoteca is also worth pausing over, both because it is a substantial work now generally given to Jacopo and because its attribution, too, has fluctuated. Berenson at one point associated the polyptych with the hand of Martino di Bartolomeo di Biagio; other critics proposed still other names; and Federico Zeri's catalogue notes register the earlier attributions of Berenson (1932) and [Cesare Brandi](/scholars.html#brandi-cesare) (1933) before the work's consolidation in Jacopo's œuvre by Longhi and, subsequently, in [Torriti's](/scholars.html#torriti-piero) catalogue of the Pinacoteca (1977). The panel represents the *Madonna and Child with Saints Clare, John the Baptist, Augustine, and Francis*, with a *Crucifixion* group in the upper register—an iconographic program that again underscores the mendicant (here Franciscan and Augustinian) orientation of much of Jacopo's production.

The little triptych survives complete with its hinged wings and crocketed central gable, a portable altar whose modest scale belies the density of its programme. In the central panel the Virgin, seated in a mantle of deep blue-green sown with gold stars over a red robe, supports the Child on her knee as he leans across to slip the ring onto the finger of Saint Catherine — here the Mystic Marriage proper, ring and all, where the Siena triptych of 1362 gives the cognate Coronation.

Catherine, crowned and clad in a gold-and-blue brocade beneath a rose-violet mantle, bows to receive the pledge; on the other side stands the youthful Sant'Ansano, protector of Siena, in his red tunic, palm in one hand and his white pennant in the other — the Sienese signature within the composition, and the strongest internal evidence that the triptych was commissioned in Siena before its Umbrian migration. Around the group, against a cloth of honour rendered entirely in punched and incised gold, six angels incline in adoration.

The wings extend the litany of holy women: at the left Mary Magdalene, wrapped in red, presents her ointment jar; at the right the saint of the long identity dispute — sumptuous in a gold-embroidered gown, a flowering branch in one hand, a red-bound book in the other, and on her head the coronet of white roses that has tipped the modern cataloguing toward Elizabeth of Hungary. Above, the triptych folds the Annunciation across its architecture: Gabriel kneels in the left pinnacle, his scroll inscribed with the angelic salutation

> ave... gratia plena, dominus tecum,

the Virgin annunciate receives it in the right, and at the apex of the central gable the blessing Redeemer appears in a roundel. The elegance of the whole — the calligraphic hems, the saturation of punchwork, the courtly slenderness of the figures — places the object squarely in the martinian register of Jacopo's maturity, and its function as a portable devotional altar makes it, with the Fiesole diptych, a witness to how that idiom travelled.

The Perugia triptych deserves a note on its own attributional history, because it exemplifies the same fluidity seen elsewhere. It was published by [Francesco Santi](/scholars.html#santi-francesco) in 1969 with an attribution to Bartolo di Fredi; it was inserted into Jacopo's catalogue by Bellosi in 1972, who was in fact reviving a proposal already made by Millard Meiss in 1951; and the ascription to Jacopo was subsequently accepted by [Miklós Boskovits](/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos) (1973) and by De Benedictis (1979). The panel is recorded as having come from the convent of San Domenico at Perugia following the suppressions of ecclesiastical property that began in 1861. Here again the pattern is instructive: a work moves from one master's catalogue to another's on the strength of successive connoisseurial judgments, with the modern consensus settling—provisionally—on Jacopo.

## A Contested Addition: The Cappellone degli Spagnoli

One further addition to Jacopo's catalogue belongs firmly in the register of the contested rather than the accepted, and it is important to label it as such. In a 1996 article in *Critica d'arte*, [Serena Skerl Del Conte](/scholars.html#skerl-del-conte-serena) proposed to add to Jacopo's corpus certain passages, strongly Gaddesque in character, of the decoration of the vault and the western wall of the Cappellone degli Spagnoli (the Spanish Chapel, the former chapter house) of Santa Maria Novella in Florence, a campaign carried out between about 1355 and 1365, before the work passed to Andrea di Bonaiuto.

This is a bold proposal, and it should be treated with corresponding caution. The great fresco cycle of the Spanish Chapel is one of the canonical monuments of Florentine Trecento painting, overwhelmingly associated with Andrea di Bonaiuto (who frescoed it in the mid-1360s); to insert the hand of a Sienese painter into its earlier phase is a significant and inherently debatable claim.

The Treccani *Dizionario Biografico* reports the proposal neutrally, as a recent addition to the catalogue, without endorsing it. In the terms of this essay, the Skerl Del Conte attribution is a scholarly hypothesis, not a consensus; it should be recorded as such, and no weight should be placed on it in characterizing Jacopo's activity. That the proposal was made at all, however, is itself testimony to the perceived Gaddesque strain in Jacopo's early manner—the very quality that had led older tradition to attribute the Pisan "Maestro degli Ordini" frescoes to Taddeo Gaddi.

## Workshop, Patronage, and the Pupil Taddeo di Bartolo

The question of Jacopo's workshop and his role as a teacher brings us to one of the most consequential aspects of his career for the later history of Sienese painting: his relationship to Taddeo di Bartolo.

Taddeo di Bartolo (Siena, c. 1362–1422) was the leading Sienese painter of his generation and a crucial vehicle for the transmission of the Trecento tradition into the fifteenth century. The scholarship is clear that Taddeo's roots lie in the Sienese context of the preceding generation, and specifically in the ambit of Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio. The Treccani entries on Taddeo state that, although his precise training is undocumented, his artistic roots are to be sought in that milieu, and that it was "attraverso Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio"—through Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio—that Taddeo looked back to Simone Martini. Taddeo's earliest signed polyptych, of 1389, is described as revealing the influence of older Sienese painters such as Jacopo. The Restituzioni catalogue for the 'Belverde' panel goes further, describing Jacopo explicitly as "Jacopo, maestro di Taddeo di Bartolo"—Jacopo, master of Taddeo di Bartolo—and noting that the transfer of the Petroni commission from the older Jacopo to the younger Taddeo is entirely plausible given their master–pupil relationship.

Here, once more, the epistemic status of the claim must be fixed precisely. The proposition that Taddeo was formed in Jacopo's orbit is scholarly consensus, resting on stylistic filiation and on the internal logic of the Sienese milieu; it is not, however, documented by a surviving apprenticeship contract, and the standard biography of Taddeo is careful to say that "we do not know who his master was." The most defensible formulation is that Jacopo is very probably the decisive formative influence on Taddeo di Bartolo, and quite possibly his master in the literal sense—but that the relationship is inferred from style and circumstance rather than established by document. If the inference is correct, it gives Jacopo a historical importance that far exceeds the modest surviving remains of his own hand: he becomes the conduit through which the martinian revival passed to the painter who would carry it into the age of Sassetta.

Of Jacopo's workshop in the narrower sense—assistants, division of labor, replicas—little can be said with confidence, and this is a real gap in our knowledge. The documented collaborations offer the firmest evidence: with Paolo di maestro Neri in the cathedral in 1366, and with Bartolo di Fredi on the vault near the Sant'Ansano chapel in 1367–1368. These collaborations situate Jacopo within the network of shared and cooperative commissions that recent scholarship, noting the practice of a mid-century Sienese *compagnia* of painters, has identified as characteristic of the period after the Black Death. The catalogue records, further, works by followers and by the "ambit" of Jacopo—for example a *San Nicola da Tolentino* at Montalcino assigned to his ambit, and a *Beato Gerardo da Valenza* at Asciano given to a follower—which indicate the diffusion of his manner among lesser hands, even if the internal organization of his bottega escapes us.

## Post-Plague Siena and Jacopo's Place in It

Jacopo's mature career unfolded in the decades after the catastrophe of 1348, when the Black Death carried off perhaps half the population of Siena and killed, among many others, the greatest painters of the preceding generation—Ambrogio and Pietro Lorenzetti among them, in all probability. The interpretation of what this demographic and cultural rupture meant for painting has been one of the central debates of Trecento art history, and Jacopo's position within it deserves explicit treatment.

The classic account is that of Millard Meiss, whose *Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death* (Princeton, 1951) argued that the calamities of the mid-century were experienced as divine punishment and produced a retreat from the naturalistic, human-centered art of the early Trecento toward a more hieratic, abstract, and spiritually charged manner recalling the previous century. Meiss's thesis dominated the field for a generation. It has since been substantially qualified—by Miklós Boskovits and, importantly for our subject, by Cristina De Benedictis, whose *La pittura senese 1330–1370* (Florence, 1979) questioned the claim that a sharp stylistic change occurred at mid-century—and more recent scholarship has emphasized instead the artistic pluralism, the changing patterns of patronage, and the new collaborative workshop practices of the post-plague decades.

Jacopo di Mino belongs squarely to the generation that had to work in the shadow of this rupture, and his career can be read as a case study in the several available responses to it. His documented survival and prosperity—his guild eminence, his civic offices, his stream of commissions—mark him as one of the painters who successfully filled the vacuum left by the death of the great masters.

His stylistic trajectory, moreover, complicates any simple Meissian narrative: rather than retreating into archaism, he moved from a Lorenzettian-Gaddesque naturalism toward a considered revival of the elegant, courtly, decorative manner of Simone Martini—a "return," but to the most refined and worldly of the pre-plague masters rather than to a penitential Duecento hieraticism. If De Benedictis is right to see in Jacopo the initiator of the neo-martinian current, then his career is evidence against the strongest form of the Meiss thesis: not a flight from beauty and naturalism into abstraction, but a deliberate cultivation of the tradition's most graceful strand. Modern scholarship has come to place Jacopo, along with Bartolomeo Bulgarini, Niccolò di ser Sozzo, the Master of the Palazzo Venezia Madonna, Luca di Tommè, Naddo Ceccarelli, and Bartolo di Fredi, among the closely related painters who defined Sienese art in the third quarter of the century.

It was in this context that Jacopo's documented association with two of these very painters occurred, and it is a context that allows us to correct a specific and widespread error, discussed in the next section.

## Debunking a Persistent Error: The "1362 Collaboration on Duccio's Maestà"

A claim that circulates widely in online and popular sources—repeated, for instance, in the English-language Wikipedia article on the painter—holds that "a document from 1362 documents his collaboration with Bartolomeo Bulgarini and Luca di Tommè in work on the Maestà of Duccio di Buoninsegna." This formulation is misleading and should be corrected, and the manner of its correction is itself instructive about how errors propagate in the literature on minor masters.

There is, in fact, a genuine document of 1362 underlying the claim. As [Cristina Ranucci](/scholars.html#ranucci-cristina) reports in her *Dizionario Biografico* entry on Luca di Tommè, the record registers "il pagamento di complessive 4 lire e 10 soldi a L[uca], a Bartolomeo Bulgarini e a Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio per 'arghomentare a levare' [spostare] la Maestà di Duccio di Buoninsegna (Milanesi, p. 50; [Freuler](/scholars.html#freuler-gaudenz), 1991, p. 66)"—a payment of 4 lire and 10 soldi, made jointly to Luca di Tommè, Bartolomeo Bulgarini, and Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio, for advising on the *moving* of Duccio's Maestà to another position within the cathedral. The document is printed in Milanesi 1854, volume I, page 50, and the practical context was the reconfiguration of the cathedral's choir around 1359–1362, which necessitated relocating Duccio's great altarpiece.

The error in the popular formulation is twofold. First, the three painters were not engaged in "work on" the Maestà in the sense of creating, painting, or restoring it; they were consulted, as senior authorities, about its physical *relocation*. Second, the phrase "collaboration ... in work on" wrongly implies an artistic collaboration on Duccio's panel, when the document attests only an advisory role concerning its removal to a new site. The correct statement is that in 1362 Jacopo was paid, together with Bartolomeo Bulgarini and Luca di Tommè, for advising on the moving of Duccio's Maestà within Siena Cathedral.

This correction matters not only for accuracy's sake but because the true fact is, if anything, more revealing than the false one: it shows Jacopo, at the height of his career, ranked among the three painters whose judgment the Opera del Duomo trusted with the handling of the city's most sacred image—precisely the kind of expert authority that the *perizie* of 1373, 1376, and 1388–1389 also attest. It is telling, and reassuring, that the most authoritative modern account of Jacopo—Magister's *Dizionario Biografico* entry—does not include the garbled "collaboration on the Maestà" claim at all; the document surfaces, correctly framed, in the literature on his colleagues rather than in his own.

Several other errors and confusions recur in the popular literature and should be flagged briefly. The birth-and-death dates "1330–1410," which appear in some database records (including Wikidata and older library authorities), are simply wrong: the documentary and scholarly consensus places his birth around 1315–1319 and his death before 1396. The attribution of the Pisan "Maestro degli Ordini" frescoes to Taddeo Gaddi, on Vasari's authority, is an error corrected by the Longhi–Bellosi reconstruction.

The inclusion of the Madonna 'Belverde' among Jacopo's secure works is an error corrected by the discovery of Taddeo di Bartolo's signature. And the notion that Jacopo designed the baptistery façade is an over-reading of the 1382 payment for a drawing he merely handed over. A further tangle concerns Frinta's 1976 hypothesis, which sought to identify Jacopo's father Mino with the painter Mino Parcis; Magister rightly judges this proposal "di scarso fondamento" (of scant foundation), observing that the shared use of certain punches in the gilded grounds suggests at most that Jacopo frequented Mino Parcis's workshop, not a family tie. Each of these corrections rests on published scholarship, and together they suggest how much of the "common knowledge" about Jacopo requires quiet emendation.

## Critical Fortune and the Historiography of a "Reconstructed" Painter

The critical fortune of Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio is, in large measure, the history of the modern discipline of Sienese connoisseurship applied to a single, difficult case. It is worth tracing that history as a coherent arc, because it illuminates both the achievements and the limits of the methods by which such painters are known.

The foundation was documentary. Milanesi's *Documenti* of 1854 established the archival skeleton—the guild inscriptions, the civic offices, the commissions, the *perizie*—that no later scholar has been able to do without. For most of the later nineteenth century and the early twentieth, Jacopo remained essentially a name attached to documents and to the two signed panels, cited in general histories ([Adolfo Venturi](/scholars.html#venturi-adolfo)'s *Storia dell'arte italiana*, volume V, of 1907, mentions him) without a coherent artistic personality.

The effort to give the name a body of work began in earnest with Frederick Mason Perkins, whose article "Su alcuni dipinti di Giacomo di Mino del Pellicciaio" appeared in the *Bullettino senese di storia patria* in 1930. Perkins—an American connoisseur long resident in Italy—made several of the attributional proposals (including, notably, the ascription of the Montepulciano *Coronation*) that would later be vindicated by Bellosi. Raimond van Marle's treatment in the second volume of his survey of the Sienese school (1934), Enzo Carli's discussions of the 1950s, and Millard Meiss's scattered but acute observations (including his 1946 note on the Italian primitives at Konopiště and his 1951 book) carried the reconstruction forward.

The two decisive interventions were, as we have seen, Longhi's isolation of the "Maestro degli Ordini" in 1955 and Bellosi's identification of that master with Jacopo in 1972. After 1972, the work of consolidation and refinement fell above all to Cristina De Benedictis, whose *La pittura senese 1330–1370* (1979) and whose synthetic essays—including the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry of 1996 and her contribution to the *La pittura in Italia* volumes on the Due- and Trecento—provided the fullest critical framing of Jacopo's development and of his historical significance as an initiator of the neo-martinian revival.

Alongside De Benedictis, Mojmír Frinta's 1976 study in the *Mitteilungen des Kunsthistorischen Institutes in Florenz*—which used the evidence of punch-marks in gilded grounds to disentangle works of Pietro Lorenzetti, the Master of the Beata Umiltà, Mino Parcis da Siena, and Jacopo—brought a new, technical instrument to bear on the attributional problems. The modern reference literature—Torriti's catalogue of the Siena Pinacoteca (1977), Magister's *Dizionario Biografico* entry (2004), the online catalogues of the Fondazione Zeri—represents the sedimentation of this century-long labor.

What emerges from this historiographical survey is a sober assessment of the state of knowledge. On the documentary side, the picture is firm and unlikely to change dramatically, barring new archival finds: Jacopo's life-dates, his guild standing, his civic offices, his commissions, and his role as an expert valuer are securely established. On the side of the œuvre, the picture is far more provisional. The two signed panels are fixed; the identification with the "Maestro degli Ordini" is a widely accepted hypothesis; and beyond that lies a body of attributions of varying solidity, several of which (the Montepulciano *Coronation*, the Perugia triptych, the Pinacoteca polyptych no. 58) have migrated through several names before settling—provisionally—on Jacopo, while others (the Todi Purgatory, the Spanish Chapel passages) remain genuinely open. A comprehensive modern monograph would be needed to stabilize the catalogue; in its absence, every list of Jacopo's works, including the one offered in this essay, should be read as a report of scholarly opinion rather than a register of established fact.

## Conclusion: The Measure of a Minor Master

What, in the end, is the historical measure of Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio? He was not a painter of the first rank—not a Duccio, a Simone Martini, or an Ambrogio Lorenzetti—and it would falsify his significance to inflate him into one. But the category of "minor master" can mislead if it is taken to imply marginality. On the evidence of the documents, Jacopo was, for some four decades, one of the most respected and most publicly engaged painters in Siena: second only to Lippo Vanni in the guild roll of 1355, trusted by the Commune and the Opera del Duomo to value his colleagues' work and to advise on the handling of Duccio's Maestà, elected repeatedly to civic office. This is the profile of a pillar of the profession, not a marginal figure.

His art-historical importance, as the best scholarship construes it, lies in two things. First, in his role—if the consensus dating holds—as an early and deliberate reviver of the manner of Simone Martini, and thus as a source of the neo-martinian current that would define Sienese painting into the fifteenth century. Second, in his probable formation of Taddeo di Bartolo, through whom that current passed to the next age. Both claims are interpretive, and both depend on a reconstructed corpus rather than on documents; they should be held with the appropriate tentativeness. But if they are even approximately right, then Jacopo occupies a genuine hinge-position in the history of Sienese painting, linking the golden age before the plague to the long, elegant afterlife of that age in the later Trecento and beyond.

The deeper lesson of his case, however, may be methodological. Jacopo di Mino del Pellicciaio is a painter about whom we know a great deal and are certain of very little—a man whose life is documented and whose art is largely conjectural. To write about him honestly is to practice, at every turn, the discipline of distinguishing what is proven from what is probable, and what is probable from what is merely repeated. The two signed panels; the guild inscriptions; the civic offices; the *perizie*; the lost frescoes of the Scala and the Duomo—these are the bedrock. The "Maestro degli Ordini," the Montepulciano *Coronation*, the pupillage of Taddeo di Bartolo—these are the persuasive reconstructions. The Spanish Chapel, the Todi Purgatory, the exact chronology of the martinian turn—these remain open. And the "collaboration on Duccio's Maestà," the design of the baptistery façade, the Madonna 'Belverde,' the dates 1330–1410—these are the errors to be cleared away. A painter seen thus, in his full epistemic complexity, is more instructive than a painter falsely made whole.

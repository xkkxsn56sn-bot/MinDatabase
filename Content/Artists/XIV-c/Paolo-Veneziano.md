## Introduction: A Painter Between Two Worlds

Few figures in the history of medieval Italian painting occupy so foundational and so precarious a position as *Paolo Veneziano*. He is foundational because the Venetian school, which in later centuries would produce Giovanni Bellini, Titian and Tintoretto, can trace its first identifiable personality to him. He is precarious because the evidence on which his identity rests is unusually thin. There are a handful of signatures, a scatter of notarial and governmental records, and a large body of works grouped around them on stylistic grounds. Between these poles lies a vast and contested territory. Within it, generations of scholars have moved panels in and out of his catalogue, pushed his birth date backward and forward, and debated whether his art should be read as the last flowering of Byzantine icon painting on the lagoon or as the first chapter of a distinctly Venetian Gothic.

Knowledge of Paolo rests on three levels of certainty. The first is what is documented, meaning signatures, dated inscriptions and archival texts. The second is what rests on broadly accepted attribution. The third is what remains hypothesis or open debate. The distinction is not pedantry. In Paolo's case the boundaries between these levels have often been blurred, and a number of persistent errors, some of them in reputable reference works, have grown up in the gaps.

Three modern contributions frame the discussion. Michelangelo Muraro's monograph of 1969 assembled and published most of the documents on which Paolo's biography rests, and argued for a restricted, rigorously defined catalogue. Rodolfo Pallucchini's survey of Venetian Trecento painting of 1964 offered an expansive and influential reconstruction of Paolo's career, including an early, "continental" phase. Filippo Pedrocco's monograph of 2003 provides the fullest modern catalogue and the reference point for much recent discussion.

## Historiography: The Slow Construction of a Painter

It is worth beginning with a fact that is easily forgotten: Paolo Veneziano, as a coherent artistic personality, is a modern invention in the strict sense, a construction of twentieth-century connoisseurship. The great nineteenth-century surveys of Italian painting knew the name from signed works but attributed to him only four paintings. Only in 1930, when <a href="/scholars.html#sandberg-vavala-evelyn">Evelyn Sandberg Vavalà</a> assembled around the signed works a corpus of roughly thirty paintings in an article in The Burlington Magazine, did Paolo emerge as a master with a recognisable development. In the same years the Russian Byzantinist <a href="/scholars.html#lazarev-viktor-nikitich">Viktor Lazarev</a> began publishing a series of studies that placed Venetian Trecento painting within the orbit of Palaeologan art. His article of 1954, "Maestro Paolo e la pittura veneziana del suo tempo", remained a landmark for decades.

From that point the historiography split into two broadly opposed tendencies, and the split still shapes the field. One tendency, represented above all by Pallucchini's 1964 synthesis, enlarged Paolo's catalogue considerably. It attributed to him paintings that more cautious critics regarded as products of his circle or school. It also proposed that the young Paolo had begun as a "continental" painter, steeped in the lessons of Giotto's work in Padua and of the Riminese masters who had translated Giotto's idiom into the more precious register of the Adriatic. The other tendency, represented by Lazarev and above all by Muraro's 1969 monograph, sought to restrict the catalogue to little more than the signed works and those indisputably close to them. It denied that Paolo had passed through a Giottesque apprenticeship. In this reading, he appeared on the Venetian scene already equipped with the balanced Gothic-Palaeologan idiom of the Vicenza polyptych of 1333. Pedrocco's monograph of 2003 broadly sided with the second group on the question of the beginnings, while assembling a large and richly documented catalogue.

The modern construction of Paolo's oeuvre has been punctuated by exhibitions. Each of them, by bringing scattered panels together, sharpened or overturned attributions. Paolo featured in the post-war exhibition of five centuries of Venetian painting held in Venice in 1945, and in the 1950 Ancona exhibition of Venetian painting in the Marche. An exhibition devoted to "Paolo Veneziano and his circle" was mounted in Zagreb in 1967, bringing the Dalmatian and Istrian material to the centre of discussion. The 1974 Venice exhibition "Venezia e Bisanzio" placed his work within the long history of Venetian-Byzantine exchange. The Rimini exhibition of 2002, "Il Trecento adriatico: Paolo Veneziano e la pittura tra Oriente e Occidente", re-framed him as the central figure of an Adriatic koine. In 2004 a Zagreb exhibition on the Gothic century in the Adriatic again gathered the Croatian works. In 2005 a Trieste exhibition of restored Istrian works, "Histria", brought out of storage panels that had been taken from Istria to Italy in 1941. In the same year an exhibition at Tours, devoted to fragments of Venetian polyptychs around Lorenzo Veneziano, examined the generation that followed him.

The most recent milestone is the first monographic exhibition devoted to Paolo in the United States. "Paolo Veneziano: Art and Devotion in 14th-Century Venice" was shown at the J. Paul Getty Museum from 13 July to 3 October 2021. It had been conceived in collaboration with the Frick Collection in New York, but the pandemic made touring impossible, and it was shown only at the Getty. Its accompanying catalogue was published by the Frick. The exhibition reflects a shift in the questions being asked. The emphasis has moved from the attributional debates that dominated the mid-twentieth century toward technical analysis, the reconstruction of dismembered ensembles, and the place of Paolo's paintings within the wider material culture of fourteenth-century Venice, including manuscripts, ivories, textiles and metalwork.

## The Documentary Evidence: What the Archives Say

Paolo's birth date is unknown, and no document records it. The dates commonly given, "about 1295", "c. 1300" or "before 1300", are inferences, not data. The chief basis for an early birth is the Pala Feriale of 1345, signed by Paolo together with his sons Luca and Giovanni. The sons had to be adults capable of professional collaboration by then. The critical literature has therefore concluded that they must have been at least about twenty, and that their father was born in the last years of the thirteenth century. This is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact.

Paolo's father was named Martino and was also a painter. This is established by a document of 25 February 1339, an act concerning the sale of a piece of land that had formed part of the dowry of Paolo's wife, Caterina Baldoino. In it Paolo is described as "magister Paulus pictor, filius quondam Martini pictoris", that is, master Paolo the painter, son of the late Martino the painter. The same document shows that by 1339 Paolo was established in his own right in the parish of San Luca in Venice. Nothing is known of Martino's works. Some critics have suggested that the anonymous painter known as the Master of the Washington Coronation could have been Martino. This is pure hypothesis, and no document supports it.

The earliest document naming Paolo is a memorandum of 1335 drawn up by the Treviso notary and collector Oliviero Forzetta. Forzetta noted that two brother painters, Marco and Paolo, lived in Venice near Santa Maria dei Frari. He wrote that "magister Marcus pictor" had a brother named Paolo, also a painter. Marco had executed paintings on cloth "in the German manner" ("pannos Theutonicos") for San Francesco in Treviso and for the Frari, and had also made stained-glass windows ("fenestre vitree") for the Frari. Paolo, for his part, possessed drawings on paper of the Death of Saint Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> and the Dormition of the Virgin. These reproduced works painted "ad modo Theutonicum" on cloth for the Franciscans at Treviso. The document is precious for several reasons. It shows that Marco alone was given the title "magister", which has led to the inference that he was the elder brother and head of a workshop. It also shows that the brothers' activity extended to media other than panel painting. And it records the circulation of drawn models in the workshop. The meaning of the phrase "ad modo Theutonicum" has been debated. Pedrocco read it as evidence of a workshop specialising in a "German", that is Gothic, manner. Other scholarship has objected that it might refer instead to particular technical recipes imported from the German lands. The matter remains open.

On 30 March 1341 Paolo appears as a witness, signing himself "ego Paulus pictor Sancti Lucae testis subscripsi", confirming his residence in the contrada of San Luca. The residence in San Luca is therefore documented at least from 1339 and again in 1341. On 16 September 1342 a "ser Paulus pinctor" was appointed by officials of the Republic to prepare the apparatus for the Festa delle Marie each year, with salary and provision. Whether this is the same man is disputed. Both a 1986 survey of Venetian Trecento painting (p. 181) and Pedrocco's monograph (2003, p. 128 n. 25) have pointed out that it might refer to a homonymous decorator of furniture, and it should not be cited as a certain record of the painter.

The records connected with the Pala Feriale belong to a central phase of Paolo's career. On 20 May 1343 the procurators of San Marco allocated 400 gold ducats. This sum probably covered the restoration and remounting of the Pala d'Oro within a new Gothic goldsmith's frame, as well as the painted cover. The cover itself was completed and signed on 22 April 1345, as its inscription records. A further payment of 20 gold ducats for a panel for the chapel of San Nicolò in the Doge's Palace is recorded on 20 January 1347. That is the modern equivalent of 20 January 1346 *more veneto*, since the Venetian year began on 1 March. The difference explains why some reference works speak of a payment in 1346 and others of 1347. They describe the same document. The altarpiece perished in the fire that struck the palace on 14 September 1483. Pallucchini proposed connecting with it two small panels of the Stories of Saint Nicholas now in the Uffizi in Florence. Studies of 1992 and 2009 have instead dated these panels considerably earlier, to the mid-1320s, so the connection remains hypothetical.

Two Dalmatian wills complete the documented record of Paolo's lifetime, and both concern Dubrovnik (Ragusa). On 3 March 1348 a certain Šimun Rastić (Simone Resti) left 80 perpers to the Dominican friars<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> to have a crucifix painted near the high altar. He also asked them to buy an ancona representing the Maestà to be placed above the crucifix. The will does not name the painter. Its connection with Paolo depends on the attribution to him of the great crucifix still in the Dominican church. On 18 April 1352 Nikola Lukarić (Nicola Lucari) ordered in his will that "magistro Polo pintori Veneti" should be paid 150 perpers for a "supraaltare", an altar dossal, for the family chapel in San Domenico. This is the only document that names Paolo in connection with a Dalmatian commission. The dossal itself is lost.

Paolo's last dated work is the *Coronation of the Virgin* in the Frick Collection, signed with his son Giovanni in 1358. The terminus ante quem for his death is provided by a document of 1362, described in the literature as a dispute or lawsuit ("controversia"). In it Paolo's son Marco is identified as son of "quondam magistri Pauli pictoris", the late master Paolo, painter. The document was published by Muraro, and the critical literature places Paolo's death by September 1362. The record does not say when or where Paolo died, only that he was dead by the time of the dispute, and statements that he died in Venice in September 1362 over-read the evidence. Paolo was active until at least 1358 and was already reported dead in 1362.

One further confusion deserves attention: the so-called "plebanus" question. Venetian Trecento painting includes a painter who signed himself "Stefanus plebanus Sanctae Agnetis", Stefano, parish priest of Sant'Agnese. His documented activity falls between about 1369 and the mid-1380s. The title "plebanus" belongs to Stefano and has nothing to do with Paolo. No source describes Paolo as a cleric. He is called "magister" and "pictor" and is documented as a married layman with a dowry-bringing wife and sons. The confusion may arise partly from old photographic captions of the Chioggia polyptych of 1349. Those captions ascribed its central Madonna to "Stefano Pievano di Sant'Agnese". Whatever the truth of that old attribution, it does not make Paolo a priest.

## The Venetian-Byzantine Context

Paolo was formed in a city whose visual culture differed from that of any other Italian centre. In the early fourteenth century the dominant pictorial language in Venice was still anchored in Byzantine tradition. That meant both the courtly art of Constantinople and the provincial idioms of the Balkans, including Serbia and Macedonia, with which Venice had close commercial ties. The decisive monument was the basilica of San Marco itself. Its mosaic programme, accumulated over two centuries, offered a vast repertory of Byzantine figure types, narrative formulae and gold-ground luminosity. The basilica also housed the Pala d'Oro, a goldsmith's retable of Byzantine enamels set in a jewelled frame. It embodied in a single object the Republic's claim to be the heir of Byzantium.

Under Doge Andrea Dandolo<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-doge-andrea-dandolo" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, who reigned from 1343 to 1354, this inherited Byzantinism acquired a programmatic character. Dandolo was a historian, a friend of Petrarch<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-petrarch" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> and the architect of an official culture that cultivated Venice's political and spiritual descent from Byzantium. Recent scholarship has spoken of the doge's "dreams" of a Venetian state art modelled on Byzantine imperial practice. It was Dandolo who commissioned the Gothic remounting of the Pala d'Oro, completed in 1345. A Latin inscription on the Pala records that "in the forty-fifth year of the thirteen hundreds", when Andrea Dandolo was doge and Marco Loredan and Francesco Querini were procurators, the ancient panel, precious with gems, was renewed. The new goldsmith's work, with settings in a French-inflected Gothic taste, already shows how Byzantine heritage and modern Gothic luxury could be combined in a single object of state. Paolo's painted cover would do the same in paint.

Venice, however, was not sealed off from the mainland. The same decades saw the rebuilding of the Doge's Palace from 1340, whose capitals are associated with the sculptor Filippo Calendario and display an advanced Gothic naturalism. They saw the arrival in Padua and the Veneto of the consequences of Giotto's Arena Chapel<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-arena-chapel" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>. And they saw a lively traffic of Gothic forms from Bologna, from the Riminese school and from beyond the Alps. Forzetta's reference to work "in the German manner" by Paolo's brother is a reminder that "Gothic" in this context could mean northern European as much as Tuscan. The luxury trades of the city added a further dimension. These included silk weaving, the import of Asian and Islamic textiles, ivory carving, glass and goldsmith's work. The Getty-Frick exhibition of 2021 placed Paolo's work alongside manuscript illumination, ivories, textiles and metalwork. It thereby underlined that his paintings belong to a material culture in which the boundaries between "painting" and the sumptuary arts were fluid.

The older literature sometimes proposed a journey by Paolo to Constantinople to account for the Palaeologan elements in his art. This hypothesis has been set aside by the critical literature. The Palaeologan components of his style can be explained by the ample presence of Byzantine works and craftsmen in Venice itself. The innovations of the Vicenza polyptych are better understood as the result of an effort to enrich the traditional formulae of the lagoon with elements derived from transalpine Gothic than as the fruit of direct contact with the Byzantine capital.

## Beginnings: The Problem of the Early Works

The problem of Paolo's beginnings is a much disputed area of his biography, and one in which errors circulate freely. The fixed point is the Vicenza polyptych of 1333. Before it lies a group of works whose attribution, dating and even authorship are a matter of debate.

The first of these, and one frequently misreported, is the painted dossal with the Stories of the Blessed Leone Bembo, today in the parish church of San Biagio at Vodnjan (Dignano) in Istria. It is a painted cover for the saint's sarcophagus, divided into fields with the figure of the Blessed Leone at the centre and his miracles at the sides. It came from the small church of San Sebastiano adjoining San Lorenzo in Venice, suppressed in 1810, and reached Vodnjan after the Napoleonic suppressions. The catalogue of the Fondazione Giorgio Cini records an inscription painted in capitals at the bottom, "MCCCXXI FACTV FVIT HOC OPVS", along with dimensions of 66 by 166 centimetres and a commissioner, the nun Tommasina Vitturi. Two points must be stressed. First, the work is dated 1321, not 1333, as the date is sometimes misreported. Second, the inscription carries no signature. The attribution to Paolo rests entirely on style. Pallucchini and others who favour a "continental" beginning considered it an early work by Paolo. The 1967 Zagreb exhibition catalogue and Pedrocco's monograph assigned it instead to a predecessor of Paolo. Muraro is reported to have questioned even the reliability of the 1321 date. The Bembo dossal is therefore best described as a dated but unsigned Venetian work of 1321 whose attribution to Paolo is a minority, if respectable, opinion.

A similar uncertainty attaches to the figures of donors, the podestà Andrea Memmo and his wife, painted beside the polychrome wooden relief of Saint Donatus in the church of Santi Maria e Donato at Murano. The relief is dated 1310. The systematic catalogue of the National Gallery of Art in Washington considers it plausible that the donor figures are by Paolo. That would make them his earliest surviving painting. Other authorities reject the attribution outright. Descriptions of Paolo as "documented from 1310" reflect this attribution, but the date belongs to the Murano relief, not to any document naming Paolo, and should not be read as the beginning of his documented career.

The same early group includes several further works. There are five small panels of the Stories of the Virgin in the Musei Civici at Pesaro, whose compositions follow Giotto's Arena Chapel scenes almost literally. There are the lateral wings with saints of the Saint Clare triptych in Trieste, and a small Crucifix with saints on a red ground in Ravenna. There is a frontal with the Stories of Saint Ursula in a Florentine private collection. And there are the frescoes recently attributed to Paolo in the church of San Fermo in Verona, which the Washington catalogue would date as early as 1319–1320. All of these are attributions without documentary support. Their acceptance depends on whether one believes that the young Paolo passed through a Giottesque, mainland phase.

The four small panels with Stories of the Virgin in the church of San Pantalon in Venice, sometimes described as the remains of a polyptych, have had a similar fortune. Pallucchini drew attention to them, and Pedrocco proposed them as youthful works of the later 1320s. Studies of 2002, 2007 and 2009 have been notably cautious, judging that their dominant Byzantine component shows none of the interest in modern Gothic forms that characterises Paolo's secure works.

A major correction concerns the *Coronation of the Virgin* in the National Gallery of Art, Washington (Samuel H. Kress Collection). It is inscribed "MCCCXXIIII", that is 1324, on the base of the throne. It was purchased by the Kress Foundation in 1952 as a work by Paolo Veneziano and long held a central place in his early catalogue. The Gallery no longer attributes it to him. It now catalogues the painting under the name of an anonymous master, the "Master of the Washington Coronation". The critical literature, from Muraro onward, has generally assigned it to a painter of more Byzantinising culture than Paolo, possibly of the preceding generation. Descriptions of it as Paolo's work in some general reference works should now be regarded as outdated. The identity of the Master of the Washington Coronation remains unknown. Proposals that he was Paolo's father Martino, or his brother Marco, are pure conjecture.

## The Signed and Dated Corpus

The backbone of any discussion of Paolo is the small group of works that carry his signature, a date, or both. They should be considered one by one, distinguishing the signed from the merely dated.

The earliest signed and dated work is the polyptych from the Franciscan church of San Lorenzo in Vicenza, now in the Musei Civici at Palazzo Chiericati. It bears the inscription "MCCCXXXIII PAULUS D[E] VENECIIS PI[N]XIT H[O]C OPUS". A seventeenth-century note describes the complete altarpiece. It showed the "most happy passing of Our Lady of Heaven", with Saint Michael the Archangel and nine other saints, male and female, on either side. Only three compartments survive, mounted as a triptych: the central *Dormitio Virginis* and the standing Saints Francis and Anthony of Padua. The two saints remain close to Palaeologan models in their tapering corporeality. The central scene follows traditional Byzantine iconography, but its handling is new. The figure of Christ in the mandorla is shifted slightly to the right, interrupting the rigid symmetry and giving the composition a gentle movement. The Virgin's mantle has a softly undulating hem, and she lies on an embroidered cloth of typically Venetian character. The angels' long wings set up a rapid ornamental rhythm. The work can fairly be called a manifesto of Paolo's first maturity, and its quality has persuaded many that by 1333 he was already an established master.

A second signed work of the same year is known only from a written source. It was a *Death of Saint Francis* signed "PAULUS VENETUS FECIT HOC OPUS MCCCXXXIII", recorded in 1650 in the collection of Count Girolamo Gualdo in Vicenza. It is lost. Its subject, like that of the Vicenza Dormition, recalls the drawings Forzetta saw in Paolo's possession in 1335, though no direct connection can be proved.

The next fixed point is a *Madonna and Child* in the Crespi collection in Milan, signed and dated August 1340: "PA[ULUS] MCCCXL M[ENSE] AG[USTI] HA[NC] PI[NXIT]". It is probably part of a larger ensemble. Here the Virgin sits solemnly on an architectural throne covered with sumptuous cloth. Two small angels like monochrome statuettes stand on the arms of the throne, and four further angels peer from behind the cloth of honour. The Gothic accent is stronger than at Vicenza, and the work served as a prototype for a series of Madonnas repeated with few variations.

The Pala Feriale of 1345, discussed in its own section below, is the only work signed by Paolo together with two sons. It is followed by the *Madonna and Child with Eight Angels*, also known as the *Madonna della Pera*, from the parish church of Santa Maria at Carpineta near Cesena, now in the Museo Diocesano e della Cattedrale at Cesena. Its inscription reads "PAULUS DE VENECIIS PI[N]XIT MCCCXLVII". The photographic archive of the Fondazione Zeri records that the panel has been transferred to canvas and measures 122.5 by 74 centimetres. The wooden throne shows a pronounced search for spatial volume. The Virgin's proportions are elongated, her clothes and jewels sumptuous, and the flesh tones lightened.

The polyptych formerly in the church of San Martino at Chioggia, now in the Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra there, is dated 1349. It shows the Virgin and Child enthroned with saints and stories of Saint Martin. Its attribution to Paolo was first made by Sandberg Vavalà in 1930 and is generally accepted. Some local and older sources, however, speak more cautiously of "Paolo Veneziano and assistants" or even of "a follower of master Paolo". As already mentioned, old photographic captions attributed its central Madonna to Stefano, plebanus of Sant'Agnese. Whether the central panel is a later substitution remains unresolved. Technically the Chioggia polyptych is important because it marks the first use of a specific punch in the decoration of the haloes.

A polyptych from the collection of the Marchese Campana, now in the Louvre, is dated 1354. It shows the Virgin and Child enthroned between Saints Francis, John the Baptist, John the Evangelist and Anthony of Padua. The central panel (inv. MI 396) carries the inscription "MCCCLIIII.M.D.T." on the throne. It measures 1 metre by 0.605 metres and is painted on poplar. Its original frame survives, though partly remade. The inscription is a date, not a signature. The work is therefore dated but not signed, and its attribution to Paolo, first proposed by Sandberg Vavalà in 1930, rests on style. The Treccani Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale (1998) groups it with the Piran polyptych of 1355 as "molto legati al linguaggio del maestro, ma con alcune durezze che postulano quanto meno la presenza della scuola": very close to the master's idiom, but with a certain hardness that implies at least the participation of the workshop. The panels came to the French state with the Campana collection, acquired from the papal government in 1861 and entering the Louvre in 1863. One of the lateral panels was deposited at the Musée des Augustins in Toulouse from 1863 to 1956. Some secondary sources misdate the Louvre Madonna to 1358, apparently by confusion with the Frick *Coronation*. The date on the panel is 1354.

The polyptych from the collegiate church of San Giorgio at Piran (Pirano) in Istria, now in Slovenia, is dated 1355. It shows the Virgin and Child enthroned with angels and eight saints, among them Mary Magdalene, Nicholas, Mark, John the Baptist, John the Evangelist, Blaise, Anthony Abbot and Catherine of Alexandria. The Zeri archive records its dimensions as 82.5 by 247 centimetres. It was formerly ascribed to an anonymous "Master of the Piran Polyptych" before being identified with Paolo. Like the Louvre polyptych, it is considered by part of the literature to show workshop participation. It left Istria for Italy in 1941 and was long kept in storage at the Museo Nazionale del Palazzo di Venezia in Rome. It is now in the Civico Museo Sartorio in Trieste, on deposit from Palazzo Venezia. Recent scholarship has associated with it a *Crucifixion*, also from Piran and also in Trieste, as a possible upper element. Recent technical study has examined the possibility that the altarpiece was mounted on a rotating device connected with the ritual unveiling of relics, a practice documented on the Istrian and Dalmatian coast. Both proposals remain hypotheses.

The series closes with the *Coronation of the Virgin* in the Frick Collection. It is the last dated work and, after the Pala Feriale, the only one signed jointly with a son. The inscription reads "MCCCLVIII PAULUS CUM IOHANINUS EIU[S] FILIU[S] PI[N]SERU[N]T HOC OP[US]", "1358. Paolo with his son Giovannino painted this work". Along the base of the throne the first two lines of the antiphon *Regina coeli* appear. A choir of angels plays a range of instruments, while three angels at the centre sing with open mouths. The style is marked by greater sobriety, a rose-coloured architectural throne, simplified garments wrapping slender bodies, and a lightened palette.

The Frick panel raises a provenance question. Its recorded history describes it as "probably from a chapel near Ravenna", then with Count Bacinetti and on the Munich art market in 1867. It was bought in 1873 by the painter Franz Reichardt and sold in 1873 or 1874 to the Prince of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, from whose collection it was dispersed around 1928, before entering the Frick through Knoedler. Since 1977, however, the literature has linked the panel with the lateral saints now in the Pinacoteca comunale of San Severino Marche, which came from the high altar of the Dominican church there, Santa Maria del Mercato. The museum presents this connection as probable rather than certain. The two accounts are not irreconcilable, since a panel may travel far after leaving its church. But the "Ravenna" provenance and the San Severino reconstruction deserve to be held in tension until a documentary link is found.

## The Pala Feriale of San Marco

The Pala Feriale, now in the Museo di San Marco, sums up Paolo's position in Venetian culture. It was the painted cover that concealed the Pala d'Oro on ordinary weekdays, the *giorni feriali*, and it was commissioned within Andrea Dandolo's remounting of the goldsmith's retable, for which the procurators allocated funds on 20 May 1343. Its inscription records that on 22 April 1345 master Paolo with his sons Luca and Giovanni painted the work.

Its fourteen panels, in two registers of seven, join a row of half-length holy figures on the model of the Byzantine iconostasis, with the *Imago Pietatis* at the centre, to seven scenes from the life and posthumous history of Saint Mark. The combination is hybrid in a precise sense: Byzantine in the type of the upper register and in its dark-toned faces, Gothic in the turn of the bodies, the slender hands and the sickle-shaped folds of the mantles, and close to Trecento narrative naturalism in the Mark stories. The scene of the *apparitio*, which shows the relics present in the basilica above the altar where the panel hung, made the cover a painted account of the saint whose body lay beneath. Whether these commissions made Paolo the Republic's "official painter" is a matter of interpretation: the phrase is common in the literature and is supported by the Dandolo commissions, but no document confers such a title on him.

A further work of state patronage is generally associated with Paolo. This is the lunette above the tomb of Doge Francesco Dandolo, who died in 1339, in the chapter house of Santa Maria Gloriosa dei Frari. It shows the doge and his wife Elisabetta Contarini presented to the Virgin and Child by Saints Francis and Elizabeth of Hungary. The attribution, proposed in 1930, is generally accepted. The painting has been connected with the doge's will of 26 October 1339, in which he chose burial at the Frari. On this reading, Muraro saw the commission as a kind of consecration of the master by the state. Pedrocco proposed instead that it was commissioned by the Franciscans. Neither view can be proved from documents, but the work's date around 1339 is generally accepted.

## The Great Polyptychs and the Structure of the Venetian Altarpiece

Paolo is credited with a decisive role in developing the large carpentered polyptych in Venice. This was a multi-tiered altarpiece with gilded, carved framing and numerous painted compartments, which would become the standard Venetian altarpiece form until the late fifteenth century.

The polyptych of the Coronation of the Virgin from the Poor Clares'<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-chiara-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> convent of Santa Chiara in Venice, now in the Gallerie dell'Accademia (cat. 21), measures 168 by 279 centimetres overall. At its centre is the Coronation of the Virgin, set within an elaborate throne against a starry sky, with Christ and Mary wrapped in sumptuous silk cloths and a choir of music-making angels. The lateral compartments contain eight scenes from the life of Christ. These run from the Nativity and Adoration of the Magi through the Baptism, the Last Supper, the Agony in the Garden and Arrest, the Way to Calvary and the Crucifixion, to the Resurrection and the *Noli me tangere*. The upper tier shows four episodes from the life of Saint Francis, together with Pentecost and the Last Judgement. Recent scholarship has observed that the carved frame of the Santa Chiara polyptych offers the oldest surviving example of the scallop-shell motif in Venetian carpentry, a feature that would recur throughout later Venetian altarpieces. The work's history shows how fragile such ensembles were. The central panel was sent to the Brera in Milan in 1808. The rest of the polyptych came to the Accademia after the Napoleonic suppressions in 1812, and the central panel was reunited with it only in 1950. The original church was destroyed by fire in 1574, so the polyptych's original position is unknown.

The dating of the Santa Chiara polyptych illustrates how far opinions about Paolo's chronology have moved. <a href="/scholars.html#van-marle-raimond">Raimond van Marle</a> first connected it with Paolo's early production in 1924. <a href="/scholars.html#longhi-roberto">Roberto Longhi</a>, in 1946, saw it instead as a nostalgic Byzantine "revival" undertaken by the master after the middle of the century, a judgement that conditioned later studies. Pallucchini accepted this view, reading it as a return to more Byzantinising formulae. Others, Muraro among them, regarded it as a workshop product less open to Gothic novelty. More recent scholarship, followed by the Gallerie dell'Accademia, places it instead between the Vicenza polyptych of 1333 and the Pala Feriale of 1345, with a museum dating of about 1335–1340. A technical argument supports this earlier dating. A study of the punchwork by Roberta Salvador (2006–2007) has shown that from the Chioggia polyptych of 1349 onward Paolo used a specific punch for the three dots crowning the decorative tendrils of the haloes. In earlier works the motif was executed freehand. The punch is absent from the Santa Chiara polyptych, which therefore probably precedes 1349. A dissenting strand of scholarship still sets the work in the early 1350s, and the matter should be regarded as not fully settled. Nevertheless the technical evidence, together with the museum's own dating, now weighs toward the earlier date.

The great polyptych on the high altar of the Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> church of San Giacomo Maggiore in Bologna is equally monumental, though its central panel is missing. It has three tiers of figures within a monumental architectural frame, with saints of elongated proportions in softly falling robes. In the cusps a Saint Raphael and a Saint George on a horse of almost classical beauty have struck critics by their "Hellenic" flavour. The polyptych is associated in the literature with a relic of the Holy Cross and with scenes of the life of Saint Nicholas of Tolentino. Its attribution to Paolo goes back to the 1930s and is generally accepted, and Pedrocco devoted a separate study to it in 2000. Its date, however, varies considerably in the sources. Some place it before the consecration of the church in 1344. Others give it around 1345. Some encyclopaedic entries prefer the beginning of the 1350s. This range of more than a decade should be acknowledged rather than concealed. The Bologna polyptych is in any case firm evidence of Paolo's activity for an important mainland religious order outside the Veneto. It also documents the presence of his art in the city that was then a leading centre of Gothic painting in the Po valley. It should be distinguished carefully from Bolognese works with which it is sometimes confused. The *Madonna dei Denti* in Bologna, for example, is a work by the Bolognese painter Vitale da Bologna and has nothing to do with Paolo.

The polyptych from the high altar of Santa Maria del Mercato, the Dominican church of San Severino Marche, now in the town's Pinacoteca civica, is the third great ensemble. Its lateral panels have been connected since 1977 with the Frick *Coronation* as their centre. They comprise eight standing saints in the main register, Catherine of Alexandria, Michael the Archangel, John the Baptist, Peter, Paul, Philip, Dominic and Ursula, and six half-length saints in the pinnacles, fourteen figures in all. The San Severino panels were not lent to the 2021 exhibition. Their figures, with flexible, elegant silhouettes and a chromatic richness of distinctly "flowering Gothic" character, show that Paolo's late art pointed toward the International Gothic.

Alongside these monumental ensembles, the critical literature attaches to Paolo or his workshop a considerable body of further works. These include the crucifix from San Samuele, now in Santo Stefano in Venice, generally dated around 1340. It is a work of intense lyricism that brought to the lagoon a type of painted cross updated on mainland models. Other works in the group are the *Madonna and Child with Two Donors* in the Gallerie dell'Accademia, the Madonna from Sant'Alvise, and a dispersed polyptych with saints in Brescia's Pinacoteca Tosio Martinengo, dated by some critics close to the Piran polyptych of 1355. Panels in Venice's Museo Correr come from the church of San Donà di Piave. Others are scattered across museums in Cambridge (Massachusetts), Chicago, Hartford, London, Los Angeles, Melbourne and New Haven. The *Lion of Saint Mark* in the Museo Correr has been reinterpreted by recent scholarship as a fragment of a lost painted cross. All of these are attributions. They command varying degrees of consensus.

## The Workshop and the Family Enterprise

The signatures of 1345 and 1358 are direct documentary proof that Paolo's production was a family enterprise. In a period when signatures were rare, Paolo chose twice to name his sons beside himself. The phrasing of the Pala Feriale inscription, "master Paolo with Luca and Giovanni his sons painted this work", and that of the Frick *Coronation*, "Paolo with his son Giovannino painted this work", present the work as collective while preserving the father's precedence. The diminutive "Iohaninus" in 1358 is intriguing. It may simply be an affectionate or customary form of address, and it should not be pressed as evidence of the son's age.

The family extended in both directions. Above Paolo stood his father Martino, documented as a painter but without any known works. Beside him stood his brother Marco, whom the 1335 memorandum calls "magister" and credits with paintings on cloth and stained glass for Franciscan churches in Treviso and Venice. Below him came at least three sons, Luca, Giovanni and Marco. The younger Marco must be carefully distinguished from his uncle of the same name. It is this second Marco who appears in the 1362 dispute as son of the late master Paolo. The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogues a *Madonna and Child Enthroned with Saints* under the name of "Marco di Paolo Veneziano", whom it describes as active from 1362 to 1390 and working in Padua and Treviso. Recent scholarship has made further proposals for his oeuvre. Yet no signed work by the younger Marco is known, and his catalogue rests on attribution.

For Luca and Giovanni the documentary record is thinner still. Apart from the two joint signatures with their father, no document concerning either is known after 1358, and no surviving work is signed by either son alone. The Washington catalogue notes that the three sons "continued to disseminate his style after the master's death". This is a reasonable inference from the abundance of Paolesque works in Venice, Istria and Dalmatia in the second half of the century, but individual hands cannot at present be securely distinguished. Dates of activity for Giovanni beginning in 1333, found in some catalogues, rest on no work bearing his name. To speak of Paolo's sons as independent masters with distinct catalogues would therefore go beyond the evidence.

The organisation of the workshop can be inferred mainly from the works themselves. Repeated Madonna types, derived from prototypes such as the Crespi Madonna of 1340, suggest the systematic reuse of models. So does the "formulaic" quality that critics detect in some figures of the Louvre, Piran and Brescia polyptychs, combined with the master's evident hand in the finer passages. Forzetta's note of 1335 that Paolo kept drawings on paper of whole compositions is the only direct documentary evidence for this practice, and it is important. It confirms that compositional models circulated on paper within the family shop. Punchwork offers a further, more technical tool for tracking workshop practice. The identification of a specific halo punch in use from 1349 shows how standardised tools can act as a kind of signature of a workshop phase.

An original product of Paolo's shop was the small portable triptych for private devotion. It was self-supporting, had closable shutters, and combined a central Virgin and Child or Crucifixion with standing saints and narrative scenes. It has been argued that the type was invented in Paolo's workshop. The triptych in the Galleria Nazionale at Parma survives intact. The 2021 exhibition also proposed, on the basis of scientific examination and visual analysis by Getty conservation scientists and curators, a reconstruction of a second triptych. Its panels are divided between the Getty Museum, the National Gallery of Art in Washington, which holds a *Crucifixion* of about 1340–1345, and the Worcester Art Museum in Massachusetts, which holds the wings with saints. The exhibition catalogue also concluded that a Virgin and Child in the Musée du Petit Palais at Avignon was too large to have been the centre of this triptych. The catalogue argued that the Avignon panel probably belonged to a similar but somewhat larger, perhaps later, ensemble, while the Worcester triptych would have had a different central Virgin. The two museums date their panels differently, the Washington *Crucifixion* to about 1340–1345 and the Getty *Annunciation* to about 1348–1350, a discrepancy that the reconstruction leaves unresolved. The reconstruction is a hypothesis grounded in technical evidence rather than documents, but a well-founded one.

## Adriatic Diffusion: Istria, Dalmatia, Emilia and the Marche

The geography of Paolo's patrons is a distinctive feature of his career. Besides Venice and the Venetian Terraferma, represented by Vicenza and Padua, his works and those of his workshop reached Bologna and the Romagna, with Carpineta near Cesena. They reached the Marche, with San Severino, and the eastern Adriatic coast. They also reached Chioggia, within the Venetian dogado, and Piran in Istria.

In Istria, the Bembo dossal at Vodnjan is of disputed attribution, and it arrived there only after the Napoleonic suppressions. Its presence in Istria does not testify to a fourteenth-century Istrian commission. The Piran polyptych of 1355, by contrast, was made for the collegiate church of San Giorgio. It is dated evidence of the workshop's activity for Istrian patrons, even though the polyptych is now in Trieste rather than in Slovenia.

The island of Krk (Veglia) preserves the polyptych of Saint Lucy, with the saint flanked by scenes of her legend. It was made for the church of Saint Lucy at Jurandvor near Baška, a sanctuary renewed in the fourteenth century. It is now in the collection of the Diocese of Krk, after a turbulent modern history that included a period in Trieste until 1945. Its attribution and date are much disputed. Croatian sources generally attribute it to Paolo and his workshop, giving dates around 1340 or even 1333. One authoritative encyclopaedic entry assigns it instead to Paolo's school, while another lists it among the works of the hypothetical early, "continental" phase. The polyptych underwent primary conservation in 2018 by the Croatian Conservation Institute, and later investigations revealed a thick layer of wax over the entire surface. Any final judgement on its quality and authorship will depend on the results of that campaign.

On the island of Rab (Arbe), the literature records a polyptych of the Crucifixion formerly in the church of San Giovanni Evangelista and now in the cathedral museum, generally dated to the 1350s. Some encyclopaedic entries also speak of a polyptych from the convent of Sant'Eufemia at Rab, which they consider among the works probably by Paolo's own hand. Whether these references describe the same object under different provenances is unclear.

Dubrovnik provides the richest combination of document and object. The great painted crucifix on the triumphal arch of the Dominican church is more than five metres high, with the Virgin and Saint John in separate lateral panels. It was first attributed to Paolo in a 1965 article in Arte veneta, and the Rastić will was published by Muraro (1969, p. 88). The crucifix was presumably painted in Venice and shipped to Dubrovnik. Its connection with Šimun Rastić's will of 3 March 1348 is a strong hypothesis, though the will does not name the painter. A local eighteenth-century chronicle reports that the plague ceased in Dubrovnik in February 1358 after the image was displayed in the church, which would imply completion by that date. This is late testimony and should be treated as such. The dossal commissioned by Nikola Lukarić in 1352 is documented but lost. Pedrocco (2003) has also proposed that Paolo painted another crucifix for the Franciscans of Dubrovnik. It was removed after the earthquake of 1667 and is known only from a seventeenth-century drawing. The documents do not settle whether Paolo himself travelled to Dalmatia, a hypothesis long discussed in the literature. The 1352 will names him as the recipient of payment, but that does not prove his presence. The hypothesis of a stay in Dalmatia with his workshop is still debated.

Croatian scholarship has also attributed to Paolo or his circle works in Zadar, Trogir and Split. They include a polyptych in the Galerija umjetnina at Split, assigned to Paolo and his workshop, and an embroidered antependium at Dobrinj on Krk whose design has been connected with him. They illustrate the breadth of the "Paolesque" phenomenon on the eastern Adriatic, but they should be treated as belonging to the circle rather than to the master until a fuller critical consensus is documented.

On the western shore, the Carpineta Madonna of 1347 and the San Severino polyptych show that Paolo's art reached the Romagna and the Marche. The Bologna polyptych places it at the heart of Emilian Gothic. This last fact matters for the history of style. Bolognese painting of the mid-century, and above all that of Vitale da Bologna, was one of the sources from which the next generation of Venetian painters drew the Gothic vivacity that distinguishes them from Paolo. The Adriatic was thus not merely a market for Paolo's exports but a space of reciprocal exchange.

## Style and Technique

Paolo's style has been described as a delicate balance between Byzantine tradition and Gothic elegance. The tension is real and can be traced through his dated works. In the Vicenza polyptych of 1333 the figures remain broadly Palaeologan, but the composition is enlivened by asymmetry and ornamental rhythm. In the Crespi Madonna of 1340 and in the Madonnas the critics place in the 1330s, the Gothic component grows stronger. It shows in the modulated hem of the Virgin's mantle, the slight rotation of her body, and the profusion of patterned textiles and jewels. In the Carpineta Madonna of 1347 and the Chioggia polyptych of 1349 the palette lightens and the line becomes more restless, a development critics read as a prelude to elements of International Gothic. The San Severino saints and the Frick *Coronation* bring a late phase of elongated, flexible figures in pale, luminous colour. In these Paolo's art comes closest to the courtly Gothic of the second half of the century.

The materials of this art were costly and deliberately displayed. Paolo's panels are painted in egg tempera on gold grounds over wooden supports. Where the wood has been identified, as at the Louvre, it is poplar, the usual timber for north Italian panel painting. The gold is enriched with punched and incised decoration, especially in the haloes. As already noted, the tools used for this punchwork have become an instrument of chronology. Paolo's blues, at least in the most important commissions, are ultramarine made from lapis lazuli imported from Afghanistan. It was used for the mantles of Christ and the Virgin in the 1358 *Coronation*. Chrysography, the hatching of draperies with fine lines of gold in the Byzantine manner, is a constant feature of his holy figures. It is a clear sign of his debt to icon painting. The cloth of honour in the Frick *Coronation* was executed in sgraffito, in which paint laid over gold is scratched away to reveal the gold beneath. It reproduces a pattern of phoenix-like creatures derived from Asian silks. Recent scholarship has noted that Catarino and Donato took up the same technique for the cloth of honour in their *Coronation* of 1372 in the Querini Stampalia, a sign of the workshop practices Paolo passed on.

Textiles deserve special emphasis. In painting after painting Paolo lavishes attention on cloths of honour and garments of patterned silk, on embroidered borders and jewelled hems. The Accademia's *Madonna with Two Donors* is celebrated for the beauty of its large-flowered fabric. The Dandolo lunette sets the Virgin against an orange cloth with large coloured flowers. These textiles are not mere decoration. They link the sacred image to the luxury goods that passed through Venice's markets, and they translate into paint the city's role as an emporium between East and West. They also explain why Paolo's Madonnas could serve as models both for mainland painters such as Guariento and, according to one strand of the literature, for the "Beautiful Madonnas" of Bohemia and Austria.

Technical examination has also revealed aspects of workshop practice. On the central panel of the Louvre polyptych, a technical study published in 1998 identified a palette of colours painted on the white preparation in an area hidden by the frame. It is a rare trace of the painter testing his colours directly on the panel. Such evidence, together with the X-radiography and reconstructed panel dimensions used in the 2021 reconstruction of the Worcester triptych, shows the extent to which technical study now drives the study of Paolo's oeuvre.

The development of the altarpiece format is the other great technical theme. Paolo did not invent the polyptych, but the Santa Chiara and Bologna ensembles show him working within monumental gilded and carved frames of several tiers. These combine a central image, rows of standing saints, narrative compartments and pinnacles. The Chioggia polyptych, partly sculpted and partly painted, served according to recent scholarship as a model for a later polyptych by Jacobello del Fiore at Omišalj on Krk. That is a measure of how long Paolo's structural solutions remained authoritative in the Adriatic.

## Iconography: Coronations, Madonnas, Narratives and Donors

Paolo's iconographic repertory is broad. One theme recurs so prominently that it has become almost his signature: the *Coronation of the Virgin*. This is a Western subject, not a Byzantine one, and it enjoyed great success in Venice throughout the fourteenth century. Paolo treated it in the centre of the Santa Chiara polyptych and in the Frick panel of 1358. It also appears in the Washington panel of 1324, which is not by him but belongs to the tradition from which he emerged. In Paolo's hands the Coronation becomes a celestial court ceremony. Christ and the Virgin sit on a single wide throne, wrapped in luxurious cloths, surrounded by choirs of musician angels and set against starry skies or architectural canopies. In the Frick panel, the inscription of the *Regina coeli* on the throne turns the image into a sung liturgy, and the sun and moon beneath the feet of Christ and the Virgin give the scene a cosmic dimension.

The Madonna is the other great subject, and here Paolo's dialogue with Byzantine prototypes is explicit. The *Madonna with Two Donors* in the Gallerie dell'Accademia fuses the Eastern type of the *Platytera*, the Virgin holding a clipeus with the image of the Child, with the Western theme of the Madonna of Mercy, who shelters the faithful under her mantle. Its date is disputed: an early placement before 1333 is considered plausible, while other critics place it in the 1330s. The Crespi Madonna of 1340 and the Carpineta Madonna of 1347 represent the enthroned Virgin of Western type, enriched with angels and textiles. A series of half-length Madonnas repeats these models with variations for private devotion.

Narrative is the third mode. The Pala Feriale gives Venice its first extensive narrative cycle on panel devoted to its patron saint. The Santa Chiara polyptych adds Christological and Franciscan cycles. The Chioggia polyptych narrates the life of Saint Martin. The Bologna polyptych includes the life of a recently deceased Augustinian, Nicholas of Tolentino. The Krk polyptych and the Bembo dossal, whatever their authorship, show the same taste for hagiographic stories told in small compartments. The Saint Nicholas panels from the lost Ducal Palace altarpiece, if Pallucchini's association is correct, show interiors with typically Venetian features such as the roof-top altana. In them figures in fashionable contemporary dress are rendered with lively, chronicle-like attention.

Finally, Paolo's donor images reflect the social world of his patrons. The doge and dogaressa in the Dandolo lunette, the couple in the Accademia Madonna, and the donors of the Murano relief, if they are his, all present the patron within the sacred image itself. This became a habit of Venetian painting. Lorenzo Veneziano's Lion polyptych of 1357 would push it further by giving the donor Domenico Lion a prominent place at the Virgin's feet.

## Legacy and the Transition to Lorenzo Veneziano

Paolo's legacy is visible on two levels. The first is the direct continuation of his workshop by his sons and the diffusion of Paolesque formulae across the Adriatic. The second is his role as the point of departure for the next generation of Venetian painters, above all Lorenzo Veneziano.

Lorenzo must be clearly distinguished from Paolo's family. The shared epithet "Veneziano" means only "the Venetian" and implies no kinship. Lorenzo's signed and dated works run from 1356 to 1372, beginning with a lost small panel of 1356 and, among surviving works, with the Lion polyptych. That polyptych was painted for the high altar of Sant'Antonio di Castello and is now in the Gallerie dell'Accademia. Its inscription names the patron Domenico Lion and records 1357 as the year the work was begun. Since no documents firmly establish Lorenzo's identity, the authoritative biographical dictionary is cautious. It tentatively links him to another Venetian family of painters, that of a Nicolò, a painter, who in 1365 named as his heir a son Lorenzo, "pictor sanctorum". No document connects Lorenzo with Paolo's family by blood or marriage, and the frequent description of him as Paolo's pupil remains an assumption. It has rarely been doubted, but recent scholarship has questioned whether his training was exclusively in Paolo's shop.

The stylistic relationship is nonetheless clear. Lorenzo inherited from Paolo the large polyptych, the Coronation and Madonna types, and the taste for gold and textiles. But he moved decisively away from Paolo's residual Byzantinism. His faces and draperies are more individualised, his line more lively, and his palette more delicately nuanced. These are qualities the critical literature links with Tomaso da Modena, Vitale da Bologna and the Paduan painting of Guariento and the young Altichiero. The Lion polyptych dates from 1357, the year before Paolo's own last dated work. The two artists thus overlapped. The overlap allows us to see two distinct conceptions of painting side by side in mid-century Venice. That of the older master remained balanced between East and West. That of the younger was already decisively Gothic.

After Lorenzo, Venetian painting of the later Trecento continued along paths Paolo had opened. These included the "plebanus" Stefano of Sant'Agnese, Catarino and Donato, and Niccolò di Pietro at the turn of the century, and in the next century Jacobello del Fiore, Michele Giambono and the Vivarini. The large Venetian polyptych, with its carved and gilded frame and its tiers of saints, persisted into the age of Antonio Vivarini, and Jacobello's Omišalj polyptych drew directly on Paolo's Chioggia structure. Even the great Renaissance altarpieces of the Bellini family can be seen as a remote transformation of the Venetian polyptych that Paolo helped to define. In a broader sense, Paolo's art established a Venetian conception of the sacred image in which colour, light, precious materials and the memory of Byzantium count for as much as narrative or spatial construction. A long tradition of criticism has seen this as a constant of Venetian painting.

## Critical Fortune and the Current State of the Question

Paolo's critical fortune can be summarised as a passage from obscurity to centrality, accompanied by persistent instability at the edges of his catalogue. In the nineteenth century he was one name among many "primitives". The connoisseurship of the 1930s turned him into a personality, and the debates of the 1950s and 1960s gave him a career. Pallucchini's expansive reading made him a painter who began under Giotto's spell and passed through phases of Gothic experiment and Byzantine "return". The more austere reconstructions of Muraro and others made him a master whose identity lay in a consistent dialogue between Palaeologan form and Gothic ornament. Pedrocco's monograph of 2003 consolidated the catalogue and the documents and proposed new dates and attributions. In 2009 a two-part reassessment of his career by <a href="/scholars.html#boskovits-miklos">Miklós Boskovits</a>, together with the Washington systematic catalogue of 2016, brought the debate on the early works back into focus. The exhibitions of Rimini (2002) and Los Angeles (2021) placed him respectively in an Adriatic perspective and in that of the material and devotional culture of fourteenth-century Venice.

Several conclusions can now be stated with reasonable confidence. The documented frame of Paolo's career, from the Vicenza polyptych of 1333 to the Frick *Coronation* of 1358 with death by 1362, is secure. The Washington *Coronation* of 1324 is no longer considered his. The Santa Chiara polyptych probably belongs to the 1330s or early 1340s rather than to a late "Byzantine revival". The small portable triptych was a speciality, perhaps an invention, of his workshop. Paolo's works and those of his shop reached an unusually wide Adriatic clientele. Several other questions remain open. They include the existence and character of an early "continental" phase, and the attribution of the Bembo dossal, the Murano donors, the San Pantalon panels and the Krk polyptych. They also include the precise extent of workshop participation in the Louvre and Piran polyptychs, the authorship of the Chioggia Madonna, the date of the Bologna polyptych, and the provenance of the Frick *Coronation*. The individual personalities of Luca, Giovanni and the younger Marco remain undefined as well.

Paolo Veneziano is thus a securely documented Venetian painter of the first half of the Trecento whose oeuvre still demands constant critical vigilance. Calling him the founder of Venetian panel painting is not a rhetorical flourish. It describes his historical role precisely. Before him, painting in Venice can only be described in terms of anonymous masters and traditions. After him, it has a history of named artists, signed works and recognisable workshops. That history begins with the inscriptions of Vicenza, San Marco and the Frick, and with the family of painters who signed them.

The panel, 142 by 90 centimetres, entered the Gallerie dell'Accademia in 1913, when it was bought from the Venetian dealer Antonio Salvadori. Its earlier location is not known. It keeps its original frame, a flat moulding painted with small alternating blocks in red, blue and gold that imitate a dentilled cornice. The frame confirms that the image was conceived as an autonomous devotional panel, not as the centre of a polyptych.

The Virgin occupies almost the whole height of the field. She is seated, but the throne is hidden by her body and by the drapery behind her, so that the figure itself becomes the structure of the image. Her dark blue mantle, sown with gold rosettes, opens over a red gown and falls in broad folds to the lower edge. The head, long and slightly inclined, with narrow almond-shaped eyes and a thin straight nose, follows the Byzantine type of the Hodegetria faces of the Palaiologan period.

Two angels fly in from the upper corners and spread behind her a red cloth of honour patterned with dark stylised flowers. The motif derives from Byzantine imperial ceremony, in which a precious textile marked the place of the sovereign. Here it closes the space behind the Virgin and lends her the dignity of a queen, while the gold ground remains visible only at the edges.

The Child does not sit on his mother's lap. He appears within an oval medallion, a clipeus of deep blue edged in gold and crossed by gold rays, which the Virgin holds before her breast. Dressed in a golden robe, he raises his right hand in blessing. The formula is that of the Platytera, the Virgin who bears the Logos within her familiar from Byzantine apses and icons and uncommon in Venetian panel painting. The medallion turns the Child into a sign of the Incarnation rather than an infant held in the arms.

At the lower left, on a much smaller scale, two donors kneel with joined hands: a woman in red with a white veil, and behind her a man in a dark robe. The Virgin lowers her right hand and draws the edge of her mantle over them. The gesture belongs to the Madonna della Misericordia, the protecting Virgin who shelters the faithful under her cloak, a theme that was spreading in Italy through the confraternities in the same decades. The fusion of the two types, Platytera and Misericordia, in a single figure is an unusual solution.

The donors are probably a married couple. The combination of the Child shown as if within the Virgin's body with a couple placed under her protection has suggested a votive meaning, perhaps linked to an awaited birth. The hypothesis cannot be confirmed in the absence of an inscription or a coat of arms, but it accounts for the choice of an iconography so closely tied to the mystery of the maternity of Mary.

The attribution to Paolo was first proposed by Evelyn Sandberg Vavalà and has not been questioned since. The work is generally placed in the second or third decade of the fourteenth century, before the polyptych of San Lorenzo signed in 1333; one proposal sets it in the later 1320s, shortly after the Bembo dossal of 1321 at Vodnjan (Dignano). Several features support an early date: the strong dependence on Byzantine models in the face of the Virgin, the flat decorative treatment of the mantle, and the absence of the more elaborate punchwork of Paolo's later production. The panel would thus be one of the first known works of the painter.

The panel was restored in 1952 and is now undergoing a new restoration. The glazes of the flesh tones, part of the colour of the Virgin's mantle and an inscription along the lower edge have been lost. The two-coloured dentilled frame and the old cruciform battens on the back are original. The reds of the gown and of the cloth of honour, the greens of the angels and the gold of the medallion read clearly.

The three panels are the surviving parts of the polyptych painted for the Franciscan church of San Lorenzo in Vicenza, now in the Pinacoteca of Palazzo Chiericati. The central panel carries Paolo's earliest signature and date, 1333, in an inscription along its lower edge. Of the original lateral compartments, only the standing Saints Francis and Anthony of Padua remain, now mounted at the sides of the central scene.

The central panel shows the Dormition of the Virgin according to the Byzantine scheme of the *Koimesis*: the Virgin's falling asleep in death, from which, according to tradition, she was taken up into heaven body and soul. She lies on a bier covered with a red and gold cloth, wrapped in a dark blue mantle, her hands crossed on her breast.

The apostles gather around the bier. Peter, with white hair, bends over her head; Paul, bald and bearded, leans over her feet; the others crowd behind, their haloes forming rows of gold. Above and around them rows of angels with gold haloes fill the upper part of the composition, and two red seraphim spread their wings above the scene.

Behind the bier Christ stands in a pointed mandorla of pale light, holding in his arms the soul of his mother, represented as a small swaddled child. The soul, reborn into eternity through death, is shown as an infant held against Christ's breast, as in the Byzantine icons of the subject.

At the top of the panel, in a blue roundel filled with angels, a second image shows Christ receiving the Virgin into heaven, the conclusion of the episode below. The two images, one above the other, join the death of the Virgin to her glorification.

In the lateral panel at the left, Saint Francis, in a brown habit girded with a cord, shows an open book. At the right, Saint Anthony of Padua, younger and beardless, holds a red book. Both are named in red letters above their heads, *S. Franciscus* and *S. Antonius*, and both stand against gold under pointed arches.

The two Franciscan saints reflect the destination of the altarpiece for a church of the order. Francis, bearded, displays his book frontally to the viewer, while Anthony holds his closed against his body; the difference in gesture distinguishes the founder, who teaches, from the follower, who preaches his word.

The panels show losses and wear. The gold of the lateral panels is worn, particularly around the gables, and the upper part of the central panel is abraded.

The four small panels are kept in the Chapel of the Sacred Nail, to the left of the high altar of San Pantalon in Venice, together with the *Madonna of the Poppy*. They narrate four episodes of the life of the Virgin: the Annunciation, the Nativity, the Presentation in the Temple and the Dormition. Their original setting is not documented. Their horizontal format and their uniform size suggest that they formed part of a larger ensemble, perhaps a dossal or the lateral compartments of a polyptych dedicated to the Virgin, of which other scenes may have been lost.

In the *Annunciation* Gabriel kneels on a narrow strip of rock at the left, his great wings still spread from flight, and raises his hand in salutation; the opening words of his greeting are written beside him. The Virgin sits within a small open building, a schematic house with columns and a gabled roof, and answers with a gesture of the hand. The ray of light that crosses the panel from the upper edge brings the dove of the Holy Spirit towards her head. A low pedestal, decorated with painted scrolls, separates the two figures and marks the threshold between the space of the angel and that of the Virgin.

The *Nativity* follows the Byzantine scheme of the cave opening into a rocky mountain, with the ox and the ass bending over the Child, angels on the summits and Joseph seated apart, absorbed in thought. One element departs from that scheme: the Virgin does not lie on a mattress beside the manger, as in the icons, but kneels before the Child in adoration, a Western formula diffused in Italy in the fourteenth century. The manger, rendered as a stone chest with blind arches, alludes to the altar and to the tomb, and thus to the sacrificial meaning of the Incarnation.

The *Presentation in the Temple* is arranged symmetrically around the altar, under a ciborium whose dark vault is studded with stars. Joseph, carrying the two doves of the offering, and the Virgin stand at the left; Simeon, holding the Child, and the prophetess Anna, with her scroll, stand at the right. The Child turns back from Simeon towards his mother and stretches out his arms, a motif of Byzantine origin that introduces a note of tenderness into the ceremonial scene.

The *Dormition* repeats the scheme of the central panel of the Vicenza polyptych of 1333 in reduced form: the bier set parallel to the picture plane, the apostles gathered at its ends, Christ standing behind in a mandorla with the soul of the Virgin in his arms. Peter, at the right, swings the censer of the funeral rite, and an apostle at the left bends towards the foot of the bier. The close relationship with the signed panel of 1333 is compatible with a date around 1330, close to the placement of the panels in the later 1320s proposed by Pedrocco, although dates around 1350 have also been suggested.

The four scenes share a palette of deep reds, olive greens and dark blues against grounds that were originally gilded. The faces, with long noses, heavy-lidded eyes and strong shadows, and the drapery articulated by fine gold highlights in the Virgin's mantle, belong to the Palaiologan idiom that Paolo practised in the first phase of his career. The rocky landscape of the Nativity, with its sharp terraces lit from above, is close to the landscapes of the narrative scenes of the Santa Chiara polyptych.

The attribution to Paolo is not unanimous, and the arguments for and against it turn on the weight given to the Byzantine component of the panels. The kneeling Virgin of the Nativity and the Child turning back to his mother in the Presentation, both Western or humanising elements within a Byzantine scheme, are among the features that favour a connection with Paolo's early work.

The state of the panels is uneven. The gold grounds are largely worn, so that the red bole beneath shows through in the Annunciation and the Presentation, and there are losses of paint, warping and worm damage. Small losses are visible in the lower left of the Nativity and at the foot of the bier in the Dormition.

The polyptych, 168 by 279 centimetres overall, comes from the convent of the Poor Clares of Santa Chiara in Venice. It is a multi-tiered altarpiece in which narrative scenes are arranged around a central episode within a carved and gilded frame, the type that Paolo established for the Venetian altarpiece.

The central panel shows the Coronation of the Virgin, a subject of Western origin that enjoyed great popularity in Venice throughout the fourteenth century. Christ and the Virgin sit side by side on a broad throne against a sky of deep blue scattered with stars, wrapped in mantles of gold-patterned silk. Christ places the crown on the head of his mother, who bows towards him with her hands crossed on her breast.

Above and around the throne a choir of angels with gold haloes plays instruments and sings, filling the upper part of the arch, while smaller angels kneel at the foot of the throne. The precious silks of the mantles, the starry sky and the angels turn the central panel into a celestial stage, described with a decorative richness that recalls the goldsmith's work of San Marco.

At the left of the central panel, in two rows of two arched compartments, are four scenes from the Infancy and Passion of Christ: the Nativity with the Adoration of the Magi, the Baptism in the Jordan, the Last Supper and the Agony in the Garden with the Arrest. The scenes are set against gold grounds, with rocky landscapes and simple architecture, and the figures are grouped in compact, rhythmic arrangements.

At the right, in the same arrangement, are four scenes of the Passion and Resurrection: the Way to Calvary, the Crucifixion, the Resurrection with the *Noli me tangere*, and the Ascension. The eight scenes form a cycle of the life of Christ that surrounds the Coronation, the culmination of the story of the Virgin.

Along the upper tier, under pointed gables, smaller scenes alternate with standing saints. At the left are Pentecost and scenes from the life of Saint Francis and Saint Clare, among them Clare receiving the veil and Francis renouncing his possessions; at the right are Francis receiving the stigmata, a further Franciscan scene and the Last Judgement. The Franciscan subjects reflect the destination of the polyptych for a convent of the Second Order founded by Clare.

The carved frame, with its twisted colonnettes, foliate mouldings, crocketed gables and pinnacles, is among the earliest surviving examples of Venetian Gothic carpentry. In the lunette above the central panel a large carved shell spreads its ribs, painted dark blue between gilded ridges. Two tall pinnacles beside the lunette carry standing prophets, identified as Elijah and Daniel.

The ensemble keeps its original structure. The gilding of the frame has been renewed in places, and the paint surface of the narrative scenes shows wear, particularly in the darker areas; the central panel, separated from the rest between 1808 and 1950, matches the lateral parts in its state of preservation.

  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano, <em>Virgin and Child</em>, c. 1340. Tempera and gold on poplar panel, 44 × 40 cm. Paris, Musée du Louvre, INV 20194 (Campana 315), on deposit at the Musée du Petit Palais, Avignon (D. 228). © Musée du Louvre, image for non-commercial educational use.</figcaption>

The panel belongs to the Louvre, which received it with the Campana collection, and is on deposit at the Musée du Petit Palais in Avignon. It measures 44 by 40 centimetres and is painted on poplar. Once inventoried as a work of Ottaviano da Faenza, it is now catalogued under Paolo Veneziano's name.

The Virgin is shown at half length against a gold ground, within a carved frame with a pointed arch, cusped and scalloped, resting on twisted colonnettes, with rosettes in the spandrels. The frame, with its Gothic profile and its classical twisted columns, gives the image the appearance of a small window or shrine.

She wears a deep blue mantle, lined in green and edged with a gold-embroidered border with pseudo-inscriptions, with a small star on her right shoulder. Beneath it are a white veil with a scalloped edge and an orange-red dress patterned with gold. Her face is long and pale, with a long thin nose, almond eyes and strongly marked rose cheeks, and she inclines her head towards the Child with a melancholy expression.

She holds the Child with both hands, her long, slender fingers spread against his body. The Child, almost naked except for a white loincloth and a pale cloth beneath him, stretches his right arm round her neck and draws her towards him, while turning his head to look out at the viewer. In his left hand he holds a small object with a sprig.

The type, with the Child embracing his mother and pressing close to her face, derives from the Byzantine *Glykophilousa*, the Virgin of tenderness. Paolo transforms it through the softness of the modelling, the Gothic elegance of the hands and the naturalism of the Child's body, solid and fleshy, which recalls the painting of the mainland.

The haloes of the Virgin and the Child are decorated with punched rosettes and small dots arranged in patterns, and incised rays spread from the Child's halo across the gold. The ornament of the borders and the haloes shows the precision of a workshop accustomed to the luxury techniques of Venetian painting.

The colour is built on a few strong contrasts: the saturated blue of the mantle against the gold, the green lining and the orange-red dress at the edges, and the pale flesh of the two figures at the centre. The strong pink of the Virgin's cheeks, laid over pale flesh, is characteristic of Paolo's Madonnas and of the Venetian taste for clear, enamel-like colour.

The panel is in good condition. The blue of the mantle is well preserved, and the gold ground is worn in places. The cloth beneath the Child is crossed by a network of cracks, and there are small losses at the edges. The carved frame shows small losses of gilding.

The small panel, 31 by 38 centimetres in its painted surface, originally had an arched top. Its contour can still be traced in the different appearance of the gilding: the upper corners were added much later to turn the panel into a rectangle. Its shape and size suggest that it stood at the top centre of a portable triptych. The composition is built on the vertical of the cross, which divides the scene into two groups: the mourners at the left and Saint John with the soldiers at the right.

At the centre Christ hangs dead on a tall wooden cross under a white tablet inscribed *I.N.R.I.* His body, thin and pale, is modelled with soft transitions, his head falls on his right shoulder, and a light, transparent loincloth is wrapped around his hips. Blood flows from the wounds of his hands and side.

Four angels in dark blue, with dark wings, fly around the cross against the gold. Three hold cups to catch the blood from the hands and the side, a motif that alludes to the Eucharist; the fourth covers his face with his hands in grief. The dark colour of the angels, unusual in Paolo's work, gives them a mournful, almost nocturnal presence.

Behind the figures runs a low green wall with crenellations, the wall of Jerusalem, outside which the Crucifixion took place. The wall, the angels collecting the blood and the skull of Adam beneath Golgotha all derive from Byzantine representations of the Crucifixion, while the swooning Virgin and the kneeling Magdalen come from Western Gothic painting.

At the left the fainting Virgin, in a dark blue mantle, collapses into the arms of the holy women, one in yellow and one in black, behind whom other heads appear. Her lowered eyes and limp arm express the swoon.

At the foot of the cross the Magdalen, in a pale mantle with long fair hair, kneels and embraces the wood, pressing her face against Christ's feet. Beneath her, in a cleft of red rock at the base of the cross, lies the skull of Adam, on which the blood of Christ falls, the sign that the Crucifixion redeems the sin of the first man.

At the right Saint John, in a red mantle over a blue tunic, gazes up at Christ with clasped hands. Beside him stand soldiers in blue armour and helmets, one with a lance, another, in a red cloak and a blue tunic, pointing up at the cross, perhaps the centurion who recognised Christ as the Son of God. The rich costumes and the lively gestures show the Gothic narrative taste of Paolo's mature work.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is worn and abraded, particularly in the later corners and around the arch, where the red bole shows through. The colours of the figures are well preserved, and the surface has a fine network of cracks.

  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano, <em>The Annunciation</em>, c. 1348–1350, gables of the wings of a portable triptych. Tempera and gold leaf on panel, 16.7 × 20.3 cm (both panels). Los Angeles, J. Paul Getty Museum, 87.PB.117. J. Paul Getty Museum, Open Content Program, public domain.</figcaption>

The two small panels, each shaped as half a gable, are joined by two small iron hinges and keep their original gilded frames. Together they measure 16.7 by 20.3 centimetres. They formed the upper parts of the wings of a portable triptych: when the triptych was closed, the angel and the Virgin met at the centre, and when it was open, they framed the central image from either side. The museum dates them to about 1348–1350.

In the left panel the angel Gabriel kneels in profile on a patch of rocky ground. He wears a pink robe, and his large wings, painted in bands of blue, white and gold, rise against the gold ground and fill the sloping edge of the panel. A blue ribbon is tied in his curly hair, and he holds a slender staff, the sign of the messenger, in his left hand.

Beside the angel, in red letters against the gold, the words of the angelic salutation are written: *Ave gratia plena Dominus tecum*, "Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with thee". The words appear to issue from the angel's mouth towards the Virgin in the opposite panel.

In the right panel the Virgin sits on a yellow throne with a red cushion, before a cloth of honour of gold and red brocade with a green lining. She wears a blue dress densely patterned with gold scrolls and a white veil over her fair hair. With her right hand raised before her breast she expresses surprise and acceptance, and in her left she holds a closed book.

A small white dove, the Holy Spirit, descends towards her from the upper left, along a gold ray. At the left of her panel stands a white stone parapet, drawn in perspective, from which a plant grows; a smaller plant grows in front of the angel. The two plants may allude to the flowering of the Virgin's purity.

The two halves of the scene are designed for the moment when the triptych was closed: the angel, kneeling at the left, and the Virgin, seated at the right, face each other across the hinge, and the salutation crosses from one panel to the other. The scale of the figures, their gestures and the gold ground make the small panels an intimate image for private devotion.

The figures are painted with great delicacy: the angel's face in pure profile, the fine gold patterns on the Virgin's dress, the cracks of the stone parapet and the leaves of the plants are rendered at a minute scale. The Gothic elegance of the poses and the bright colour belong to Paolo's work of the late 1340s.

The panels are in good condition. The gold ground is crossed by fine cracks and worn in places, the pink robe of the angel has a network of craquelure, and the frames show losses of gilding at the edges. The two panels are still joined by their iron hinges.

### The Pala Feriale, signed with Luca and Giovanni and dated 1345 (Venice, Museo di San Marco)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-10.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-10-zoom.jpg" alt="Two long horizontal panels, one above the other, each divided into seven compartments with gold grounds. In the upper row, half-length figures: from left to right Saint George with a lance and shield, Saint Mark with a book, the mourning Virgin in dark blue, the dead Christ standing upright before the cross with crossed hands, Saint John the Evangelist in blue and red, Saint Peter with the keys, and Saint Nicholas in a bishop's vestments with crosses. In the lower row, seven narrative scenes set in architecture and landscape: a consecration of a bishop, Saint Mark with a cobbler before a city with a tall tower, a prison with two haloed figures at a window and soldiers below, a scene of violence under a canopy, a ship with a large patterned sail in a storm beside red rocks, a crowd with clergy and a doge inside a church, and a scene with soldiers and a man on the ground before a building.">
  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano with his sons Luca and Giovanni, <em>Pala Feriale</em>, signed and dated 22 April 1345, the weekday cover of the Pala d'Oro of San Marco. Tempera and gold on panel, two panels, each c. 59 × 325 cm. Venice, Museo di San Marco. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Pala Feriale belongs to the history of the Pala d'Oro, the gold and enamel retable of the high altar of San Marco, which it was made to cover. The Pala d'Oro itself was the product of several campaigns. Commissioned in Constantinople under Doge Pietro I Orseolo at the end of the tenth century, it was remade and enlarged in 1105 under Doge Ordelaffo Falier, and enriched in 1209 with enamels brought from Constantinople after the Fourth Crusade. By the fourteenth century it was the most precious object in the possession of the Venetian state, a sum of Byzantine goldsmith's work and Venetian appropriation.

The decisive campaign for the Pala Feriale came in the 1340s. Andrea Dandolo, procurator of San Marco and from January 1343 doge, ordered in 1342 the enlargement and remounting of the Pala d'Oro, carried out by the Venetian goldsmith Giovanni Paolo Boninsegna, who gave it the Gothic form it still has. The procurators allocated funds in May 1343, and the painted cover was completed on 22 April 1345, the date inscribed on it. The two works, the goldsmith's retable and its painted cover, belong to a single programme of renewal of the high altar.

The function of the cover follows from the liturgy of San Marco. The Pala d'Oro was shown only on the great feasts of the church year; on ordinary days, the *giorni feriali*, it was turned or closed, and the faithful saw a painted image in its place. A *pala feriale* was therefore not a lesser substitute but a second face of the high altar, seen far more often than the gold retable it concealed. Its imagery had to speak to the daily congregation of the ducal church.

The commission carried high prestige for a painter in fourteenth-century Venice. San Marco was not the cathedral of Venice, which stood at San Pietro di Castello, but the chapel of the doge and the shrine of the city's patron saint, administered by the procurators on behalf of the state. To paint the cover of its high altar was to work at the centre of the Republic's religious and political image.

The inscription, *MCCCXLV mensis aprilis die XXII magister Paulus cum Luca et Iohanne filiis suis pinxerunt hoc opus*, presents the work as the product of a family workshop under the authority of its master. The precision of the date, to the day, is rare in Venetian painting of the period and matches the solemnity of the commission. The signature also records, for the first time, the names of two of Paolo's sons as painters.

The two registers combine two traditions of image. The upper row of half-length figures, with the dead Christ at the centre between the Virgin and John and four saints at the sides, takes up the Byzantine *Deesis* and the row of icons on the templon screen. The lower row of seven narrative scenes belongs to the Western tradition of the hagiographic cycle, told in sequence like the predella of a Gothic altarpiece.

The central image of the upper row, the dead Christ standing upright before the cross, is the *Imago Pietatis*, the Man of Sorrows. The type originated in Byzantium, where it was associated with the liturgy of Holy Week, and spread rapidly in the West in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as an image of private and Eucharistic devotion. Set above the altar, it presented the body of Christ directly over the place where the Mass was celebrated.

The choice of the saints in the upper row reflects the cults of San Marco and of the state. Mark, the patron of Venice, whose body lay in the church, appears beside the Virgin. Peter, the prince of the apostles, recalls the bond between the apostle and his disciple Mark. Nicholas, the patron of sailors, whose relics the Venetians claimed to possess at San Nicolò del Lido, and the warrior saint, generally identified as George, complete a group of protectors of a maritime republic.

The lower row tells the story of Mark from his mission in Alexandria to the presence of his relics in Venice. The legend joined the evangelist's life, preaching and martyrdom in Egypt, known from early Christian tradition, to the Venetian account of the theft of his body from Alexandria in 828 by two merchants, Buono da Malamocco and Rustico da Torcello, its transport by sea, and its arrival in the city. The cycle thus joins the universal history of the Church to the foundation legend of Venice.

The last scenes refer to the miraculous rediscovery of the body. During the rebuilding of the church in the eleventh century the location of the relics was lost, and according to Venetian tradition it was revealed in 1094, when, after prayers and fasting by the doge and the people, the body showed itself in a pier of the basilica. This *apparitio* was celebrated every year on 25 June and was a central episode in the civic identity of Venice.

The Mark stories had a long history in San Marco before Paolo. The mosaics of the chapels and of the façade, among them the thirteenth-century mosaic over the Porta di Sant'Alipio showing the body carried into the basilica, had already established a Venetian iconography of the saint. Paolo transferred this monumental tradition to the small scale of panel painting, with a new narrative vivacity.

The narrative scenes show how Paolo assimilated the spatial language of mainland painting. The architecture, opened like a stage, the ships with their sails and rigging, the crowds in the church and the soldiers in armour are rendered with an attention to circumstance that recalls the painting of Giotto's followers in Padua and Bologna. At the same time the gold grounds and the dark, burnished faces of the upper row keep the Byzantine character expected of an image for the high altar of San Marco.

The Pala Feriale remained in use for about a century. In the fifteenth century it was replaced by another painted cover, and Paolo's panels were removed from the altar. Their survival, unlike that of many medieval altarpieces of Venice dismantled or destroyed in later centuries, is due to their connection with the treasury and the museum of the basilica.

The panels, now displayed in the Museo di San Marco in the former Sala dei Banchetti, were restored in 1995 by Luigi Savio under the direction of Ettore Merkel of the Soprintendenza of Venice, with funding through Save Venice; a protective case was made at the same time. Each register is made up of seven compartments, now mounted as two long horizontal panels; the vertical bands between the compartments mark the divisions of the original structure, which was designed to be seen above the altar at a distance.

The Pala Feriale is often described as the beginning of Venetian narrative painting on panel. It is closely bound to the Venetian state, made for the high altar of the doge's church and signed by three members of his family. It shows the painter at the point where the Byzantine heritage of Venice and the Gothic narrative of the mainland were joined in a single work.

**Upper register.** *Saint George.* At the left a young warrior saint, with curly fair hair, wears a gold and dark garment over armour and holds a lance on his shoulder and a round shield. He is generally identified as Saint George; a strand of the literature has read him as Theodore, the earlier patron of Venice.

*Saint Mark.* The evangelist, with dark hair and beard, wears a red tunic and a dark mantle, raises his right hand in blessing and holds an open book, his Gospel. He stands next to the Virgin, on the left of the central group, as patron of the church.

*The Virgin.* The mourning Virgin, in a dark blue mantle drawn over her head, inclines towards Christ and holds out her right hand towards him. Her face, long and pale, expresses a restrained grief.

*The dead Christ.* At the centre Christ stands upright before the dark cross, naked to the waist, his head fallen on his shoulder, his eyes closed and his hands crossed before him, with the wounds visible. His body, modelled in pale ochre against the gold, is the focus of the whole cover.

*Saint John the Evangelist.* At the right of Christ the Evangelist, old and white-bearded, in a blue tunic and a red mantle, raises his right hand towards Christ in a gesture of lament and testimony.

*Saint Peter.* Peter, with short grey curly hair and beard, wears a blue tunic and a yellow mantle and holds his keys. His massive head and direct gaze give him a strong individual presence.

*Saint Nicholas.* At the right end Nicholas, bishop of Myra, bald and white-bearded, wears a red chasuble and a white omophorion marked with black crosses, in the Byzantine manner, and holds a red book.

**Lower register.** *A consecration.* In the first scene a haloed bishop in a red chasuble, holding a book, stands before a church with a domed façade, while kneeling and standing figures in red attend him. The scene is identified as Mark's appointment as bishop of Alexandria.

*Mark and the cobbler Anianus.* Before a city with a tall tower, perhaps the lighthouse of Alexandria, and a ship at its quay, Mark, haloed, sits between two figures. The scene is identified with the healing of the cobbler Anianus, who wounded his hand while mending Mark's sandal and became his first convert in Alexandria.

*Christ appears to Mark in prison.* A tall red building with arcades and a central window frames two haloed figures, Christ and Mark, seen inside the prison, while soldiers and guards stand and crouch outside, one beside a round shield.

*A scene of violence under a canopy.* Beneath a classical canopy, executioners raise their arms against a figure lying on the ground, while others flee at the right. The scene is related to Mark's martyrdom in Alexandria or to the miracle of the slave whom the saint protected from torture; the identification of this and the last panel varies in the literature.

*The ship in the storm.* A ship with a large white sail marked with black crosses rides on a dark sea beside red rocks, its crew gesturing in alarm. The scene shows the transport of Mark's body from Alexandria to Venice and the saint's intervention to calm the storm. An inscription runs along the bottom.

*The apparitio.* In a church with arches and columns, a crowd of clergy in mitres and laymen, among them the doge, gathers before a pier of the basilica, in which the body of the saint is revealed. The scene records the presence of the relics in San Marco, above whose altar the panel hung.

*The final scene.* Before a building with an arched portico, soldiers and figures stand around a man on the ground, at the right. The scene, damaged and of uncertain reading, is usually connected with the miracle of the slave or with the posthumous miracles of the saint.

### Madonna and Child with Eight Angels (Madonna della Pera), signed and dated 1347 (Cesena, Museo Diocesano e della Cattedrale)

The panel comes from the parish church of Santa Maria at Carpineta, in the hills near Cesena, and is now in the Museo Diocesano. The signature and date are written in white capitals on the base of the throne. Its popular name, *Madonna della Pera*, comes from the pear that the Virgin offers to the Child. It documents Paolo's work for a patron in the Romagna.

The Virgin sits on a tall wooden throne with a high back, crowned by Gothic pinnacles and finials, and a seat with a cushion. The throne is drawn in perspective, with its sides receding and its base projecting towards the viewer, and it occupies most of the panel. The pinnacles at the top rise into the gold ground and frame the Virgin's crowned head.

She wears a crown over a white veil and a dark blue mantle patterned with gold stars, which covers her head and falls over her knees to the base of the throne. Beneath it an orange dress is visible at the breast and sleeves. Her face is long and pale, with a thin nose and small mouth, and she inclines her head towards the Child.

In her right hand she holds out a pear to the Child. The fruit, a common attribute of the Virgin and Child in fourteenth-century painting, alludes to the Incarnation and to the sweetness of the Redemption, as the apple recalls the fall from which Christ redeems mankind.

The Child stands on his mother's lap, supported by her left arm, in a transparent shirt and a green cloak that leaves his legs bare. He reaches with both hands towards the pear and turns his head towards his mother. The standing pose and the naturalistic movement of the Child depart from the frontal formulas of Byzantine icons.

Eight small angels, in blue and red garments with coloured wings, stand behind and above the throne, four on each side, some looking down at the Virgin, some at the viewer. Their figures, set among the pinnacles of the throne, form a heavenly court that frames the central group.

The crowned Virgin, the elaborate throne and the angels repeat the type of the Madonna signed in 1340, adapted to a larger panel for a church outside Venice. The motif of the fruit, absent from the earlier model, adds a gesture of exchange between mother and Child.

The panel has been transferred from its original wooden support to canvas, and its surface shows losses, particularly at the edges and in the upper part of the gold ground. The blue of the mantle and the gold stars are well preserved.

### Crucifix with the Virgin and Saint John (Dubrovnik, Dominican Church)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-14.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-14-zoom.jpg" alt="A large painted cross hangs within the pointed stone arch of a church apse, set in a later surround of gilded openwork foliage that spreads around the arms and shaft. Christ hangs with his head inclined to his right and his body curving gently to the side, arms stretched almost horizontally, wearing a thin loincloth. Above his head a small darkened panel shows a haloed figure, and two carved gilded angels in flight flank the upper arm. The four terminals hold quatrefoil medallions with the symbols of the Evangelists: the eagle at the top, the winged lion at the left, the angel with a book at the right, the winged ox at the foot. Below, at either side, two separate narrow panels in gilded pinnacled frames show the mourning Virgin in a blue mantle, one hand raised to her face, at the left and Saint John the Evangelist in a pink mantle over blue at the right, both standing on gold grounds.">
  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano, <em>Crucifix with the mourning Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist</em>, c. 1350. Tempera and gold on panel. Dubrovnik, Dominican church (Sveti Dominik), triumphal arch. Photo: Sailko, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/">CC BY 3.0</a>, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The crucifix hangs in the opening of the triumphal arch of the Dominican church of Dubrovnik, in the position for which painted crosses were made in the Mendicant churches of the fourteenth century: high above the entrance to the choir, facing the nave and the lay congregation. More than five metres high, it is the most substantial evidence of Paolo's work for Dalmatian patrons.

The cross is a separate shaped panel, and the two mourners are painted on independent narrow panels set at either side, a solution that differs from the Tuscan type in which the Virgin and Saint John occupy the ends of the crossbar. The arrangement recalls the triumphal-arch groups of the Byzantine and Venetian tradition, where the cross and the mourners were displayed on the iconostasis beam as distinct images.

The figure of Christ is shown dead, with closed eyes and the head fallen on the right shoulder. The body bends in a moderate curve, and the ribs and abdominal muscles are rendered through soft gradations rather than through the linear schematism of earlier Venetian crosses. The thin loincloth, through which the legs show, follows models that had reached Venice from the Giottesque crucifixes of Padua and Rimini, combined with the elongated proportions and the restrained pathos of Paolo's Byzantine training.

The four terminals carry quatrefoil medallions with the symbols of the Evangelists: the eagle of John at the top, the lion of Mark at the left, the angel of Matthew at the right, the ox of Luke at the foot. Above the head of Christ a small panel, now darkened, holds a haloed figure in place of the titulus. The two angels in flight beside the upper arm are carved and gilded in the round and belong to the later surround. The scheme presents the Crucifixion as the centre of the Gospel witness, and in the church of the Order of Preachers it accords with the Dominican emphasis on the preaching of the Gospel.

The Virgin, wrapped in a blue maphorion, raises one hand to her face in the gesture of grief of Byzantine lament, and Saint John, in a pink mantle over a blue tunic, turns his head upwards towards the cross. Both stand on gold grounds within narrow arched fields. Their elongated, slightly swaying silhouettes and the soft falls of the drapery correspond to the figures of the mid-century polyptychs, and in particular to the standing saints of the Louvre polyptych of 1354.

The gilded openwork foliage that surrounds the cross and the pinnacled frames of the lateral panels are later additions, probably of the seventeenth or eighteenth century. They alter the original silhouette of the cross, which in the fourteenth century would have appeared against the stone of the arch with only its painted and gilded terminals.

The attribution rests on the stylistic comparison with Paolo's signed works and on its probable connection with the bequest of Šimun Rastić of 1348. A date around 1350 is consistent with both. The scale of the work and the quality of the head of Christ suggest the direct involvement of the master, although the extensive surfaces of the cross and of the lateral panels make the participation of the workshop likely.

Seen from the nave at a considerable height, the crucifix is difficult to study in detail. In its present state the painted surface reads clearly in the figure of Christ, in the medallions and in the two mourners, and the gold grounds of the lateral panels retain much of their original brilliance.

The four panels, each about 75 by 23.5 centimetres, are the lateral elements of a polyptych whose central image is lost. They came to the municipal collections of Brescia in 1866 on deposit from the Congregazione di Carità, the body that administered the property of suppressed pious institutions, and their original church is not documented. The Italian title under which the series is sometimes cited connects it with the church of Santi Cosma e Damiano in Brescia, but no early source confirms this provenance.

All four panels have lost their original tops. The plain upper sections are later insertions, and the outlines still visible in the gold ground, where the surface changes in tone above the heads, show that each saint stood under a pointed or trilobed arch, as in the lateral compartments of the Louvre polyptych of 1354. The present sequence, with the Baptist and Paul at the ends and the two bishops at the centre, is the arrangement of the museum display and need not reflect the original order.

Saint John the Baptist, emaciated, with dishevelled hair, wears the camel-hair tunic that shows at the chest and the legs under a brownish mantle fastened by a blue jewel. He points with his right hand towards the place where the central image would have stood and holds a long scroll with the words of his witness, *Ecce Agnus Dei*. The gesture confirms that his panel was placed to the left of the centre, facing it.

The two bishops, mitred and blessing, carry the richest ornament of the group. The first wears a green chasuble with a red orphrey, the second a red chasuble patterned in gold with a dark blue orphrey worked with interlace. Paolo renders the precious textiles with the same care given to the vestments of the saints in the Pala Feriale and in the Louvre polyptych, with gold patterns laid over the colour of the fabric.

The identity of the bishops is uncertain. They have been read as Ambrose and Augustine, the two Latin doctors, or as Filastrius and Apollonius, bishops of Brescia venerated in the city. The second reading would point to a commission for a Brescian church and would make the polyptych one of the few works sent by Paolo to Lombardy. Neither identification is supported by inscriptions or distinctive attributes.

Saint Paul, with the sword of his martyrdom and the book of the Epistles, closes the group at the right. The red mantle, drawn across the body in a broad sweep and turned back to show a green lining, is a fluent passage of drawing. The modelling of the face, with soft transitions from shadow to light, is close to the heads of the standing saints of the Louvre polyptych and of the Piran polyptych.

The attribution to Paolo was put forward by Giuseppe Fiocco in 1930–1931 and accepted by <a href="/scholars.html#berenson-bernard">Bernard Berenson</a>, Michelangelo Muraro and Filippo Pedrocco. Proposals for the date range from the fifth decade to the third quarter of the century. The proportions of the figures, the ample drapery and the richness of the textiles favour a date close to the Louvre polyptych, around 1350–1355.

The panels were restored by Mauro Pelliccioli in 1951. The figures are well preserved, with the green ground strips, the vestments and the faces intact, while the gold grounds show abrasion along the edges and around the lost arches.

### Madonna of the Poppy (Venice, San Pantalon, Chapel of the Sacred Nail)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-16.jpg" data-zoom-src="/Images/Paolo-Veneziano/paolo-veneziano-16-zoom.jpg" alt="Half-length Virgin and Child on a gold ground under a rounded arch, photographed before cleaning with the bare edges of the panel visible. The Child, seated on her left arm, wears a short red tunic with an embroidered gold panel on the chest and white undersleeves, looks up at his mother and reaches for the flower. In the spandrels above the arch carved and gilded acanthus leaves on a red ground frame two roundels, each with a small half-length figure on gold.">
  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano, <em>Madonna and Child (Madonna of the Poppy)</em>, c. 1350. Tempera and gold on panel, 98 × 74 cm. Venice, church of San Pantalon, Chapel of the Sacred Nail. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel, 98 by 74 centimetres, hangs in the Chapel of the Sacred Nail in San Pantalon, beside the four scenes from the life of the Virgin. The Virgin is shown at half length under a rounded arch, holding the Child on her left arm. An old photograph, taken with the panel out of its frame, shows its bare edges, the nail holes and the losses along the borders.

The Virgin wears a dark blue mantle sown with small gold stars, drawn over her head and edged with a gold band that frames the face. Her head inclines towards the Child, but her gaze is turned to the viewer, in the manner of the Byzantine Hodegetria, who points the faithful towards her Son. The face, oval and smooth, with a long straight nose and a small mouth, is modelled with softer transitions than those of Paolo's early Madonnas.

In her right hand the Virgin holds a red poppy by its stem. The poppy, a flower associated with sleep and death and with the blood of the Passion, announces the sacrifice of Christ, and the Child, who reaches out to touch it, accepts his destiny. The motif belongs to the repertory of the devotional Madonnas of the fourteenth century, in which a flower or a fruit offered to the Child carries a symbolic charge.

The Child is not swaddled in the Byzantine manner but dressed in a short red tunic, with white undersleeves and an embroidered panel of gold on a dark ground at the chest, and his legs are bare. He sits on a dark cloth over his mother's arm, turns his head upwards to look at her and extends his hand towards the flower. The pose, natural and lively, reflects the knowledge of mainland models that Paolo assimilated in the middle years of his career.

Above the arch, the spandrels are filled with carved and gilded acanthus leaves on a red ground, framing two roundels with small half-length figures on gold, probably angels. The carving is in relief and may belong to a later reframing of the panel; its relation to the original structure has not been established.

The pairing of a hieratic, frontal gaze with the affectionate exchange between mother and Child places the panel among the works in which Paolo reconciled the Byzantine icon with the Western devotional image. The palette, reduced to the dark blue of the mantle, the red of the tunic and the flower, and the gold of the ground, concentrates attention on the two figures and on the symbolic object between them.

The panel is generally dated around 1350. The fuller modelling of the Child and the softness of the Virgin's face support a date in Paolo's maturity, close to the Madonnas of the later 1340s and the 1350s.

The panel shows lifting and losses of paint, worm damage and cracks, especially along the edges and in the gold ground, where the bole shows through in several places. The figures themselves are relatively well preserved.

  <figcaption>Paolo Veneziano (attributed) or workshop, <em>The Crucifixion</em>, mid-fourteenth century. Tempera and gold on panel, 109 × 60.5 cm. Athens, Byzantine and Christian Museum, BXM 01097. Image: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>

The panel, 109 by 60.5 centimetres, with a rounded top, is preserved in the Byzantine and Christian Museum in Athens. Its earlier history and original destination are not known. The format, tall and arched, suggests an independent devotional image or the central element of a small altarpiece rather than a narrative compartment, and the size places it between the portable triptychs and the large polyptychs of Paolo's workshop.

The composition follows the type of the crowded Crucifixion, the *Crucifixion with many figures* that became widespread in Italian painting during the fourteenth century. The cross rises at the centre to the top of the panel, against a gold ground closed above by a band of dark sky with stars. Behind it runs the crenellated wall of Jerusalem, painted in dark red with gold scrolls, which divides the heavenly zone of the angels from the earthly zone of the crowd.

Christ hangs dead, his head fallen on his right shoulder, his body long and pale, the loincloth transparent over the thighs. Small angels circle the cross, some covering their faces, some raising their arms in lament, and two collect in chalices the blood flowing from his hands. The motif of the angels gathering the blood, of Western origin, gives the scene a Eucharistic meaning.

At the lower left the Virgin, in a dark mantle bordered with gold, swoons and is supported by the holy women and by Saint John, in a pale yellow mantle. Mary Magdalene, with her red mantle and long golden hair loose on her shoulders, kneels at the foot of the cross and embraces it. The fainting Virgin and the Magdalene at the cross, prominent in Franciscan devotional literature and in Tuscan painting, are here among the emotional centres of the image.

The upper part of both sides is filled with soldiers on horseback, their helmets and spears forming a dense mass against the wall. At the right a mounted figure with a halo points towards Christ: the centurion who recognised him as the Son of God, sometimes identified with Longinus. Below him, the crowd of the right side includes a soldier with a round shield, a group of men dividing Christ's garment, and at the edge two old men in conversation, a motif drawn from Byzantine representations of the scene.

The image thus combines two traditions. The gold ground, the type of Christ and the conversing elders derive from Byzantine painting, while the crowded composition, the horsemen, the fainting Virgin and the Magdalene at the cross belong to Western Gothic art. This fusion is characteristic of Paolo's production around the middle of the century and of the works destined for clients who expected both languages.

The attribution oscillates between Paolo himself and his workshop. The quality of the head of Christ and of the group of the holy women is high, while some heads in the crowd at the right are more summary. The repetition of types familiar from Paolo's narrative scenes points to the use of workshop models, and the panel offers an example of how such models were combined in a larger composition. A date around the middle of the century is consistent with both readings.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is well preserved, with some abrasion around the edges, while the dark band of sky at the top and parts of the lower edge have suffered losses. The colours of the mantles, especially the reds and the dark blue of the Virgin, remain intense.

The polyptych consists of a taller central panel with the Virgin and Child enthroned and two lateral panels, each with two standing saints under paired arches. It is dated 1354 by an inscription on the base of the throne. The three panels keep their carved frames, with round arches, twisted colonnettes and a base decorated with foliage, which give a clear idea of the appearance of a mid-fourteenth-century Venetian altarpiece of moderate size.

The structure follows the type that Paolo developed for the Venetian altarpiece: a central image of the Virgin flanked by single standing saints, each framed as a separate compartment within a continuous architectural frame. Here the lateral saints are grouped in pairs on single planks, which reduces the number of separate panels and simplifies the carpentry.

The four saints reflect a Franciscan context. At the left stand Saint Francis, in a brown habit, holding a red book and a small cross, and Saint John the Baptist, in a green mantle over his camel-hair garment, holding a scroll. At the right stand Saint John the Evangelist, white-bearded, in a blue tunic and a red mantle, reading an open book, and Saint Anthony of Padua, young, in a brown habit, holding a red book. Their names are inscribed in red against the gold beside their heads.

The two Franciscan saints are placed at the outer ends, the two Johns next to the Virgin. The symmetry is reinforced by colour: the brown habits of the friars frame the composition, while the green of the Baptist and the red and blue of the Evangelist introduce brighter tones near the centre.

The saints stand on a dark ground, frontal or slightly turned towards the centre, with long, narrow bodies and heads tilted gently forward. Their faces, with long noses, heavy-lidded eyes and dark shadows, keep the Byzantine character of Paolo's figures, while the soft fall of the drapery and the slight turn of the bodies show the Gothic elements of his style.

The relation between the central panel and the laterals is one of scale and colour. The Virgin, larger and set on a throne that projects towards the viewer, dominates the ensemble, while the saints, smaller and placed on a flat ground, form a frieze around her. The red cloth of honour behind the Virgin and the dark blue of her mantle are echoed in the garments of the Evangelist.

The polyptych shows how Paolo's shop adapted the large carpentered altarpiece to a more modest scale, with five figures instead of the many compartments of the great polyptychs, while keeping the same structure of a dominant central image and a frieze of saints.

The polyptych is in good condition. The gold grounds are worn and scratched in places, particularly in the lateral panels, and the surface has a fine network of cracks. The colours of the garments are well preserved. The frames show small losses of gilding.

The central panel shows the Virgin enthroned with the Child, under a round arch carried on twisted colonnettes. On the front of the throne's base runs the inscription with the date, *MCCCLIIII M D T*, in small capitals. The inscription gives only the year and a group of initials; it carries no name.

The Virgin sits on a throne of pink and white marble, seen slightly from above, with a projecting step and two green cushions at the sides. Behind her hangs a cloth of honour of red and gold brocade patterned with small dark crosses, which fills the upper part of the panel and frames her head and shoulders against the gold.

She wears a crown set with stones over a white veil, and a dark blue mantle densely patterned with gold palmettes, which covers her from head to feet and falls over the step in a long, curving hem. Beneath it is a red dress patterned in gold, and at her breast a round gold brooch. The patterned mantle, which almost conceals the body beneath it, is a characteristic feature of Venetian painting, derived from Byzantine and Eastern textiles.

Her face, under the crown, is long and pale, with a thin nose, almond eyes and rose cheeks, and her head is gently inclined. She holds out her right hand, towards the Child, while her left hand supports him at the waist.

The Child sits on her left knee, in a white shirt and an olive-green cloak with a gold border, his right arm stretched towards his mother's hand and his gaze lowered. His halo is incised with rays. The gesture, the exchange between the hands of mother and child, gives the solemn, frontal image a moment of intimacy.

The throne, with its pink frame, its white seat and its stepped base, is constructed with an attempt at depth: its sides recede, and the step projects towards the viewer. This spatial construction, combined with the flat, ornamental surface of the mantle and the cloth of honour, is typical of Paolo's mature works, in which Gothic space and Byzantine ornament coexist.

The type derives from the *Madonna* signed in 1340 now in the Crespi collection, with the Virgin enthroned before a cloth of honour, which the workshop repeated with variations. Here the crown, the dense pattern of the mantle and the large scale of the figure in relation to the throne give the image a particularly regal character.

The panel is in good condition. The gold ground is crossed by scratches and small losses, particularly at the upper right, and the surface has a fine network of cracks. The blue and gold of the mantle and the brocade of the cloth of honour are well preserved.

The left panel of the polyptych contains two standing saints, each under his own round arch, separated by a twisted colonnette. The arches, with their cusped and foliated intrados, and the base decorated with a running leaf pattern belong to the original carved frame. The panel shows at a larger scale the figures that, in the whole altarpiece, frame the Virgin.

Saint Francis stands frontally in a brown habit, girded with a knotted cord, his hood thrown back. He holds a red book with metal clasps against his body and, in his right hand, a small gold cross. His face, with a short beard, a tonsure and dark, deep-set eyes, is grave and still. His name, *S. Franciscus*, is inscribed in red capitals beside his head.

Saint John the Baptist stands beside him, gaunt, with long dark hair and a beard, in a green mantle thrown over a dark camel-hair garment, which shows at his chest and legs. With his right hand he points towards the centre of the altarpiece, where the Child sat on his mother's lap.

In his left hand the Baptist holds a long unrolled scroll inscribed *Ecce agnus Dei, ecce qui tollis peccata*, an adaptation of the words with which he pointed out Christ in the Gospel of John. The scroll, curving down beside his body, links his gesture to the figure of the Child in the central panel. His name, *Iohes Bap[tista]*, is inscribed beside his head.

The two figures contrast in colour and pose: Francis, in his plain brown habit, stands still and compact, while the Baptist, in green, is thin and elongated, his body slightly turned and his feet set apart. The difference expresses the two types of sanctity, the recent Franciscan saint and the prophet of the desert.

Both figures stand barefoot on a dark ground, against a gold ground that is incised with the outlines of their haloes. The drapery is modelled with soft transitions and few, long folds, and the faces keep the dark shadows and strong contours of Paolo's Byzantine heritage.

The faces and the repetitive treatment of the drapery, close to those of the right panel, show the workshop practice of reusing figure types. Francis, in particular, closely resembles Anthony of Padua on the opposite panel, so that the two Franciscan friars mirror each other at the ends of the altarpiece.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is crossed by numerous scratches, especially around the figure of Francis, and the surface has a network of cracks. The colours of the garments and the inscription on the scroll are well preserved, and the carved frame shows small losses.

The right panel of the polyptych measures 83.4 by 52.5 centimetres and contains Saint John the Evangelist, nearest the Virgin, and Saint Anthony of Padua at the outer edge. The museum record, which dates it to the third quarter of the fourteenth century, catalogues it as the right lateral of the altarpiece whose central panel bears the date 1354.

Saint John the Evangelist is represented, according to the Byzantine tradition, as an old man, bald, with a high forehead and a curly white beard. He bends his head over an open book which he holds in both hands, absorbed in reading. The book shows the opening words of his Gospel, *In principio*, "In the beginning was the Word".

He wears a blue tunic with a gold border at the neck and a large red mantle lined in green, which is drawn over his left shoulder and falls in long, angular folds to his feet. The slight forward bend of his body, the inclination of his head and the movement of the mantle give the figure a sense of inner concentration that distinguishes it from the frontal, motionless friar beside him.

Saint Anthony of Padua, the Franciscan preacher who died in 1231, is shown young, tonsured, with a short beard, in a brown habit with a hood and a knotted cord. He stands frontally and holds against his body a red book with clasps, the sign of his learning and preaching. His face, with large dark eyes and a small mouth, looks straight out at the viewer.

The names of the two saints are inscribed in red capitals beside their heads, *Iohes Evg* and *S. Antonius*. Like the scroll of the Baptist on the left panel, the inscriptions help the viewer to identify the saints at a distance and give the polyptych a legible, almost didactic character.

The two figures show two contrasting modes of Paolo's art. The Evangelist, with his expressive head and his elaborate drapery, belongs to the Byzantine tradition of the apostle portrait, enlivened by Gothic movement. The friar, with his simple silhouette and his plain brown habit, follows the more recent type of the mendicant saint, rendered with solemn frontality.

The Evangelist's book is painted with care: the gilded edges of the pages, the clasps of the binding and the lettering in red and black are rendered at a minute scale. The open book, turned partly towards the viewer, makes the words of the Gospel legible to those who stood before the altar.

The panel is in fair condition. The gold ground is crossed by numerous fine scratches, particularly around the figure of Anthony, and the surface has a network of cracks. The colours of the garments, the red and blue of the Evangelist and the brown of the friar, are well preserved, and the carved frame shows small losses.

The panel, 109.9 by 68.6 centimetres, was probably the central panel of a large altarpiece. The composition is built on a strict symmetry, broken only by the gesture of Christ, whose arm crosses from one half of the panel to the other.

Christ and the Virgin sit side by side on a broad throne of pink and red architecture, with a high back crowned by two pointed gables, each set with a blue roundel. The architecture, with its arches, pilasters and Gothic gables, is drawn with lively colour and gives the composition a monumental frame. Between the two figures hangs a cloth of honour of gold with a delicate pattern, which fills the space between their haloes and the seat.

At the right Christ, with long reddish hair and beard, wears a gold tunic and a deep blue mantle with a gold border, and holds in his left hand a slender cross-staff. With his right arm he reaches across to place a jewelled crown on the head of his mother, its gold merging with the gold of her halo, punched with a fine pattern. His gaze, directed towards her, and the long diagonal of his arm unite the two halves of the composition.

At the left the Virgin, in a deep blue mantle over a gold dress, inclines her head to receive the crown and crosses her hands on her breast in a gesture of humility. Her pale face, with rose cheeks and lowered eyes, has the delicate, melancholy expression of Paolo's Madonnas. The two blue mantles, almost identical in colour and border, make mother and son a matched pair.

Above and around the throne a choir of angels with red and gold wings plays instruments: fiddles, lutes, a psaltery, trumpets and a tambourine, while two angels at the sides of the throne hold portative organs. At the feet of the two figures lie a dark disc with a face surrounded by rays and a pale face with curly hair, the sun and the moon, signs of the cosmic dimension of the Virgin's coronation as Queen of Heaven. The inscriptions on the steps of the throne, in gold letters on red and green bands, form part of the painted architecture.

The angels, crowded above and beside it, are each given individual poses and instruments, while the faces repeat a single type. The share of Giovanni in the execution cannot be distinguished from his father's hand.

The panels come from the high altar of Santa Maria del Mercato, the Dominican church of San Severino Marche, and are now in the town's Pinacoteca civica. They form the two wings of a polyptych whose central panel left the Marche long ago. In the present display the wings are set side by side, so that the eight saints of the main register form a continuous row and the gap left by the lost centre is closed.

The main register presents eight standing saints under round arches carried by twisted colonnettes. From left to right they are Catherine of Alexandria, Michael the Archangel, John the Baptist, Peter, Paul, an apostle identified by inscription as Philip, Dominic and Ursula. The order follows a clear hierarchy: the apostles Peter and Paul stood nearest to the centre, flanked by the Baptist and the young apostle, while the two virgin martyrs closed the ends of the altarpiece.

Above the main register runs a band of carved shell-shaped lunettes, and above the outer compartments rise two groups of three gabled pinnacles, each with a half-length saint. At the left are a bishop in a blue chasuble sown with gold stars, a young saint holding a red banner and the model of a walled town, perhaps Severinus, patron of the city, and Peter Martyr, with the palm of his martyrdom. At the right are Thomas Aquinas, with rays issuing from his breast, an open book and the model of a church, and two apostles with scrolls, one of them named as Thomas. The six half-length figures of the upper tier and the eight saints of the main register make fourteen saints in all.

The programme is shaped by its Dominican destination. Dominic stands in the main register, with the lily of purity and the book of his preaching, and two later saints of the order, Peter Martyr and Thomas Aquinas, occupy the upper tier. Thomas, canonised in 1323, holds the book of his doctrine and a church, emblem of the order he sustained by his teaching. The local saint with the town model joins the order to the city in which the convent stood.

The figures are tall and slender, with small heads and long, flowing mantles that fall in ample curves to the ground. Michael, in a gilded cuirass over a red cloak, plants his spear in the dragon at his feet with a graceful turn of the body. Catherine and Ursula wear mantles lined with ermine and patterned in gold, and Ursula gathers two of her companions under her cloak, an allusion to the eleven thousand virgins of her legend.

The carving of the frame is largely preserved: the twisted colonnettes, the trilobed arches with crockets, the band of shells and the pinnacles with their gables. It shows the type of gilded wooden structure that Venetian workshops produced for altarpieces sent to the Adriatic coast, and that Paolo had developed in the Santa Chiara polyptych.

The connection of these wings with the *Coronation* in the Frick Collection, signed by Paolo and his son Giovannino and dated 1358, was proposed in 1977 and is now widely accepted, though not proven. If the reconstruction is correct, the polyptych is among the last works of Paolo, painted with the collaboration of his son. The precious textiles, the elongated proportions and the soft colour of the saints agree with the Frick panel.

The panels are in good condition. The gold grounds and the colours of the mantles are well preserved, and the frame has lost only some of its crockets and the finials of the pinnacles. The gap between the two wings and the lack of pinnacles above the inner compartments suggest that the upper tier was once more extensive.

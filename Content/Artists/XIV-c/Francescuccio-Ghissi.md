*Francescuccio Ghissi* was a painter of the 14th-century Italian Gothic tradition, working between the established workshop practices of his era and the stylistic developments of later medieval art. Born in Fabriano in the Marche region of central-eastern Italy, Ghissi emerged from the fertile artistic environment of this renowned paper-making city during the mid-14th century, though his exact birth date remains undocumented in surviving records. The earliest documentary evidence of his professional activity appears in 1345, when the Confraternita di Santa Maria del Mercato in Fabriano recorded a payment to "Francescuccio di Cicco," establishing the first chronological anchor for understanding his artistic trajectory.

His name appears in various forms throughout documented sources, including "Francesco di Cecco Ghissi" and "Franciscutius Cicchi," reflecting the fluid naming conventions of medieval Italian artists. The artistic milieu of Fabriano during Ghissi's formative years was dominated by the Gothic style, particularly as interpreted through the influential workshop of [Allegretto Nuzi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Allegretto-Nuzi.html), which would prove instrumental in shaping the young artist's aesthetic vocabulary. His documented activity spans from 1345 to 1374, the date of the signed Madonna of Humility at Montegiorgio. A painter named Franceschino di Francesco of Fabriano is recorded as resident in Perugia in 1389, but his identification with Francescuccio is not secure.

Evidence suggests that Ghissi maintained an active workshop throughout his career, producing works that demonstrate both individual artistic vision and adherence to the established conventions of Gothic painting in the Marche region. His career, documented over almost three decades, testifies to his professional success and the sustained demand for his particular interpretation of contemporary religious iconography. The painter's death date and circumstances remain unrecorded. His last fixed point is the Montegiorgio panel of 1374; a Madonna of Humility dated 1395 and signed by a "Franciscus" is usually given to a namesake.

## Family and Workshop Organization

The genealogical and familial context surrounding Francescuccio Ghissi remains largely obscure in surviving documentary sources, reflecting the common challenge of reconstructing the personal lives of medieval artisans despite their professional prominence. The name "Ghissi" has long been treated as a family surname. [Matteo Mazzalupi](/scholars.html#mazzalupi-matteo) has argued, however, that the form CHISSI in the Montegiorgio signature of 1374 is a variant of the same patronymic as CICCHI in the signature of 1359, so that both refer to his father Cecco. No details about his parents, siblings or children survive. The variation in his recorded name, particularly the appearance of "di Cecco" or "di Cicco," indicates that his father's name was likely Cecco (a diminutive form of Francesco), following the common Italian patronymic naming convention of the period.

Other painters with similar names appear in later fourteenth-century Fabriano records, which complicates the reconstruction of his career. A Madonna dell'Umiltà formerly in the Fornari collection in Fabriano and now in the Carminati collection at Gallarate is signed "Francisc[u]s me fecit" and dated 1395. Its much weaker quality has led most scholars to keep it apart from his catalogue and to give it to a namesake, identified by some with Franceschino di Francesco (also called Franceschino di Cicco), who is documented in 1396–97 and 1399 working for the Chiavelli, lords of Fabriano. This uncertainty reflects the complex dynamics of medieval workshop inheritance, where artistic techniques and commissions often passed between generations within artisan families. The absence of detailed family records may indicate that the Ghissi household, while professionally successful, did not achieve the level of social prominence that would have ensured extensive documentation of their private affairs. In medieval Italian towns, artisan families often lived in clustered neighborhoods near their workshops, creating extended networks of professional and familial relationships that facilitated the transmission of artistic knowledge. The potential existence of painters with similar names raises important questions about the organization of his workshop and the extent to which family members participated in the production of signed works. Contemporary records from other medieval workshops indicate that sons, brothers, and nephews frequently served as apprentices and collaborators, gradually assuming greater responsibility for major commissions as senior family members aged.

The professional establishment of Francescuccio Ghissi's workshop appears to have followed conventional patterns of medieval artistic organization, with the master painter overseeing a structured hierarchy of assistants, apprentices, and specialized craftsmen. Evidence from his documented works suggests that his atelier maintained consistent standards of technical execution and iconographic interpretation throughout his documented career, implying effective management of collaborative production processes. The workshop's location in Fabriano positioned it advantageously within the broader network of Marchigian artistic centers, facilitating access to both local ecclesiastical patronage and regional commercial opportunities.

The 1345 payment from a Fabriano confraternity suggests that Ghissi worked early on producing devotional images for lay religious organizations, a lucrative market segment within 14th-century Italian art. The consistent quality of works attributed to his hand suggests that he maintained direct oversight of major commissions while delegating preparatory work and minor details to qualified assistants. Evidence of collaborative relationships with other established masters, particularly Allegretto Nuzi, indicates that Ghissi's workshop participated in larger artistic enterprises that required coordination between multiple professional studios. The geographic distribution of his works across multiple locations in the Marche suggests that his workshop developed efficient systems for transporting completed panels and coordinating installation at distant ecclesiastical sites.

Comparison of the surviving works suggests consistent approaches to panel preparation, gilding and tooling, pointing to standardized workshop procedures that ensured reliable results across different commissions. The surviving works suggest that Ghissi's workshop remained active for some decades, requiring effective systems for training new personnel and maintaining institutional knowledge as staff members matured and potentially established independent practices. The apparent specialization in Madonna dell'Umiltà compositions indicates that the workshop developed particular expertise in this iconographically complex subject, potentially creating efficiency advantages through repetition and refinement of established compositional formulas.

## Ecclesiastical and Civic Patronage

The patronage network supporting Francescuccio Ghissi's artistic career demonstrates the complex interplay between ecclesiastical institutions, civic organizations, and private devotional practice characteristic of 14th-century Italian religious culture. His earliest documented commission, facilitated by the Confraternita di Santa Maria del Mercato<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-confraternita-santa-maria-mercato-fabriano" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> in Fabriano, establishes the importance of lay religious organizations in sustaining artistic production within smaller Italian cities. The confraternity system provided institutional support for artists like Ghissi, offering regular commissions for devotional images that served both liturgical and social functions within urban religious life.

Documentary evidence indicates that multiple churches in Fabriano commissioned works from Ghissi's workshop, including Santa Lucia (also known as San Domenico), suggesting his recognition as a preferred artist for local ecclesiastical projects. The patronage of mendicant orders, particularly the Dominicans and Augustinians, appears to have been especially significant in Ghissi's career development, as these communities actively promoted specific devotional iconographies that aligned with his artistic specializations.

His signed Madonna dell'Umiltà of 1359, originally created for the church of Santa Lucia in Fabriano, exemplifies the type of devotional commission that formed the backbone of his professional practice. The distribution of his works across multiple ecclesiastical sites in the Marche region, including churches in Montegiorgio (the Madonna signed and dated 1374 at Sant'Andrea), Ascoli Piceno (the signed Madonna of Humility at Sant'Agostino), and Fermo, indicates a patronage network that extended well beyond his home city. The commissioning of altarpieces for specific chapel dedications, such as the Saint John Altarpiece, demonstrates the artist's ability to create complex narrative cycles that satisfied sophisticated theological and liturgical requirements. Evidence suggests that Ghissi's workshop developed particular expertise in creating works for Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> communities: the Montegiorgio panel of 1374 comes from Sant'Agostino, the signed Madonna at Ascoli Piceno is in Sant'Agostino, and the triptych with Saints Nicholas of Tolentino, Augustine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustine-of-hippo" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> and Stephen carries an Augustinian programme. The genesis of the Madonna of Humility type has itself been linked to the Augustinian order. The consistent iconographic program evident in his Madonna dell'Umiltà series suggests coordinated patronage efforts, possibly reflecting broader theological campaigns promoting specific aspects of Marian doctrine among lay populations.

Regional ecclesiastical networks may have extended Ghissi's activity beyond Fabriano. Umbrian echoes in his style have suggested a stay in Perugia, where his hand has been recognized in the frescoes of the Guidalotti chapel in San Domenico and of the chapel of Saint Anthony Abbot in Sant'Agostino; the 1389 record of a Franceschino di Francesco of Fabriano in the city, however, cannot be securely referred to him. Work in Fabriano cathedral is reported by local historians and by the attribution of frescoes there to Nuzi's workshop, in which Ghissi may have taken part, but no documented commission survives. Civic patronage appears to have complemented ecclesiastical commissions, though specific examples of municipal or governmental projects remain less well-documented in surviving sources.

The creation of works for abbatial communities, including the polyptych for the abbey of Santa Maria dell'Appennino, indicates patronage relationships with monastic institutions that possessed substantial resources for artistic commissions. Analysis of his documented works suggests that Ghissi's patrons valued both artistic quality and iconographic innovation, as his Madonna dell'Umiltà compositions incorporate sophisticated theological symbolism that reflects educated clerical input. The geographic spread of his commissions implies the existence of intermediary networks, possibly including traveling clergy or merchant connections, that facilitated introductions between the artist and distant potential patrons. Evidence of collaborative projects with other established masters suggests that some commissions involved consortiums of patrons who coordinated funding for major artistic enterprises requiring multiple workshops.

The repainting of the 1359 Madonna by the Master of Collamato, and its restoration by Giovan Battista De Magistris in 1674, recorded by an inscription on the back of the panel, indicate the continued value placed on his artistic contributions by later generations of ecclesiastical patrons. The preservation of signed and dated works suggests that his patrons recognized the importance of artistic attribution, possibly reflecting growing awareness of individual artistic identity within ecclesiastical commissioning practices. The predominance of panel paintings over fresco decoration in his surviving output, beside the frescoes attributed to him in the chapter hall of San Domenico in Fabriano, may reflect specific liturgical requirements or practical considerations expressed by his ecclesiastical patrons.

## Gothic Style and Technical Mastery

Francescuccio Ghissi's artistic style demonstrates a sophisticated synthesis of Gothic conventions with regional Marchigian traditions, creating a distinctive visual language that balanced decorative elegance with spiritual intensity. His approach to figure modeling reflects the Gothic preference for elongated proportions and graceful linear rhythms, though his interpretation often exhibits a characteristic flatness that distinguishes his work from that of his contemporaries. The decorative sensibility evident in his compositions aligns with broader Gothic aesthetic principles, emphasizing ornamental detail, rich color harmonies, and luxurious surface treatments that enhanced the devotional impact of religious imagery.

Technical analysis of his works reveals consistent approaches to panel preparation and ground application that demonstrate mastery of traditional tempera painting methods prevalent in 14th-century Italian workshops. His use of gold backgrounds in major works follows established Gothic conventions while incorporating subtle variations in application and tooling that reflect individual artistic interpretation. The linear quality of his drawing style, particularly evident in drapery treatment and facial features, demonstrates sophisticated understanding of Gothic draftsmanship principles while maintaining distinctive personal characteristics.

Color selection in his documented works reveals preference for rich, saturated hues typical of Gothic painting, including deep blues, vibrant reds, and lustrous gold accents that create visually striking devotional images. His approach to spatial organization reflects Gothic conventions of hierarchical arrangement and symbolic rather than naturalistic spatial relationships, prioritizing spiritual significance over empirical observation. The iconographic sophistication evident in his Madonna dell'Umiltà series demonstrates deep understanding of contemporary theological developments and their visual expression within Gothic artistic traditions. Surface decoration in his works exhibits the Gothic fascination with intricate patterning, evident in textile representations, architectural details, and ornamental borders that enhance overall compositional richness.

The evolution of Ghissi's style throughout his documented career reveals both consistency in fundamental approach and subtle development in technical sophistication and compositional complexity. His early works, particularly the 1359 Madonna dell'Umiltà, establish characteristic features including delicate facial modeling, flowing linear rhythms, and careful attention to decorative detail that would remain constant throughout his production. Comparative analysis with contemporary Gothic masters reveals Ghissi's particular sensitivity to surface pattern and ornamental enhancement, creating works that satisfy both devotional requirements and aesthetic appreciation. His treatment of religious narrative, evident in the Saint John Altarpiece series, demonstrates ability to adapt Gothic stylistic conventions to complex storytelling requirements while maintaining visual coherence across multiple panels.

The architectural elements incorporated into his compositions reflect Gothic sensibilities regarding spatial definition and symbolic representation, using structural details to enhance rather than compete with figural elements. His approach to landscape elements, though limited by Gothic conventions, shows subtle awareness of natural observation while maintaining primary emphasis on spiritual rather than physical reality. The consistent quality maintained across works produced over several decades indicates systematic workshop methods that preserved technical standards while allowing for individual expression. Analysis of his drawing technique reveals sophisticated understanding of linear expression, using varied line weights and rhythmic patterns to create dynamic visual effects within overall compositional stability. His palette choices demonstrate awareness of contemporary color theory and symbolic associations, employing specific hue combinations to enhance devotional meaning and emotional response. The integration of contemporary Gothic ornamental vocabulary with traditional Italian painting techniques creates a distinctive synthesis that distinguishes his work within the broader context of 14th-century Marchigian art.

## Artistic Influences and Formation

The artistic formation of Francescuccio Ghissi appears to have been fundamentally shaped by his association with the workshop of Allegretto Nuzi, one of the most prominent painters active in the Marche during the mid-14th century. This relationship, initially characterized by direct collaboration and subsequently evolving into independent practice, provided Ghissi with access to sophisticated artistic techniques and established patronage networks that facilitated his professional development. Documentary and stylistic evidence suggests that Ghissi worked as a collaborator or assistant in Nuzi's workshop during the 1360s and 1370s, participating in major commissions that exposed him to complex iconographic programs and advanced painting techniques.

The influence of Nuzi's approach to figure modeling, compositional organization, and decorative treatment remains evident throughout Ghissi's independent career, though filtered through his own artistic sensibility and technical preferences. [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico)'s scholarly analysis has identified specific works where Ghissi's hand can be distinguished within larger collaborative projects, revealing his gradual assumption of greater responsibility for major commissions as his technical skills matured. The Madonna dell'Umiltà iconography that became central to Ghissi's artistic identity appears to have been transmitted through the Nuzi workshop tradition, though Ghissi's interpretation incorporated distinctive personal elements that distinguished his works from direct copies.

Beyond the immediate influence of Allegretto Nuzi, Ghissi's style demonstrates awareness of broader currents in 14th-century Italian painting, including echoes of Giottesque narrative: in the chapter-hall frescoes of San Domenico in Fabriano he quotes a passage from the Franciscan cycle at Assisi, and he knew the manner of Puccio di Simone and of Andrea da Bologna. Echoes of Umbrian painting in his work have suggested a prolonged stay in Perugia. The technical sophistication evident in his panel paintings indicates familiarity with established workshop practices developed in major artistic centers, possibly acquired through direct observation or traveling journeymen who transmitted advanced techniques. Analysis of his drawing style reveals influences from manuscript illumination traditions, suggesting possible exposure to the sophisticated decorative arts that flourished in centers like Fabriano and other Marchigian cities.

The broader context of 14th-century Italian painting provided Ghissi with access to diverse stylistic influences that shaped his artistic development beyond his immediate workshop training. Evidence suggests familiarity with Florentine artistic developments, particularly the linear grace and narrative sophistication associated with followers of Giotto, though filtered through regional interpretations rather than direct contact. His understanding of Gothic conventions indicates awareness of artistic developments occurring north of the Alps, possibly transmitted through manuscript examples, portable objects, or traveling artists. The sophisticated iconographic programs evident in his religious works suggest access to advanced theological texts and scholarly interpretation that influenced his visual representation of complex doctrinal concepts. Contemporary artistic developments in neighboring regions, particularly the innovations occurring in Sienese and Florentine workshops, appear to have influenced certain aspects of his compositional approach and technical methodology.

The decorative sensibility evident in his works reflects broader Gothic aesthetic principles while incorporating distinctive regional characteristics that identify his production with specific Marchigian traditions. His approach to narrative illustration demonstrates familiarity with established iconographic conventions while incorporating personal interpretations that enhance emotional impact and devotional effectiveness. The consistent high quality of his documented works suggests systematic study of masterworks by established artists, possibly through direct observation during travels or through detailed drawings and studies that circulated between workshops. Evidence of technical innovation within traditional Gothic frameworks indicates active engagement with contemporary artistic problems and solutions, rather than passive reproduction of established formulas. The synthesis of diverse influences evident in his mature style demonstrates sophisticated artistic intelligence capable of integrating multiple sources into coherent personal expression.

## Geographic Movement and Professional Networks

The documented geographic range of Francescuccio Ghissi's professional activity reveals an artist whose career extended well beyond his home city of Fabriano, encompassing significant portions of the Marche region and extending into neighboring Umbria. A stay in Perugia, suggested by Umbrian features in his style and by frescoes attributed to him there, would be the most distant extension of his professional network; the 1389 record of a Fabriano painter in the city names a Franceschino di Francesco and is not secure evidence for Francescuccio himself. The distribution of his works across multiple cities including Fabriano, Montegiorgio, Ascoli Piceno, and Fermo indicates systematic cultivation of regional patronage networks that sustained his workshop's productivity throughout his career.

Evidence suggests that his artistic reputation facilitated invitations to work in locations where local artistic resources were insufficient for major commissions, positioning him as a specialist capable of fulfilling sophisticated iconographic requirements. The concentration of his documented works within the Marche region reflects both practical considerations regarding transportation of completed panels and the artist's strategic focus on developing sustained relationships within accessible geographic areas. His documented collaboration with Allegretto Nuzi on projects located in various cities suggests participation in collaborative networks that coordinated artistic resources across regional boundaries.

The apparent establishment of long-term relationships with specific ecclesiastical institutions, evidenced by multiple commissions from similar patronage sources, indicates successful cultivation of repeat clientele that provided economic stability. Analysis of his documented works suggests that he developed efficient systems for managing distant commissions, possibly including preliminary visits for consultation and final installation supervision. The geographic spread of his artistic influence, extending from his home base in Fabriano to various regional centers, demonstrates successful adaptation to diverse local requirements while maintaining consistent artistic quality. Older sources also mention lost works: an early nineteenth-century history of Marchigian art records paintings by him in San Francesco at Fabriano, and local historians recall frescoes in the cathedral and in San Francesco at San Severino Marche, of which nothing remains.

The professional networks that facilitated Francescuccio Ghissi's geographic mobility appear to have included both ecclesiastical connections and secular commercial relationships characteristic of 14th-century Italian artistic practice. His association with mendicant religious orders, particularly evident in his specialization in Madonna dell'Umiltà iconography, may have provided access to inter-regional networks of ecclesiastical contacts that facilitated commissions in distant locations. The merchant networks centered in Fabriano, renowned for its paper production and commercial connections, likely provided practical support for transporting completed works and facilitating financial arrangements with distant patrons.

Evidence suggests that his collaboration with other established masters created professional relationships that extended his geographic reach through shared commissions and mutual recommendations. The documented presence of his works in collections that were subsequently dispersed indicates that his artistic reputation attracted the attention of collectors and patrons whose activities extended beyond immediate regional boundaries. A stay in Perugia, if confirmed, would suggest successful integration into established artistic communities in foreign cities, requiring both artistic credibility and social connections that facilitated professional acceptance. The survival of his works in various ecclesiastical locations implies the development of installation and maintenance relationships that ensured proper care of completed commissions over extended periods. Analysis of contemporary travel routes and commercial connections suggests that Ghissi's geographic mobility followed established patterns of movement that facilitated efficient travel and communication between artistic centers. The apparent coordination of his activities with those of other artists working in similar geographic areas indicates participation in broader professional networks that shared information about available commissions and technical innovations. ## Death

Neither the place nor the date of Francescuccio Ghissi's death is known. His last fixed point is the Montegiorgio Madonna of 1374; the Madonna dell'Umiltà dated 1395 and signed by a "Franciscus" is generally given to a namesake. The statement of a seventeenth-century local historian that he died at Florence in 1386 cannot be verified, and it sits uneasily with the record of a Franceschino di Francesco of Fabriano living in Perugia in 1389. His most significant artistic legacy consists of his innovative development of the Madonna dell'Umiltà iconography and his Saint John Altarpiece, works that demonstrate his sophisticated understanding of both theological requirements and artistic possibilities. The Madonna dell'Umiltà of 1359 in the Fabriano Pinacoteca, his earliest signed work, is an example of 14th-century devotional painting that combines theological sophistication with accessible spiritual expression.

  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-01.jpg" alt="Small gilded tabernacle panel with a pointed Gothic frame and twisted colonnettes: the Virgin, seated low in a dark blue gold-patterned mantle and red gown, nurses the Christ Child against a gold ground.">
  <figcaption>Our Lady of Humility, c. 1350–1355, tempera and gold on panel, 34.6 × 19.9 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

The work is a small vertical devotional panel — a portable tabernacle picture — executed in tempera and gold on a poplar panel of modest dimensions (34.6 × 19.9 cm), consistent with private domestic devotion rather than an altarpiece. It survives with (or within a reconstruction of) its engaged Gothic frame, which is integral to the reading of the object: the sacred image is presented as though glimpsed through a miniature architectural aperture, a *maestà* enshrined.

The gilded frame takes the form of a cusped ogival (pointed) arch surmounted by a steep crocketed gable. Curling leaf-crockets run up both raking sides of the gable and terminate in a foliate finial or fleuron at the apex. The spandrel zone within the arch is filled with blind tracery — trefoil and multifoil cusping — imitating the microarchitecture of a church portal or reliquary. The arch is flanked by two slender spiral-twisted (tortile, "barley-sugar") colonnettes rising from bases to foliate, Corinthianizing capitals, from which the inner molded arch appears to spring. Below the picture field is a plain gilded panel functioning as a predella-like base, closed by a stepped molding at the foot. The entire frame is water-gilded and burnished, and the tooling catches light as an active devotional surface.

Against a burnished gold ground — the timeless, aspatial gold of divine presence — the Virgin is seated low, close to the ground rather than upon an elevated throne. This is the defining feature of the Madonna of Humility (*Madonna dell'Umiltà*), the fourteenth-century type that made the Virgin's *humilitas* visible by placing the Queen of Heaven on the earth (or on a cushion). The subject is here fused with the Maria Lactans or Madonna del Latte (the Nursing Virgin): the Christ Child is drawn to the Virgin's breast, and she has parted her garment to suckle him. The combination is theologically pointed, uniting the Virgin's humility with the reality of the Incarnation and Christ's true human nature.

She is shown roughly half- to three-quarter length, her body inclined and her head tenderly bowed toward the Child, eyes lowered in a gentle, melancholic absorption. A punched and rayed gold nimbus frames her head. She wears a deep blue mantle rendered as a luxury figured silk — the blue ground worked over with a gold brocade lattice of repeating ogival/lozenge compartments enclosing stylized floral or pomegranate-like motifs (a rendering of the kind of costly patterned textiles, ultimately of Near-Eastern inspiration, prized in Trecento Italy). Beneath the mantle appears a red gown, likewise enriched with gold damask ornament across the bodice, and a green veil or lining edges her face. The gold patterning is achieved with mordant gilding and fine sgraffito-like detailing over the color.

The infant is held across her lap and turned inward toward the breast, his upper body bare and his lower body wrapped in a coral-pink (salmon) cloth. He too bears a gold nimbus. His pose — the nursing gesture, the small grasping hands — humanizes the divine child while the gold ground and haloes preserve the sacred register.

There are no additional figures, donors, saints, or narrative scenes in this panel: the composition is confined to the two protagonists. Behind and beneath the Virgin one can read a reddish drape or cushion spread on the ground (a cloth of honor consistent with the humility iconography), with a bluish-grey passage at the lower right; the field is otherwise pure gold. The halos and the border where the gold meets the arch show the elaborate punchwork (tooled rosettes and radiating rays) typical of this Marchigian milieu — the very category of decoration catalogued by [Mojmír Frinta](/scholars.html#frinta-mojmir-svatopluk), and a useful diagnostic for grouping and attribution.

The attribution to Francescuccio Ghissi (Francescuccio di Cecco, documented in Fabriano 1345–1374) places the panel squarely in the orbit of Allegretto Nuzi, Ghissi's principal model and likely master. Ghissi's manner refines Nuzi's sweetness into a more linear, decoratively insistent idiom, well exemplified here by the crisp textile patterning and the tender, downcast facial type. The attribution is not unanimous: Walter Angelelli, in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*, observes that the panel is so graceful and subtle that it could be either Ghissi's masterpiece or one of the finest works of Allegretto Nuzi himself.

### Our Lady of Humility (Fabriano)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-02.jpg" alt="The Virgin of Humility seated on the ground nursing the Child, in a dark blue mantle with gold lozenges and a red gown with gold birds, before a white cloth of honour tied with red ribbons; a crescent moon lies at her feet.">
  <figcaption>Our Lady of Humility, 1359, tempera on panel, 173 × 122 cm, Pinacoteca civica Bruno Molajoli, Fabriano.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a large-scale devotional panel in tempera and gold on wood, and it holds a special place in the artist's oeuvre because it is signed and dated 1359 — the earlier of the two signed and dated panels around which the reconstruction of Francescuccio Ghissi's career is built, the other being the Madonna of 1374 at Sant'Andrea in Montegiorgio. Along the base runs a painted inscription (now abraded but partly legible) carrying the artist's signature, FRANCISCUTIUS CICCHI FECIT HOC OPUS, and the date, one of the documents that anchor the Fabriano master's chronology. The image combines the Madonna of Humility (the Virgin seated low upon the ground) with the Maria Lactans / Madonna del Latte (the Nursing Virgin), and enriches that type with apocalyptic and cosmological symbolism, as described below.

The Virgin is seated on the ground in token of her *humilitas*, shown nearly to full length, her body turned three-quarters and her head gently inclined toward the Child. Her face is of the sweet, softly modelled type characteristic of the Nuzi–Ghissi milieu, with lowered gaze and delicate features; a double gold nimbus — an inner rayed disc within an outer circle — frames her head.

She wears a splendid costume rendered as costly figured silks:

- A deep blue mantle worked all over with a dense gold lattice of lozenge compartments enclosing star-and-fleuron motifs — a luxury textile of the kind prized in Trecento Italy — falling in broad folds across the ground.
- Beneath it, a red-crimson gown patterned in gold with an aviary-and-vine design: small birds set among curling vine or leaf scrolls, a motif ultimately reflecting imported Near-Eastern and Italian brocades.
- A gold-edged veil frames her face, and a ring is visible on her hand as she supports the Child.

The infant Christ is cradled across her lap and turned inward to the breast in the nursing gesture, one small hand reaching up. His body is largely bare, wrapped below in a pale blue and rose cloth, and he too bears a rayed gold halo. The intimate exchange of gazes and gestures between mother and child humanizes the sacred pair while the gold ground preserves the transcendent register.

Several features lift this beyond a simple Madonna del Latte into a richer Marian iconography:

- At the lower left, beneath the Virgin's mantle, appears a crescent moon, and radiating golden rays issue from beneath and around her figure. These are attributes of the Woman of the Apocalypse (Revelation 12:1 — "clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet"), an image increasingly assimilated to the Virgin and, in time, to Immaculate iconography. The Madonna of Humility here becomes a cosmic queen paradoxically seated upon the earth.
- Behind the Virgin, a white cloth of honor (a curtain or canopy) is drawn up and tied back with red ribbons/laces at either side, its peaked form crowning her like a tent or baldachin and setting her figure off against the sky.

Behind the white cloth a bluish sky and a horizon appear, with a raised floor and a stylobate below. According to the museum, floor, stylobate and sky come from a repainting by the Master of Collamato in the second half of the fifteenth century: they are another hand's, a century later, and say nothing about Ghissi's handling of space. A later restoration by Giovan Battista De Magistris, in 1674, is recorded by an inscription on the back of the panel. The composition is enclosed within a painted multi-lobed (scalloped) ogival arch — a cusped foliate border in gold and rose-red against a dark green spandrel — from which the red ribbons appear to hang. The reddish painted margins at the outer edges represent the panel's framing structure.

As is visible, the panel shows its age: extensive craquelure, some losses and abrasion (notably in the gold of the halo and the lower inscription), and the wear one expects of a large fourteenth-century devotional picture. None of this obscures the refined linear handling of the textile patterning and the tender facial types that make the attribution secure.

Because it is signed and dated 1359 (the Montegiorgio Madonna is signed and dated 1374), this Fabriano *Madonna dell'Umiltà* is, with that panel, the cornerstone of Ghissi's corpus and the yardstick against which the unsigned panels (such as the two Vatican panels) are measured. It confirms his formation in the immediate circle of Allegretto Nuzi, whose sweetness Ghissi translates into a more insistently linear, decoratively dense manner; the two artists' hands are repeatedly weighed against one another in the Marchigian Trecento literature.

### Dead Christ and angels; Adoration of Baby Jesus

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-03.jpg" alt="Two-register panel on gold: above, the Man of Sorrows in his tomb between two mourning angels; below, the Christ Child lying on the ground, adored by two angels, the Virgin and St Joseph.">
  <figcaption>Dead Christ and angels; Adoration of Baby Jesus, c. 1360, tempera and gold on panel, 39.3 × 28.5 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a small vertical panel in tempera and gold on wood (39.3 × 28.5 cm), preserved with its plain gilded molded frame. Unusually, the picture surface is divided horizontally into two superimposed registers, each carrying a distinct but theologically linked subject. A gilded, punch-decorated dividing band — tooled with a frieze of small arcading and dotted borders — separates the two scenes and unifies them visually. The whole is set against a continuous burnished gold ground, and the surface shows a fine, dense craquelure (age cracks) typical of a fourteenth-century panel of this age.

At the center of the upper zone is the Imago Pietatis (the *Man of Sorrows* / Dead Christ in the tomb). Christ is shown half-length, rising from — or standing within — the sarcophagus, framed by a pointed, cusped aperture painted in a rose-red border against a dark, near-black recess that evokes the shadowed interior of the tomb. His eyes are closed and his head bows to his right; his long hair and beard are rendered with fine linear strokes, and a punched gold nimbus surrounds his head. His arms are crossed over his chest, the wrists showing the bleeding wounds of the nails, with further drops of blood marking the side wound — the visible signs of the Passion presented for devotional contemplation. A white cloth (the shroud or loincloth) girds his hips.

Flanking the tomb-aperture, one to either side, are two mourning angels, each turned inward toward Christ. They wear red undergarments with deep ultramarine-blue mantles and display large red wings. Each angel presses or crosses the hands upon the breast in a gesture of grief and reverence, and each bears a punched gold halo. The pairing frames the dead Christ as an object of sorrowful adoration.

The lower zone presents the Nativity conceived as an Adoration of the Child. At the lower left, the naked Christ Child lies stretched out on a bed of golden straw laid over the ground, his head to the left, a rayed cruciform-type gold nimbus radiating around him. He is the smallest figure yet the compositional focus toward which all others incline.

At the left stand (or kneel) two angels, closely paired, in red robes with red wings and gold haloes, their hands joined in prayer as they gaze down at the Child in adoration.

At the center-right kneels the Virgin Mary, shown in profile, bowing deeply over the Child. She wears the traditional deep-blue mantle, richly edged and lined with gold and worked with fine gold hem-ornament, over a blue gown; a gold-bordered veil covers her head, framed by a punched halo. Her hands are crossed upon her breast in the gesture of humble adoration, her eyes lowered toward her son.

At the far right kneels Saint Joseph, an elderly, grey-haired and bearded man, draped in an ochre-golden mantle over a red robe, with a gold halo. He raises both hands in prayer and adoration, his weathered features and white hair contrasting with the youthful figures and marking his traditional role as the aged guardian.

The ground at the base is rendered as a low, undulating strip of brownish earth, anchoring the sacred event in a minimal terrestrial setting while the gold ground behind lifts it into the timeless register of devotion.

The two registers are deliberately paired to bracket Christ's life between its beginning and its sacrificial end: below, the Incarnation — the newborn Christ adored by his parents and the angels; above, the Passion and death — the same Christ displayed as the Man of Sorrows, mourned by angels. Read together they form a compact meditation on Redemption, the kind of layered devotional imagery well suited to private prayer.

The attribution to Francescuccio Ghissi (documented in Fabriano, 1345–1374) places the panel within the Marchigian Trecento and in the close orbit of Allegretto Nuzi, his likely master. The dense punchwork in the haloes and dividing band, the sweet downcast facial types, and the crisp linear handling of hair and drapery hems are all characteristic of Ghissi's decoratively refined idiom.

### Madonna and Child between a Saint Bishop and Saints John the Evangelist, John the Baptist and Venantius; in the cusps: Christ adored by two angels between Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint Anthony the Abbot

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-04.jpg" alt="Five-part gabled polyptych: the Virgin and Child at the centre, a bishop saint and St John the Evangelist on the left, St John the Baptist and a young martyr on the right, small figures in the gables; the gold is heavily abraded.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child between a Saint Bishop and Saints John the Evangelist, John the Baptist and Venantius; in the cusps: Christ adored by two angels between Saint Catherine of Alexandria and Saint Anthony the Abbot, follower of Allegretto Nuzi (Francescuccio Ghissi?), c. 1375–1399, tempera on panel, 122 × 173 cm, Pinacoteca civica Bruno Molajoli, Fabriano.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a horizontal polyptych in tempera and gold on wood, composed of five vertical compartments crowned by steep gabled cusps and set on a continuous plinth. Each main compartment is enclosed within a painted cusped ogival arch, and above each rises a triangular gable (cuspide) carrying a small half-length figure. The whole is unified by the burnished gold ground, now considerably abraded, with extensive losses to the gold and gesso especially in the central gable and along the upper edges — the wear typical of a large fourteenth-century altarpiece that has lost its original engaged framework. The hierarchy is the standard one: the enthroned Virgin at the sacred center, flanked symmetrically by standing saints, with a celestial register of Christ and adoring angels and further saints in the gables above.

At the center stands (or is enthroned behind) the Virgin holding the Christ Child, flanked by areas now dark where the gilding and paint have been lost. She wears a red-crimson gown patterned in gold beneath a veil, her head gently inclined, with the soft, sweet facial type characteristic of the Fabriano master. The Christ Child, in a rose-pink tunic, is held on her arm and turns toward the viewer, one hand raised. Both bear gold haloes. The Virgin's slightly swaying pose and the intimate handling of the Child are hallmarks of Ghissi's idiom, refined from that of Allegretto Nuzi.

*First panel (far left) — Saint Nicholas.* An older, bearded bishop in a mitre, raising his right hand in blessing and holding a crozier (pastoral staff) in his left. He wears a rose-pink robe beneath an ornamented cope with a gold-embroidered orphrey. The mitre and crozier mark him as a bishop-saint; the regional catalogue of cultural heritage identifies him as Saint Nicholas.

*Second panel — Saint John the Evangelist.* A youthful, beardless saint with fair hair, draped in a blue undergarment and a golden-ochre mantle, holding a closed book (rendered dark blue and starred with gold) — the attribute of his authorship. His serene, idealized features contrast with the aged bishop beside him.

*Fourth panel — Saint John the Baptist.* A gaunt, bearded ascetic with long hair, wearing his traditional camel-hair garment partly visible beneath a rose-pink mantle. He gestures with one hand and holds a furling scroll carrying his prophetic inscription (the *Ecce Agnus Dei* text), his pointing gesture directing devotion toward Christ.

*Fifth panel (far right) — Saint Venantius.* A youthful martyr, the patron saint of Camerino and titular of Fabriano cathedral, shown holding a tall staff with a banner/standard (vexillum) and dressed in red — the attributes appropriate to this young soldier-martyr. His inclusion is a strong regional (Marchigian) marker.

Small half-length figures occupy the five triangular gables:

- Far-left gable — Saint Catherine of Alexandria, a crowned female saint.
- Second gable — an adoring angel, inclined in prayer toward the center.
- Central gable — Christ, half-length and blessing, as the title of the work records; the gable is largely lost and no figure can be read in the photograph.
- Fourth gable — a second adoring angel, mirroring the first.
- Far-right gable — Saint Anthony the Abbot, the elderly desert father.

Together the upper register crowns the earthly assembly of saints with a heavenly one, presided over by Christ.

The programme is a classic *sacra conversazione* distributed across separate compartments: the Virgin and Child enthroned at the center, attended by a bishop, the two Saints John (Evangelist and Baptist), and the local patron Venantius — a combination that points to a Marchigian, very possibly Fabrianese, destination. The celestial tier of Christ, angels, and the martyr-saint Catherine with the hermit Anthony completes the devotional hierarchy from earth to heaven.

The attribution should be stated with caution. The regional catalogue records a critical consensus for a follower of Allegretto Nuzi, perhaps Francescuccio Ghissi, and dates the work to the last quarter of the fourteenth century (1375–1399). The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* lists among Ghissi's early works a polyptych with the Madonna and Child and four saints, painted for the abbey of Santa Maria dell'Appennino and now in the Pinacoteca of Fabriano; whether it is this panel is not established. The sweet facial types, the linear crispness of the drapery, and the decorative textile grounds are all consistent with his hand.

### Our Lady of Humility (Fermo)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-05.jpg" alt="The Virgin of Humility nursing the Child against a gold ground tooled with vine scrolls and scattered dark stars, in a dark mantle with vine leaves and a red gown patterned with birds.">
  <figcaption>Our Lady of Humility, tempera and gold on panel, 95 × 77 cm, Pinacoteca civica, Fermo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a devotional panel in tempera and gold on wood (95 × 77 cm, cut down on all sides), showing the Madonna of Humility fused with the Maria Lactans / Madonna del Latte (the Nursing Virgin). The composition is confined to the two protagonists set against a spectacular worked gold ground, and the panel is notable for the richness of its tooling and star ornament, which gives the image an overtly cosmic, apocalyptic character. Its state shows the wear of age — scattered losses, abrasion, and paint flaking (notably across the gold and in the halo) — but the refined surface work remains legible.

The Virgin is seated low in token of her *humilitas*, shown to about three-quarter length and turned three-quarters, her head inclined toward the Child with a grave, tender expression and a slightly elongated, softly modelled face of the sweet Nuzi–Ghissi type. Around her head is an exceptionally elaborate incised and punched halo: a rayed golden disc filled with fine engraved scrollwork and encircled by a band of inscription incised in Gothic letters: MATER ORDINIS PRAEDICATORUM ("Mother of the Order of Preachers"), which ties the panel to a Dominican setting.

She wears:

- A dark blue-black mantle strewn with a scattered gold pattern of vine leaves and grape clusters and small fleurons, edged in gold and drawn up over her head as a veil.
- Beneath it, a sumptuous red-crimson gown rendered as a costly figured silk, its gold brocade filled with an intricate design of confronted birds/peacocks and vine scrolls — the kind of luxury textile, ultimately of Near-Eastern derivation, prized in Trecento Italy.

The infant Christ is cradled low across her lap and turned inward to the breast in the nursing gesture, one small hand reaching to the breast. His body is largely nude, the flesh softly modelled with rounded limbs, wrapped below in a pale cloth; he wears a plain gold halo. The naturalistic handling of the plump infant body against the flat splendour of the gold and textiles is characteristic of the manner.

The most distinctive feature is the ring of pointed stars — rendered in dark, engraved outline — scattered across the burnished gold field around and above the figures, together with the radiate halo. This clothing of the Virgin in a starry, sunlike field evokes the Woman of the Apocalypse (Revelation 12:1 — "a woman clothed with the sun… and upon her head a crown of twelve stars"), assimilating the humble Nursing Madonna to the cosmic Queen of Heaven. The chased scrollwork filling the background gold and the punched detailing further enrich this celestial setting.

The image compresses several Marian ideas into one intimate icon: the humility of the Virgin seated on the ground, the Incarnation made tangible through the act of nursing, and the apocalyptic/cosmic queenship signalled by the stars and radiate nimbus. The Dominican inscription agrees with the report, in the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale*, that the panel comes from the Dominican church of Santa Lucia in Fabriano, the same church as the signed Madonna of 1359.

### Enthroned Madonna with Six Angels

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-06.jpg" alt="The Virgin and Child enthroned cheek to cheek, in a dark blue mantle with gold lozenges before a red and gold cloth of honour, with three angels on each side, hands joined or crossed.">
  <figcaption>Enthroned Madonna with Six Angels, tempera and gold on panel, 138 × 99 cm, Musée du Petit Palais, Avignon.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a large vertical panel in tempera and gold on wood (138 × 99 cm) representing the Maestà — the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, attended by a court of angels. Unlike the *Madonne dell'Umiltà* above, where the Virgin sits humbly on the ground, here she is elevated on a throne and presented as Queen of Heaven, the most solemn and hieratic of the Marian formulas. The composition is symmetrical and courtly, the two protagonists framed by six angels and set against a burnished gold ground. The surface shows the expected age-wear — craquelure, abrasion, and scattered losses, particularly across the gold and the darker passages of the mantle.

At the center the Virgin is enthroned, shown nearly to full length, her head tenderly inclined toward the Child in the affectionate glykophilousa manner (the "sweet-loving" type, cheek drawn close to cheek). Her face is of the softly modelled, slightly melancholic Nuzi–Ghissi type, with lowered gaze; a punched and incised gold halo frames her head, and a white veil edges her face beneath the mantle.

She wears:

- A deep blue-black mantle worked all over with a gold pattern of star-and-fleuron lozenge motifs, edged with a broad gold orphrey band, falling in ample folds over her lap and cascading to the foot of the throne.
- Beneath it, a red-crimson gown visible at the bodice and sleeves, the traditional undergarment of the Virgin.

The Christ Child is held high against her breast, cheek to cheek with his mother, his body largely nude and softly wrapped below in a blue-grey cloth. He too bears a gold halo. His plump limbs and the intimate cheek-to-cheek embrace humanize the otherwise majestic image.

Behind the pair rises a cloth of honor — a richly patterned textile in red and gold worked with a dense floral/foliate design, hung behind the throne to signal royal dignity. The throne's architectural elements are partly visible at either side, where architectural elements rise beside the flanking angels.

Three angels are ranged on each side, disposed in two tiers, all with gold haloes and turned inward toward the Virgin in attitudes of prayer and adoration:

- The upper pair, at the very top corners, incline toward the throne with hands joined in prayer, their large wings spread behind them.
- The middle and lower angels on each side stand in ranked profile, several holding their hands crossed on the breast or pressed together, gazing up toward the Virgin and Child. They wear robes of rose-pink, pale yellow, and blue-grey, some crossed by dark, gold-bordered diagonal bands (a deacon-like sash or stole), each with a gold halo.

The angelic court, symmetrically arrayed, transforms the panel into a heavenly throne-room and reinforces the queenly majesty of the central group.

This is the regal counterpart to the humility images: where the *Madonna dell'Umiltà* stresses the Virgin's lowliness and the tender reality of the Incarnation (often through nursing), the *Maestà* proclaims her exalted status as enthroned Mother of God and Queen of Heaven, surrounded by her celestial retinue. The affectionate cheek-to-cheek embrace nonetheless preserves the note of tenderness even within the solemn courtly formula.

### Our Lady of Humility; The Nativity; The Crucifixion; The Annunciation (top left and right)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-07.jpg" alt="Small hinged triptych in a glazed case on green velvet: the nursing Virgin of Humility in the centre, the Nativity in the left wing and the Crucifixion in the right, the Annunciation in the two gables.">
  <figcaption>Our Lady of Humility; The Nativity; The Crucifixion; The Annunciation (top left and right), tempera on panel, 450 × 209 mm (central panel), National Trust, Polesden Lacey, Surrey.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a small portable triptych in tempera and gold on wood — a hinged, folding altarpiece designed for private devotion and travel, with a large central panel (450 × 209 mm) flanked by two narrower wings that would fold inward to protect the painted surfaces. It is here displayed within a later glazed wooden case against green velvet, with a museum accession number ("18") and a nameplate reading "Fra[ncescuccio] Ghissi" at the base. The central panel is crowned by a steep gabled peak and set on a stepped, molded base; each wing terminates in a triangular gable carrying a subsidiary scene. The gold ground is worked with fine tooling and, in the central compartment, scattered incised stars. The surface shows the wear of age — craquelure, abrasion, and scattered losses.

The main compartment shows the Madonna of Humility fused with the Maria Lactans / Madonna del Latte (the Nursing Virgin). The Virgin is seated low upon the ground in token of her *humilitas*, shown three-quarter length, her head tenderly inclined toward the Child. She wears a deep blue mantle densely worked with a gold lattice of foliate/scroll ornament, over a dark red gown, the Child wrapped in a red-orange cloth patterned in gold; a punched gold halo frames her head, and the gold ground above is scattered with incised stars — an apocalyptic/cosmic note (the Woman of the Apocalypse) recurring throughout Ghissi's Madonne. The Christ Child, largely nude and turned inward to the breast in the nursing gesture, bears a gold halo. Both faces are of the sweet, softly modelled Nuzi–Ghissi type.

The left wing is divided into two zones:

- Main (lower) scene — the Nativity / Adoration. At the lower left, the Virgin in red reclines or kneels beside the manger; the newborn Christ Child lies swaddled, attended by the ox and ass behind. The aged, bearded Saint Joseph sits in the foreground in his traditional pose of contemplative withdrawal. Haloes are punched in gold. The scene depicts the Incarnation — the earthly beginning of Christ's life.
- Gable (upper) — an Annunciation figure. In the triangular cusp above stands a small pale-robed figure, apparently the Angel Gabriel, set against tooled gold. Together with the Virgin Annunciate in the right gable, this completes the Annunciation split across the two wing-peaks.

The right wing likewise carries two zones:

- Main (lower) scene — the Crucifixion. The crucified Christ hangs on the cross at center, his body rendered with the pathos of the Passion. To the left stands the mourning Virgin Mary in dark blue, and to the right the youthful, beardless Saint John the Evangelist in his customary red-and-green drapery, both haloed, in attitudes of grief. This is the sacrificial end of Christ's life — deliberately paired with the Nativity opposite.
- Gable (upper) — the second Annunciation figure. In the cusp sits the Virgin Annunciate, in a dark blue mantle over red, against tooled gold.

The programme is a compact meditation on Incarnation and Redemption, of the kind ideally suited to a portable devotional object. The Annunciation is announced across the two gables at the summit (the conception of Christ); the Nativity (left) and Crucifixion (right) bracket his earthly life from birth to sacrificial death; and the Madonna of Humility/Madonna del Latte at the center holds these together in the tender image of the Mother nursing the Son whose birth and death surround her. The whole moves, top to bottom and wing to wing, through the central mysteries of salvation.

### The Resurrection of Drusiana

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-08.jpg" alt="St John, at left, raises his hand toward a bier draped in red and carried by two bearers, while a kneeling man entreats him and a woman raises her arms before a white building with a tower; a red seraph hovers at upper left; the frame nameplate reads Allegretto Nuzi.">
  <figcaption>The Resurrection of Drusiana, c. 1370, tempera on panel, 34.3 × 36.8 cm, Portland Art Museum, Portland.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel depicts The Resurrection of Drusiana, an episode drawn not from the canonical Gospels but from the apocryphal Acts of John, which reached the painter through the *Golden Legend* of Jacobus de Voragine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-jacopo-da-varagine" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, the source followed by the whole cycle. In the story, Drusiana, a devout Christian woman of Ephesus who had been moved by John's preaching, died; as the Apostle re-entered the city he encountered her funeral cortège being carried out to burial, and by his command she was miraculously raised from the dead. The scene is therefore a miracle narrative centered on the saving word of the Apostle.

This is a small horizontal narrative panel in tempera and gold on wood (34.3 × 36.8 cm), preserved in a later gilded frame. The action is set within a painted cusped ogival arch springing from spiral (barley-sugar) colonnettes, which frames the scene like a stage and opens onto a gold ground and an architectural backdrop. Federico Zeri regarded the gilt pastiglia twisted colonnettes of the first two scenes of the series as later additions, distinguished by their different lustre and made to present each fragment as a self-contained picture. The surface shows extensive craquelure typical of a fourteenth-century panel.

A nameplate on the frame reads "THE RESURRECTION OF DRUSIANA / ALLEGRETTO NUZI / SCHOOL FABRIANO, ACTIVE 1346–1373/4 / KRESS COLLECTION".

The figures, left to right:

- Saint John the Evangelist stands at the far left, the protagonist of the miracle. He is youthful and beardless with fair hair, haloed, draped in a rose-pink mantle over a dark blue-black robe. He extends his right hand in a commanding, pointing gesture toward the bier — the very gesture by which he calls Drusiana back to life. His calm, upright authority anchors the composition.

- A kneeling supplicant, a smaller figure in brownish-ochre dress, kneels at John's feet with hands raised toward him in entreaty — a witness or petitioner begging the Apostle's intervention, a device that draws the viewer into the drama.

- The bier and Drusiana. At the center, the body of Drusiana is borne on a litter draped in red cloth; she lies wrapped, her form indicated beneath the covering. The bier is carried by bearers: a central figure in a coral-orange tunic with a wrapped head-cloth (turban) supports the front, and a second bearer in a pale cream-white tunic with red hose carries the rear, straining under the weight. Their movement conveys the interrupted funeral procession.

- A woman in the background, at the right beside the buildings, throws up her arms in a gesture of astonishment or grief — a bystander reacting to the miracle, her raised arms a standard motif of wonder.

Behind the figures rises a passage of Trecento architecture — a white building with a tiled pitched roof, arched windows, a balcony, and a slender tower or campanile at the left — evoking the city of Ephesus and giving the miracle a concrete urban stage. At the upper left, a small red seraph/angel hovers within the arch, a celestial presence marking the sacred and miraculous character of the event. The gold ground behind is lightly tooled with foliate ornament.

This panel is especially important for the Nuzi–Ghissi question because it comes from the celebrated Scenes from the Life of Saint John the Evangelist cycle, a dossal reconstructed by Federico Zeri in 1949, in which eight narrative scenes in two registers flanked a central Crucifixion (Art Institute of Chicago); the surviving panels are now divided among Portland, Raleigh, New York and Chicago. The cycle is one of the principal touchstones for defining Ghissi's narrative manner as distinct from his master's — the lively, slightly angular figures, the anecdotal gestures, and the crisp architectural stage-sets are characteristic.

The frame nameplate assigns the panel to Allegretto Nuzi, which reflects an older attribution (Kress Collection labels frequently preserve the connoisseurship of the mid-twentieth century). Zeri's 1975 proposal of a design by Nuzi executed by Ghissi, Keith Christiansen's attribution to Ghissi (1982) and Matteo Mazzalupi's assignment of the whole execution to Francescuccio (2017) have moved the Saint John cycle — and this *Drusiana* panel — to Francescuccio Ghissi, exactly the kind of Nuzi-versus-Ghissi reattribution that runs through the whole literature. This makes the panel a useful documented case-study of how works have migrated between the two names.

### Saint John the Evangelist Correcting the Philosopher Crato (Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art)

  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-09.jpg" alt="Gold-ground panel framed by a gilded arch on twisted columns. On the counter lie a heap of fragments and an anvil. Facing him, a bearded philosopher in a red gown and a grey fur cape holds a hammer. Two young men stand behind the philosopher. At the upper corners, outside the arch, fragments of other figures are visible: a haloed figure in red holding a palm on the left, and a nude figure in a vat on the right.">
  <figcaption>Saint John the Evangelist Correcting a Philosopher Named Crato, panel from a dismembered altarpiece of Saint John the Evangelist, c. 1370–1380. Tempera and gold leaf on panel, 35.6 × 41.3 cm (frame 55.2 × 57.1 cm). Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art, GL.60.17.18, Gift of the Samuel H. Kress Foundation.</figcaption>
</figure>

This panel is one of three scenes from the Saint John altarpiece now in the North Carolina Museum of Art. The others are in the Portland Art Museum, the Metropolitan Museum of Art and, for the central Crucifixion, the Art Institute of Chicago. The opening scene is in Portland. In the *Golden Legend*, which the cycle follows, the Crato episode comes immediately after the raising of Drusiana on John's return to Ephesus. It is therefore the second scene of the narrative. It also directly introduces the next Raleigh panel, *Acteus and Eugenius Implore Saint John the Evangelist to Restore Their Wealth*, since John's teaching here is what persuades Acteus and Eugenius to give up their own fortunes.

In the account of Jacobus de Voragine, the philosopher Crato stages a public lesson in contempt for the world in the forum at Ephesus. He has two rich young brothers sell their inheritance, buy precious stones with the proceeds, and smash them in front of everyone. John reproves him. Renouncing wealth to win people's admiration is vanity, and true renunciation means giving one's goods to the poor, as Christ taught the rich young man. Crato challenges the apostle: if his master is truly God, let him make the stones whole again so that their value can go to the poor. John gathers the fragments and prays, and the gems are restored. Crato and the two young men believe, and the stones are sold for the benefit of the poor. The painting shows the decisive moment, when John gathers the fragments from the counter just before the miracle.

Ghissi builds the scene on a strong opposition of two parties facing each other across a long wooden counter, a sort of *banco* with a small arcade along its back edge, set obliquely in space. John comes in from the left in a long diagonal curve that runs from the hem of his mantle to his outstretched hand. With that hand he scoops up a few fragments, close to the small pile still lying on the counter. The second element of the still life, a grey anvil drawn in sharp foreshortening, makes clear how the stones were destroyed. On the right, Crato stands upright and still in profile, holding the hammer raised at the centre of the panel. The hammer and John's hand are the two poles of the story, the instrument of destruction and the gesture of restoration, separated by a short stretch of gold ground.

The philosopher is characterised by a distinctly fourteenth-century outfit: a long scarlet gown, a hood of the same colour, and a short shoulder cape of grey fur worked in a pattern of small scales, a fur of the vair type. Garments of this kind belonged to doctors, jurists and university masters in the Italian cities of the Trecento. The Ephesian philosopher is thus recast in the image of a contemporary learned man, a figure the Fabriano public could recognise at once. Crato's bearded face and grave expression are not caricatured. Ghissi presents him as a respectable opponent, which makes the apostle's victory more convincing and prepares for his conversion.

Behind Crato stand the two young men whose fortune has just been pulverised. The one in front wears a short green tunic, grey-green hose and a purse at his belt, and he crosses his hands over his chest. The other, wrapped in a blue-green mantle, rests a hand on his brother's shoulder and looks toward John. Their attitude, attentive and already inclined toward the saint, anticipates their conversion. The purse links back to the theme of money, which runs through the whole first part of the cycle. Behind John a bearded, hooded disciple appears, in a light robe. His presence turns the apostle's action into a public act witnessed by a member of his community.

Unlike the *Acteus and Eugenius* panel, which has a simple rectangular field, this scene is enclosed by a gilded arch that rests on two twisted columns at the sides. Federico Zeri, however, regarded the gilt pastiglia twisted colonnettes of the first two scenes as later additions, recognizable by their different lustre and intended to present each fragment as a self-contained picture. The slight difference in size (35.6 × 41.3 cm against 34.9 × 38.1 cm) may reflect later trimming. In the two upper corners, outside the arch, fragments of figures are visible. On the left is a haloed saint in a red garment holding a palm. On the right is a nude figure immersed up to the chest in a vat. The latter may suggest the ordeal of the boiling oil at the Porta Latina, an episode in John's legend. Zeri observed that half-figures of saints occupied the spandrels between the arched scenes and matched those at the edges of the Chicago Crucifixion, and Mazzalupi identifies among them Venantius, patron of Fabriano, and Louis of Toulouse<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-louis-toulouse" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>. The palm-bearing martyr may therefore be Venantius; the identification of these fragments is not established.

The panel shows the typical features of Ghissi's narrative production. The figures are tall and slender, the heads small and oval, and the noses long and straight, with features drawn by a sure line and light highlights on the flesh. The colour relies on bold, flat juxtapositions: John's rose against blue, Crato's scarlet against grey fur, the brothers' acid green against blue-green. The space is that of a shallow stage, with a dark floor strip and a gold ground that rises above the counter, which seems to float without strong cast shadows. The drawing of the scales in the fur and of the arcading on the counter shows an attention to ornamental detail that runs through the whole series. A fine craquelure covers the gold and the paint layers without compromising the legibility of the scene.

Like the other panels of the cycle, this scene was long published under the name of Allegretto Nuzi, including in the 1960 catalogue of the NCMA's Kress collection. Federico Zeri proposed in 1975 that the altarpiece was designed by Nuzi and executed by Francescuccio Ghissi. Keith Christiansen published it as the work of Francescuccio di Cecco Ghissi in 1982, followed by Pietro Zampetti and [Giampiero Donnini](/scholars.html#donnini-giampiero) in 1992. The Kress records still keep the formula "attributed to." The panel belonged to Baron Alberto Fassini in Rome and was acquired by Samuel H. Kress on 4 March 1932. It was given to the National Gallery of Art in 1939, deaccessioned in 1952 and returned to the Kress Foundation, then given to the North Carolina Museum of Art in 1961. In 2016–17 it was reunited with the other surviving panels in the exhibition *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece*, shown in Raleigh and then in Portland.

### Acteus and Eugenius Implore Saint John the Evangelist to Restore Their Wealth (Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Francescuccio-Ghissi/francescuccio-ghissi-10.jpg" alt="Gold-ground panel showing Saint John the Evangelist, haloed and wearing a blue tunic and pink mantle, at the centre. Two young men in long robes stand to his right and beg him with outstretched hands; one offers him a handful of gold. Behind them, reeds grow on a rocky slope, pebbles lie along the shore, and a sailing ship rides on a dark sea. On the left, two young men in short secular tunics watch; one points and wears a purse at his belt.">
  <figcaption>Acteus and Eugenius Implore Saint John the Evangelist to Restore Their Wealth, panel from a dismembered altarpiece of Saint John the Evangelist, c. 1370–1380. Tempera and gold leaf on panel, 34.9 × 38.1 cm (frame 55.2 × 57.1 cm). Raleigh, North Carolina Museum of Art, GL.60.17.19, Gift of the Samuel H. Kress Foundation.</figcaption>
</figure>

This small narrative panel belongs to one of the most coherent hagiographic cycles to survive from Trecento Fabriano. It is one of eight known panels of a fourteenth-century Italian altarpiece depicting the Crucifixion and scenes from the life of Saint John the Evangelist. The panels are now divided among four American museums. The opening scene is in the Portland Art Museum; three are in the North Carolina Museum of Art, Raleigh; three are in The Met; and the concluding panel is lost. The central Crucifixion is in the Art Institute of Chicago. According to the Metropolitan Museum, the narrative scenes were arranged in two tiers on either side of a Crucifixion. This arrangement is a lateral, legible format suited to a devotional programme read episode by episode. The altarpiece was painted about 1370, probably for a church in the artist's native Fabriano, in the Marches. Matteo Mazzalupi has proposed San Francesco in Fabriano, rejecting the older link with the collegiate church of San Nicolò, but neither the church nor the patron has been securely identified.

The cycle follows the thirteenth-century *Golden Legend* of Jacobus de Voragine. In that text, the episode of Acteus (Atticus) and Eugenius comes immediately after the confrontation with the philosopher Crato. John's example of renouncing wealth persuades the two young men to sell their estates for the poor and follow him, and they became the apostle's followers. They soon regret their poverty when they see their former servants dressed in fine clothes. John then has them cut rods from the woods and gather pebbles from the shore, and he turns these into gold and precious stones, giving back the worldly riches they now long for. At the NCMA, the panel follows *Saint John Reproving the Philosopher Crato* and precedes the three consecutive scenes at the Met. In those scenes, Satheus, raised to life, tells Acteus and Eugenius about the heavenly riches they forfeited by backsliding, and they beg the saint for forgiveness. The Raleigh panel is therefore the moment of moral failure, which the Met's *Saint John the Evangelist with Acteus and Eugenius* (69.280.2) answers with penitence.

Ghissi builds the scene as a tightly argued exchange between two groups across a narrow gap of gold ground. John stands slightly left of centre, set off by his tooled halo and by the strong blue of his tunic against the rose mantle. He places his right hand on his breast and extends his left, open-palmed, toward the suppliants. The two young men on the right lean in from a rising slope, and their stooping, overlapping profiles form a single diagonal wedge directed at the saint. The front youth stretches out an empty, imploring hand. The figure behind him holds a bundle of rods against his body and pushes forward a clutch of golden objects. These are the transformed rods, now gold, made visible as the material evidence of the miracle. The gap between John's open palm and the offered gold is the dramatic hinge of the picture. It leaves the viewer to weigh the apostle's spiritual counsel against the material gift he has just granted.

The right third of the panel is not mere setting. It holds the props of the miracle. A tuft of slender reeds grows from the ochre hillside, the source of the "rods" of the legend. Along the edge of the dark sea, the shore is scattered with pale pebbles, the raw material John turned into gems. The sea, painted in deep green-black with small curling strokes for the waves, rises steeply in the medieval convention of tilted ground. A single sailing ship rides on it, its white sail swollen against the dark water. The ship most obviously places the scene on the coast of Ephesus. It may also suggest maritime trade and mercantile wealth, which would fit an episode about the lure of riches. That reading is an interpretation, not one recorded in the literature. The way the narrative is staged, with the landscape doing explanatory work, is typical of the cycle as a whole.

Two young men in short, fashionable tunics with scalloped hems close the composition on the left. One wears bright orange hose, a hood draped over his shoulders, and a purse (*scarsella*) at his belt, and he points across the scene. Their worldly dress contrasts deliberately with the long, plain robes of Acteus and Eugenius. In light of the *Golden Legend*, they may be read as the former servants whose finery provoked the two converts' regret. Alternatively, they may be ordinary witnesses who confirm the miracle. In either case, the purse makes the theme of money explicit. The pointing gesture directs the viewer from the world of commerce to the saint, the true source of value in the scene.

The panel shows the hallmarks of Fabriano painting after mid-century. The figures are elongated and slightly stiff, with small heads, and their faces have strong, regular features and blond hair laid in parallel locks. The flesh is modelled with soft greenish underlayers, and fine highlights pick out noses, brows and knuckles. The drapery falls in long tubular folds with little sense of the body beneath. Its effect depends on colour harmonies, such as rose against blue for John and ochre against salmon and slate for the young men. Across the top runs an ornamental band, and the halo is worked with punched and incised decoration. The fine network of craquelure across the gold and the paint layers is typical of tempera on panel of this date. It does not obscure the clarity of the drawing.

The panel was long given to Allegretto Nuzi, the leading Fabriano painter of the period. [Bernard Berenson](/scholars.html#berenson-bernard) published it as Nuzi in 1936. The early Kress literature kept the same name, including the National Gallery of Art preliminary catalogue of 1941 and Fern Rusk Shapley's handbook of 1952 and catalogue of 1968. In 1949 Federico Zeri reconstructed the whole dossal, still under Nuzi's name. In 1975 he refined his position: he attributed the design of the altarpiece to Nuzi and its execution to Francescuccio Ghissi. Giampiero Donnini consolidated Ghissi's catalogue in studies of 1973 and 1996. Keith Christiansen attributed the panel to Francescuccio di Cecco Ghissi in the *Metropolitan Museum of Art Bulletin* in 1982. Later contributions include Pietro Zampetti and Donnini (1992), Christopher Lloyd's 1993 catalogue of the Chicago collection, and Perri Lee Roberts's *Corpus* of 2009. In 2017 Matteo Mazzalupi assigned the entire execution of the dossal to Francescuccio. Zeri's idea of a Nuzi design executed by Ghissi still explains the cycle's closeness to Allegretto's figure types. The Kress records still describe the panel as "attributed to" Ghissi.

The panel was in the collection of Baron Fassini in Rome and then with Contini Bonacossi in Florence. It entered the Samuel H. Kress Foundation in 1932, and the Foundation gave it to the NCMA in 1961. In the meantime, it was given to the National Gallery of Art in 1939, deaccessioned in 1952 and returned to the Kress Foundation. In 2016–17 the scattered panels were shown together in the exhibition *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece*. It opened in Raleigh and then travelled to Portland, and it reassembled the eight known panels together with a re-creation of the missing one, for the first time in more than a hundred years. The project was carried out with Duke University, where Ingrid Daubechies's team used digital techniques for the virtual reconstruction. It showed that the panel's meaning depends on its place in a sequence: it is a scene of temptation, and it calls for the scene of penitence that follows it.

This panel is the first of the three scenes from the Saint John dossal now in the Metropolitan Museum, and in the narrative it follows directly on the two Raleigh panels devoted to Crato and to Acteus and Eugenius. The dossal, reconstructed by Federico Zeri in 1949, comprised eight narrative scenes arranged in two tiers on either side of a central Crucifixion, now in the Art Institute of Chicago. The surviving panels are divided among Portland, Raleigh, New York and Chicago, and the final scene is lost. The Met's three panels recount consecutive episodes. They pass from the raising of Satheus, through the penitence of Acteus and Eugenius, to the destruction of the temple. Together they make the central hinge of the cycle.

In the *Golden Legend*, the episode is set in Ephesus shortly after John has turned rods and pebbles into gold and gems for the two backsliding disciples. A young man, married only thirty days before, dies, and his mother, his widow and their kin bring the bier before the apostle and beg him to restore him. John prays and raises him. He then commands the risen youth to tell Acteus and Eugenius what glory they have forfeited and what punishment they have earned. The resurrection is therefore not an isolated wonder. It is the instrument by which the two converts will be called back, and the panel prepares the scene that follows it in the dossal.

Ghissi organises the scene around the long horizontal of the bier, which crosses almost the whole width of the panel beneath a large field of gold. The red pall falls in heavy folds over the sides, and the two pairs of poles, held by a stooping bearer in green on the left and a youth in orange on the right, frame the group. John stands above the bier on the left. His right arm stretches out in a commanding gesture toward the dead man, and his index finger points to the head on the pillow. The youth, wrapped in white, is shown at the moment his eyes open: the miracle is happening, not yet completed.

Around the bier the painter sets a carefully graded chorus of reactions. In the foreground a man in a short grey tunic kneels with joined hands, his face lowered toward the saint's feet. Beside him, a woman wrapped in a pale rose mantle and another in a yellow veil kneel with hands raised in supplication. They are presumably the mother and the widow of the legend, the two figures who in the text implore John's help. A small head appears behind the bier, between the kneeling women and the dead youth. It belongs to a further mourner, partly hidden by the pall.

The two groups of standing witnesses at the sides complete the composition. On the left, behind John, two youths in rose-coloured garments observe the scene with grave attention, one with his hand at his breast. They may be meant as Acteus and Eugenius themselves, present at the miracle that will be turned against them. However, the Met's account of the panel does not name them, and the identification remains a reading of the scene. On the right a compact crowd of men and women presses forward. A man in a black tunic and orange mantle raises his open hand in amazement, and the heads behind him are stacked one above the other in the manner of Trecento crowds.

The panel differs from the other two Met scenes in its framing. It has no painted or applied arch: the gold ground runs to a straight upper edge decorated with a band of punched ornament. Zeri used the identical crack pattern of the panels as evidence that the scenes had been cut from the same plank, together with their identical subject matter, style and punchwork. The difference between painted surface and overall size recorded by the museum (34.9 × 39.4 cm against 35.9 × 41 cm) reflects the narrow unpainted margins that survive at the edges. These margins are a useful indication of how the scenes were originally set into the dossal's carpentry.

Stylistically the panel shows Ghissi's narrative manner at its most fluent. The figures are slender, with small oval heads and long straight noses. The drapery is laid in long, regular folds, and the colour is arranged in broad, bright fields: the red of the pall, John's rose mantle over a dark tunic, the yellows, oranges and pale greys of the mourners' garments. The fashionable short tunics, coloured hose and pointed shoes of the lay figures place the miracle in the streets of a fourteenth-century Marchigian town. The crowded but legible composition shows the clarity that distinguishes the cycle from the more repetitive devotional panels long thought to define the painter.

The panel, with its two companions, belonged to Mrs. W. Murray Crane of New York, under whose name it was photographed for the Frick Art Reference Library as a work of Allegretto Nuzi. She gave it to the Metropolitan Museum in 1969. Zeri included the three Crane scenes in his reconstruction of 1949, still under Nuzi's name. In 1975 he proposed that Nuzi designed the dossal and Ghissi executed it. Federico Zeri and Elizabeth Gardner catalogued the panels under Francescuccio Ghissi in the Metropolitan Museum's catalogue of Sienese and Central Italian paintings in 1980. Keith Christiansen illustrated them as Ghissi's in his study of fourteenth-century Italian altarpieces in 1982. Matteo Mazzalupi assigned the whole execution of the dossal to Francescuccio in 2017. The panel was shown with the other surviving scenes in *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece* (Raleigh, 2016–17; Portland, 2017).

  <figcaption>Saint John the Evangelist with Acteus and Eugenius, panel from a dismembered altarpiece of Saint John the Evangelist, c. 1370. Tempera on wood, gold ground, 36.5 × 41.3 cm. New York, The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 69.280.2, Gift of Mrs. W. Murray Crane, 1969.</figcaption>

This panel follows the raising of Satheus and brings to a close the story of Acteus and Eugenius, which began in the Raleigh panel where the two disciples beg John to restore their wealth. According to the Metropolitan Museum's summary, Satheus, raised to life, rebukes the two former disciples for their interest in worldly goods, and they return to John and beg forgiveness. Ghissi shows both moments in a single field, in the continuous narrative familiar from Trecento hagiographic cycles. The rebuke occupies the left half of the panel and the penitence the right. The same two young men appear twice, transformed in dress and attitude between one episode and the next.

In the *Golden Legend*, the risen youth describes to Acteus and Eugenius what he has seen in the other world. He tells them of the angels who wept for them and the demons who rejoiced at their fall, and of the palaces of heavenly glory they have lost for the sake of earthly riches. Moved by his words, they fall at the apostle's feet and ask for mercy. John prescribes thirty days of penance and prays that the gold and gems may return to their original nature as rods and stones. When this is done, the two are restored to grace. The panel compresses this sequence into two confrontations and one gesture of reconciliation.

On the left, Satheus stands in full figure, wearing a red cap, a salmon-coloured tunic and bright red hose. His right hand points upward to the heavens he has seen, and his left hand is open toward his listeners. Acteus and Eugenius, facing him, are dressed in the short pleated tunics, coloured hose and striped hoods of fashionable young men. One wears a purse at his belt, the emblem of the worldly attachment for which he is being reproached. One youth raises his hand in a gesture of protest or dismay, while the other lifts his fingers to his cheek. Ghissi renders the moment of moral recognition through these small gestures rather than through any marked facial expression.

On the right the same two men kneel before John, now in long, ash-grey robes that recall the habits of penitents. The apostle, in his usual dark tunic and rose mantle, bends his head and lays his right hand on the head of the nearer man in absolution, while blessing with his left. The second penitent holds out a few small pieces of gold in his open hand, returning the wealth that had led them astray. Behind them, a tuft of reeds rises from a dark rocky slope. It recalls the rods cut in the woods for the earlier miracle, now about to revert to their original nature, and links this scene visually to its Raleigh counterpart, where the same reeds grow on the shore.

At the top of the field, centred under the arch, hangs a dark almond-shaped form fringed with short rays. It has no equivalent in the other surviving scenes. The motif may evoke the opening of the heavens of which Satheus speaks, a celestial vision set above the narrator's raised hand. A reading of it as a dark cloud would make less narrative sense. The museum's summary of the panel does not discuss it, so this reading remains a hypothesis. It is nonetheless consistent with the panel's theme, the contrast between earthly and heavenly treasure.

The panel keeps its arched field, formed by a gilded moulding that springs from simple capitals at either side. In the upper corners, outside the arch, are fragments of the half-length saints that once filled the spandrels between the scenes of the dossal. On the left is a friar in a brown habit holding a red book, and on the right a bishop with a crozier and a red cope. Zeri observed that these spandrel saints matched those at the edges of the Chicago Crucifixion, where he recognized Saint Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>; Mazzalupi identifies Venantius and Louis of Toulouse among the lateral ones. The friar and the bishop here may therefore be Saint Francis and Saint Louis of Toulouse. Their presence supports Mazzalupi's proposal of a Franciscan church, San Francesco in Fabriano, as the dossal's original home.

The painting is a clear example of the qualities that distinguish Ghissi's narrative panels. The figures are elongated, the heads small and regular, and the hair rendered in fine parallel strokes. The colour is distributed in broad, flat areas: orange and salmon on the left, rose and slate on the right, set off against the dark rock and the gold. The repetition of the two protagonists in contrasting clothing, worldly finery on one side and penitential grey on the other, makes the moral transformation of the story immediately legible to the viewer. Seen beside the Raleigh scene where the same two men plead for the return of their riches, the panel completes a small diptych within the cycle on the right and wrong uses of wealth.

The panel's history is shared with its two companions in the Metropolitan Museum. It was owned by Mrs. W. Murray Crane, photographed for the Frick Art Reference Library under Nuzi's name, and given to the museum in 1969. Zeri published it in his 1949 reconstruction of the dossal as a work of Allegretto Nuzi, then in 1975 assigned its execution to Ghissi on a design by Nuzi. Zeri and Elizabeth Gardner catalogued it as Ghissi in 1980, and Keith Christiansen published it under the same name in 1982. In 2017 Matteo Mazzalupi gave the whole execution of the dossal to Francescuccio. It was shown in the 2016–17 exhibition *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece* in Raleigh and Portland.

The third Met panel shows the destruction of the Temple of Diana at Ephesus, the last of the three consecutive episodes preserved in New York. According to the Metropolitan Museum, this deed led to a further trial for the saint, the poisoned drink represented in the Raleigh panel. In the dossal the scene therefore marks the transition between the story of the two backsliding disciples and the final confrontations with the pagan priesthood. In the *Golden Legend*, the pagans of Ephesus drag John to the temple of Diana and order him to sacrifice to the goddess. He prays instead, and the temple collapses and the idol is shattered. Aristodemus, the high priest, then stirs up the crowd and demands the test of the poisoned cup.

Ghissi builds the scene on a simple three-part structure. On the left, John kneels in profile with his hands raised and joined, his head tilted back toward the sky. In the centre stands the temple, a small, tall building seen obliquely, with a pitched roof of yellow tiles and a round window in the gable. On the right are the pagan witnesses. The space between John and the temple is left almost empty, so that the gold ground and the bare strip of floor carry the line of the saint's prayer directly to the building.

The collapse is shown at the moment it begins. The walls of the temple are split by jagged cracks, part of the roof edge is breaking away, and small blocks of masonry lie fallen on the ground beside it. Inside the deep arched niche, a slender nude statue topples forward from its plinth. The column on which it stood is broken in two, its upper section falling while its lower part stands. The idol is not given the attributes of Diana. Ghissi follows the widespread medieval convention of the falling idol, a pale, naked figure that stands for pagan worship in general.

Behind John, an elderly bearded man in a turban and a pale mantle kneels or bends in the same direction as the saint. He has been read as a companion or disciple of the apostle. His oriental headgear, however, is the same as that given to the pagans of Ephesus in the Raleigh Poisoned Chalice. He may therefore be a pagan witness converted by the miracle, or the priest who will soon challenge John. The panel does not settle the point.

On the right, a man wearing a wreath or diadem, a purple mantle over red sleeves and a long red underskirt points emphatically at the temple with his outstretched right arm. He holds a staff or rod in his left hand, and a bearded companion stands behind him. His gesture and insignia mark him as a figure of authority among the pagans. Given the sequence of the legend, he may well be Aristodemus himself, the priest who will demand the poisoned cup in the next scene, or a magistrate of the city. As in the other panels, the identification follows from the text and the logic of the gestures rather than from any inscription.

The panel retains its gilded arch and the fragments of the spandrels outside it. In the upper right corner is a red seraph with outspread wings and a tooled halo, painted in a single flat red. In the upper left corner a red fragment survives of another figure. These spandrel decorations recall the arrangement Zeri reconstructed for the dossal. In that arrangement, half-length saints and angels filled the spaces between the arched scenes and continued at the edges of the central Crucifixion. The seraph is a natural choice for a dossal centred on the Crucifixion, where seraphim often accompany the cross.

The painting shows Ghissi's characteristic attention to architecture. The temple is constructed with simple planes, a shallow niche and a few ornamental details, and its oblique placement gives the panel a modest depth. The colour is restrained: John's red mantle and dark tunic, the warm brown of the temple walls, the purple and red of the pagan leader, set against a broad field of gold. The cracks across the temple walls and the tilting column are drawn with a precise, almost diagrammatic line. Through it the miracle is made visible as a physical event without any loss of decorum.

The panel belonged to Mrs. W. Murray Crane and was photographed for the Frick Art Reference Library under Nuzi's name as the “falling idol” scene, then given to the Metropolitan Museum in 1969. Zeri included it in his 1949 reconstruction of the dossal as a work of Allegretto Nuzi. In 1975 he proposed a design by Nuzi and an execution by Ghissi. It was catalogued as Ghissi by Zeri and Elizabeth Gardner in 1980 and by Keith Christiansen in 1982. In 2017 Matteo Mazzalupi gave the whole execution of the dossal to Francescuccio. The panel was shown in *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece* (Raleigh, 2016–17; Portland, 2017).

This is the third scene from the Saint John altarpiece now in the North Carolina Museum of Art, and the latest in narrative order among the surviving panels. In the cycle, which follows the *Golden Legend*, the scene comes immediately after the Met panel showing the destruction of the Temple of Diana. According to the Metropolitan Museum, that miracle led to a further trial for the saint: surviving a poisoned drink, the subject of the panel in North Carolina. Since the concluding panel of the series is lost, the Raleigh *Poisoned Chalice* is today the last surviving scene of the narrative. It leads toward the end of the story without showing it. The other panels are in the Portland Art Museum, the Metropolitan Museum and, for the central Crucifixion, the Art Institute of Chicago.

In the account of Jacobus de Voragine, after the collapse of the temple, Aristodemus, the high priest of the idols, stirs up the crowd against John. He declares that he will believe only if the apostle drinks a cup of poison. To show the strength of the venom, he first has it given to two men condemned to death, who die at once. John takes the cup, protects himself with the sign of the cross and drinks without suffering any harm. Aristodemus then raises a further condition: John must bring the two dead men back to life. The apostle hands him his own mantle, and when it is laid over the bodies the condemned men rise again. Aristodemus is converted. The panel shows the central moment of this sequence, the drinking, while the dead men still lie on the ground waiting for the next miracle.

Ghissi organises the scene with great clarity in three zones. On the left, John stands alone, isolated against the gold ground, in strict profile, with his head tilted back and the cup raised to his mouth. In the centre, the two bodies of the condemned men lie on the bare ground, forming a horizontal band that separates the apostle from the crowd. On the right, the onlookers press together in a compact group that is cut off by the edge of the frame, suggesting a larger crowd beyond. The large empty field of gold above the corpses is a deliberate interval. It isolates John's action and turns the drinking into a kind of liturgical gesture held in suspense.

The apostle's figure is built on few elements: a dark tunic, a broad red mantle gathered over the left arm, and a punched halo that stands out against the gold ground. The calm vertical of his body contrasts with the abandonment of the bodies on the ground and the agitation of the witnesses. The small cup that John brings to his lips is the iconographic core of the scene. From this episode came one of the Evangelist's most widespread attributes in late medieval art, the chalice from which a serpent or small dragon emerges as a sign of the neutralised poison. In Ghissi's panel the vessel is still shown in its narrative function, as an object being used in the action, rather than as an emblematic attribute.

The bodies of the two condemned men are laid side by side, parallel to the picture plane. Their legs are stretched toward John and their heads are turned toward the crowd, with one arm resting on the ground. They wear short tunics, one light and one darker, with a band around the waist. Their simple dress, very different from the ample mantles of the other figures, marks their humble status as convicts. The pale, uniform flesh tones and the rigid, motionless limbs convey the immediate effect of the poison. Placing them so that they lie toward the feet of the apostle and of the witnesses also makes them the visual pivot of the next miracle, the restoration to life.

The group on the right brings together several types of figure. In the foreground, a white-bearded elder in a yellow mantle and a hood points with his right index finger at the bodies on the ground. His prominent position and demonstrative gesture suggest that he may be identified with Aristodemus, who demands the proof and then the resurrection. Next to him, a bearded man in a red mantle, with a turban-like headdress, lifts his hand in a gesture of astonishment or prayer. Behind them, younger faces and other heads crowd together, partly cut off. The use of oriental-style headgear to characterise the pagans of Ephesus is a common convention in Trecento painting. The identification of Aristodemus with the yellow-robed elder rests on the text of the *Legenda* and on the logic of the gestures, not on an inscription.

The panel has the same formal vocabulary as the other scenes of the cycle: elongated figures, small heads, regular profiles and blond or white hair rendered in fine parallel strokes. The colour plays on the contrast between John's red mantle and the yellow of the witness's cloak, set against the dark ground. A row of punched decoration runs along the upper edge of the gold. The panel is shown in a gilded frame with twisted colonnettes at the lower corners. These recall the twisted columns that support the arch in the *Crato* panel, which Federico Zeri regarded as later additions. Unlike the *Crato* and *Acteus and Eugenius* panels, whose frames measure 55.2 × 57.1 cm, this one has a smaller frame (42.9 × 44.5 cm). The difference suggests that the present framing does not belong to the same series of modern frames. How much of the original carpentry survives is not known.

The museum's bibliography for the panel opens with Arduino Colasanti's 1906 study on Allegretto Nuzi and Francescuccio di Cecco Ghissi, published in *L'Arte*. It then entered the Kress literature under the name of Allegretto Nuzi. Under that name it appears in Bernard Berenson (1936), Federico Zeri, who reconstructed the whole dossal in 1949, the National Gallery of Art preliminary catalogue of 1941, Fern Rusk Shapley (1952 and 1968), and the NCMA catalogue of 1960. In 1975 Zeri proposed for the whole altarpiece a design by Nuzi executed by Ghissi. The attribution to Ghissi was then established by Keith Christiansen (1982) and accepted by Pietro Zampetti and Giampiero Donnini (1992); in 2017 Matteo Mazzalupi assigned the entire execution of the dossal to Francescuccio. The panel was also discussed by Christopher Lloyd (1993) and by Perri Lee Roberts (2009), and it was reproduced in the volume edited by Lucia Biondi and Andrea De Marchi, *Elogio del Trecento fabrianese* (2017). It passed from the collection of Baron Fassini in Rome to Contini Bonacossi in Florence, then to the Kress Foundation in 1932 and to the NCMA in 1961. It was shown in the exhibition *Reunited: Francescuccio Ghissi's St. John Altarpiece* (Raleigh 2016–17; Portland 2017).

## Collaborative Works and Attribution Challenges

The complex relationship between Francescuccio Ghissi and Allegretto Nuzi presents ongoing challenges for art historical scholarship, as the two artists worked in close collaboration on multiple projects that blur traditional boundaries of individual attribution. Federico Zeri's analysis of their professional relationship identified specific works where Ghissi's distinctive hand can be distinguished within larger collaborative enterprises. The polyptych with the Coronation of the Virgin in the Southampton City Art Gallery, formerly given wholly to Allegretto Nuzi, belongs to this phase of collaboration; its wings with angels and saints, now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, show the somewhat stiff, mechanical handling that Zeri recognized as Ghissi's.

The triptych featuring Saints Nicholas of Tolentino, Augustine, and Stephen in the Fabriano Pinacoteca represents another example of collaborative production where Ghissi's individual contribution has been identified through careful stylistic analysis. These collaborative works demonstrate the sophisticated organizational systems operating within major 14th-century workshops, where established masters could coordinate individual strengths to produce ambitious artistic projects. The attribution of specific hands within collaborative works requires detailed technical analysis that considers both stylistic characteristics and documentary evidence about workshop organization. Ghissi's "less fluid and often mechanical" approach, as identified by Zeri, provides criteria for distinguishing his contributions from those of his more established collaborator. The gradual recognition of Ghissi's individual artistic personality has required reassessment of multiple works previously attributed entirely to other masters, demonstrating the ongoing evolution of art historical scholarship. The complexity of these attribution questions reflects the sophisticated professional relationships that characterized major Italian workshops during the Gothic period.

The scholarly process of identifying Francescuccio Ghissi's individual contributions within collaborative projects has revealed important insights about 14th-century workshop practices and artistic production methods. Technical analysis of disputed attributions employs multiple methodological approaches, including detailed examination of drawing techniques, color application, and compositional strategies that reveal individual artistic personalities. The identification of Ghissi's "somewhat mechanical and stiff" hand within Allegretto Nuzi's later production suggests his increasing influence within the workshop hierarchy as the senior master aged. This pattern of artistic evolution reflects common workshop dynamics where younger collaborators gradually assumed greater responsibility for major commissions while maintaining established stylistic conventions.

The documentation of payments and commissions is scant, limited essentially to the payment of 1345, which makes the two signed works all the more important. Ghissi's signed and dated works provide essential reference points for developing criteria that distinguish his individual style from that of contemporary collaborators. The attribution to Ghissi of the frescoes of the Dormition of the Virgin and the Death of Saint Arsenius in the chapter hall of San Domenico in Fabriano shows his participation in mural decoration alongside his panel painting. The identification of his influence on other contemporary artists, including Andrea da Bologna in his Madonna of Humility at Corridonia, signed and dated 1372, reveals the broader impact of his artistic innovations beyond his immediate workshop environment. The ongoing scholarly debate about specific attributions reflects both the high quality of collaborative works produced in major 14th-century workshops and the sophisticated methods required for distinguishing individual contributions. The recognition of Ghissi's artistic personality has enhanced understanding of regional artistic development and the complex networks of influence that shaped 14th-century Italian painting.

## Conclusion

Francescuccio di Cecco Ghissi should be understood not as a secondary provincial figure but as a structurally important painter of the Fabriano school, whose career shows how a master rooted in a single Marchigian town could mediate between local devotional demand and the wider Gothic languages of central Italy. His figure, however, rests on a narrow documentary base. A confraternity payment of 1345 and two signed and dated panels, the Fabriano Madonna of Humility of 1359 and the Montegiorgio Madonna of 1374, are the only fixed points of a career that unfolded over some three decades. Everything else is attribution, and the reconstruction of his work exemplifies the balance between archival evidence and stylistic inference that medieval art history requires.

That reconstruction has been, above all, a work of separation. Ghissi's hand had to be distinguished from that of Allegretto Nuzi, in whose workshop he trained and worked. It also had to be distinguished from those of namesakes active in Fabriano at the end of the century, to whom the Gallarate Madonna of 1395 and the Perugian record of 1389 more probably belong. Matteo Mazzalupi's reading of the signatures, which takes CICCHI and CHISSI as two forms of the same patronymic, has removed even the apparent family surname. What remains is a painter defined by his works rather than by a biography.

The Madonna of Humility is the centre of that body of work. In the signed panels of 1359 and 1374, in the Fermo panel and in the smaller pictures attributed to him, Ghissi returns to the Virgin seated on the ground and nursing the Child, wrapped in gold-patterned silks and joined, in several versions, to the signs of the Woman of the Apocalypse. Older scholarship read this repetition as monotony. It is better understood as a deliberate theological and liturgical specialization, answering a precise demand from Dominican and Augustinian churches, where the type carried a doctrine of humility, incarnation and Marian privilege. The halo of the Fermo panel, inscribed MATER ORDINIS PRAEDICATORUM, makes that link explicit, and the panels from Santa Lucia Novella and from the two churches of Sant'Agostino point the same way.

Stylistically, his language is defined by an exacting linearism, decorative density and a controlled emotional tone. Gold grounds, textile patterning and halo tooling give the panels their Gothic splendour, while the intimate exchange between Virgin and Child and the carefully staged hierarchies of saints preserve the clarity of their religious function. The stiffness that Federico Zeri described as a less fluid and often mechanical hand, measured against Nuzi's fluency, is also a preference for line, ornament and legible gesture, which produces an art at once sumptuous and didactic.

The Saint John dossal, now divided among Portland, Raleigh, New York and Chicago, overturns the image of a painter of a single subject. Zeri reconstructed it in 1949 under Nuzi's name and in 1975 proposed that Nuzi designed it and Ghissi executed it; Mazzalupi has since assigned the whole execution to Francescuccio. The surviving scenes show a narrator of considerable clarity. Their compositions are built on opposed groups across a field of gold, and their figures wear the dress of contemporary Fabriano, as in the philosopher Crato's vair-lined cape. Their landscape and still-life details, such as the reeds and pebbles of the Acteus and Eugenius panel and the hammer and anvil of the Crato scene, carry the evidence of each miracle.

The relationship with Nuzi remains the key to both painters, but it is not one of simple dependence. Ghissi took part in works from Nuzi's workshop, such as the Southampton Coronation with its Houston wings and the triptych of Saints Nicholas of Tolentino, Augustine and Stephen in Fabriano, where shared models and divided labour produced works that resist modern categories of authorship. After Allegretto's death in 1373 he appears as the continuator of the workshop, serving confraternities, mendicant orders and monastic foundations. His influence reached other painters, including Andrea da Bologna. The frescoes attributed to him in the chapter hall of San Domenico in Fabriano show him drawing on the Franciscan cycle at Assisi, and Umbrian echoes in his style suggest contacts beyond the Marche. The spread of his works from Fabriano to Montegiorgio, Fermo and Ascoli Piceno maps the channels through which commissions and iconographies circulated in the region.

Several questions remain open. The Vatican Madonna of Humility hovers between Ghissi and Nuzi. The polyptych with Saint Venantius in the Fabriano Pinacoteca is given to him only tentatively, and its date is uncertain. The Perugian frescoes depend on stylistic attribution alone. The works recorded in San Francesco at Fabriano and at San Severino Marche are lost, and the date and place of his death are unknown. The dispersal of the Saint John dossal among American collections, where Kress labels long kept the name of Nuzi, shows how easily works moved between the two names. The very instability of these attributions has nonetheless been productive, forcing closer study of technique, punchwork and workshop practice. The 2016–17 exhibition that reunited the dossal showed how much can be recovered when the panels are seen together again.

Ghissi's significance therefore lies less in innovation than in consolidation and continuity between Nuzi's generation and the later Gothic painting of the Marche. He gave the Fabriano school a recognizable devotional image, carried Nuzi's workshop into the following years, and showed, in the Saint John dossal, that the same linear discipline could serve a sustained narrative. His case also illustrates a method. It begins from the few signed and documented works, then weighs the attributions built on them, and keeps the conjectures visibly apart. The panels examined in this entry follow that order, and they show a painter whose modest documentary trace is matched by a coherent and distinctive body of work.

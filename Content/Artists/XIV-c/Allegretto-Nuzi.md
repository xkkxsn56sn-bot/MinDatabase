*Allegretto Nuzi*, also occasionally referred to as Allegretto di Nuzio, was born around the year 1315 in the flourishing town of Fabriano, situated in the Marche region of central Italy. The city at that time was known for its vibrant community of artisans, paper-makers, and merchants, and this stimulating environment left a deep impression on the young painter. Accounts of his earliest years remain scant in the surviving documentation, yet art historians agree that Fabriano’s cultural climate had a decisive impact on his formative development. Although the precise day and month of his birth have not survived the erosion of centuries, the approximate date is generally accepted by modern scholars based on stylistic comparisons with other artists of the period, as well as fragmentary records of his early commissions.
He belonged to a family whose roots in Fabriano stretched back at least a few generations, and it is known that his parents maintained close ties with local guilds. His father, according to a later notarial document, may have been involved in the growing trade of textiles or possibly in the craft of paper-making, for which Fabriano would become famously renowned. Though no personal letters from the immediate household have surfaced, the family’s modest standing in the community is suggested by scattered references in municipal archives, indicating a household of comfortable but not extraordinary means. Allegretto’s mother, who likely hailed from the region’s rural outskirts, is thought to have influenced his early religious fervor by encouraging devotions to the Virgin Mary, a theme he would lavishly depict in his later altarpieces.

Relatives on his mother’s side appear to have maintained a small property outside the city walls, in a pastoral setting where the young painter would have spent time during festivals or times of civil strife. These glimpses of rural scenery are occasionally sensed in the naturalistic touches that slip into his otherwise highly stylized work, suggesting that the tranquil rhythms of the countryside left a quiet but persistent impression on him. Although the family was not aristocratic, they possessed a sturdy network of friends and neighbors, many of whom participated in the local confraternities devoted to religious charity and communal worship. This environment formed the bedrock of Allegretto’s spiritual and social consciousness, which in turn is repeatedly echoed in the sensitive expressions of the saints and Madonnas he would later render in luminous tempera.

## Familial bonds

Family life for Allegretto was further shaped by the tumultuous times in which he lived, marked by shifting political allegiances and occasional unrest within Fabriano’s ruling classes. The position of his kin, balancing fealty to one noble family or another and preserving a semblance of neutrality amid factional disputes, required careful diplomacy. These precarious political currents likely underscored the family’s belief in faith and community solidarity, and from a young age Allegretto seems to have grasped the potent combination of devotional imagery and civic identity. Documents hint that at least one of his brothers or cousins may have pursued a life in religious orders, while another relative occasionally served as a minor civic official, creating multiple channels of local influence that would later assist him in acquiring artistic commissions.

From the earliest days of his training, he was encouraged by relations on both sides of his parentage to cultivate his gift for painting. Various references speak of an innate talent recognized by his immediate family, who spared few resources to help him secure an apprenticeship with a local master. Though the exact name of his teacher remains uncertain, it seems likely that his parents placed him under the guidance of a recognized painter in Fabriano’s workshop network, ensuring that he learned the rudiments of pigment preparation, gilding, and panel construction. Family lore, though scant, hints that his mother and father were strongly supportive, possibly even paying for specialized materials like costly gold leaf—a reflection of the family’s deep faith and their hope that the young Allegretto might bring spiritual distinction not just upon himself, but upon their entire household.

The support of his siblings and cousins also proved vital when he was faced with the expenses of setting up his own modest workshop, once his apprenticeship had concluded. There are references to small loans within the broader family circle, an indication of the trust and pride they felt in Allegretto’s promising talent. Notarial documents, sporadically preserved, detail how the Nuzi kin helped negotiate fair terms for his earliest commissions, enabling him to hone his craft and begin establishing a name that would eventually resonate beyond Fabriano’s borders. Such familial solidarity remained a defining feature of his life, and its traces can be found in the thoughtful ways he represented sacred familial bonds in his compositions, particularly in his renditions of the Holy Family, Saint Anne, and the young Virgin Mary.

## Patronage

He was soon able to attract the attention of notable patrons whose backing would shape the trajectory of his career. Fabriano, at the time, was replete with opportunities for artists who could satisfy the demands of both religious institutions and rising merchant families. The local confraternities, which were fervently dedicated to venerating specific saints or performing acts of charity, were among the earliest circles to commission Allegretto’s paintings. They desired splendid and spiritually uplifting works for chapels, altars, and processional banners, and Nuzi’s sensitive color palette and delicate draftsmanship met their expectations for devotional grandeur. These confraternities, acting as a collective body of patrons, spurred his early breakthroughs, granting him the chance to demonstrate both skill and piety.
As word of his ability spread, civic authorities also began to commission him for projects tied to local governance or ceremonial display. Although the mid-fourteenth century was riddled with disruptions, the ruling councils deemed art a significant expression of political prestige, and so they allocated funds for various civic commissions that included ornamental frescoes or ceremonial panels. Allegretto’s conscientious approach, combined with his willingness to incorporate heraldic emblems or symbols of authority, positioned him as a favored creator of such official imagery. While the documentation is patchy, references in the municipal accounts clarify that he was occasionally entrusted with decorative undertakings intended to reflect both spiritual orthodoxy and civic magnificence.

Notable noble families, including certain feudal lords who oscillated between alliances with Papal States and other regional powers, found in Allegretto a painter who could convey their devoutness without neglecting subtle messages of worldly stature. His refined sense of composition, coupled with the breathtaking gold leaf that characterized so much of his oeuvre, effectively impressed patrons seeking to cast themselves as devout yet powerful protectors of the faith. Some of these commissions took shape in the form of small, personal devotional panels intended for private chapels, while others were full-scale altarpieces that adorned major churches. His willingness to balance tradition with inventive flourishes captivated these noble circles, who, in turn, provided him with stable financial backing.

Religious orders, such as the Franciscans<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> and Dominicans<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, also turned to Allegretto when embellishing their churches in Fabriano and nearby towns, commissioning him to create large polyptychs or narrative cycles that celebrated their respective saints and theological views. The painter was adept at tailoring his compositions to the contemplative ethos of each order, subtly modifying the expressions of saints or the arrangement of figures to align with particular spiritual devotions. This capacity to adapt without forsaking his signature elegance of line and color endeared him to monastic leaders, who left records praising the clarity of his storytelling and the radiant sincerity of his figures.

Later in his career, he attracted patrons from beyond Fabriano, including certain bishops, abbots, and aristocratic families residing in the broader region of the Marche, as well as from Umbria. Some sought him for the refinement of his illuminated style, and others coveted a painter who carried the aura of Fabriano’s emerging reputation in the arts. His patrons, both secular and ecclesiastical, saw in him an artist who could unify their desires for devotional gravitas with the graceful touches of courtly sophistication that were beginning to define the proto-International Gothic current. It was ultimately this wide range of benefactors—civic, noble, and religious—that assured Allegretto Nuzi a lasting presence in the artistic records of his era.

## Painting style

His painting style was a harmonious blend of tradition and subtle innovation, reflecting the transitional nature of Italian art in the mid-fourteenth century. In his earliest surviving works, one can discern the hallmarks of the Italo-Byzantine tradition, notably in the rigid postures of holy figures and the abundant use of gilded backgrounds, which conferred a divine luminosity upon scenes of the Virgin and Child or episodes from Christ’s life. Yet even in these early pieces, Allegretto’s personal touch emerged in the graceful arcs of his figures’ silhouettes and the gentle tilt of their heads, suggesting a measure of empathy and accessibility that was not always present in the more austere icons of the preceding generation.

As he matured, his palette broadened, reflecting a heightened interest in delicate tonal transitions and the interplay of light and shadow across drapery. The folds of garments became increasingly flowing, and the meticulously rendered brocades and textile patterns hinted at a refined sensibility that aligned with the developing Gothic taste for ornamental splendor. However, he never allowed decorative motifs to overshadow the spiritual gravity of his compositions, carefully balancing rich embellishments with the solemnity of the subject matter. While angels and saints might be adorned with swirling lines of gold, their expressions remained reverent, their gestures measured and imbued with a quiet dignity.

A distinctive feature of his approach was the manner in which he positioned holy figures, often arranging them in symmetrical groupings that created an immediate sense of order and narrative clarity. This was particularly evident in his multi-paneled altarpieces, where each saint’s posture and orientation played a deliberate part in guiding the viewer’s gaze toward a central Christ figure or the Madonna. Allegretto’s ability to orchestrate devotional images in a fluid yet purposeful configuration exemplified his mastery of compositional design and highlighted his respect for liturgical functionality: he wanted worshippers to feel both awe and consolation as they beheld his works.
He introduced subtle nuances in the facial features of his figures, imbuing them with a soft humanity that distinguished his works from those of strictly conservative painters. The slight inclination of a saint’s head, the wistful curve of a mouth, or the tender exchange of gazes between mother and child bespoke a desire to bridge the gap between the human and the divine. This compassionate sensibility resonated deeply in an age marked by conflicts and epidemics, offering a reassuring vision of sanctity to congregations who sought solace in the tranquil realm of painted altars. While the gold-laden backgrounds set the scene in a heavenly dimension, Allegretto’s figures never lost their approachable warmth.

He was also adept at smaller devotional panels, intended for private homes or personal chapels, which required a more intimate scale of representation. In these miniature altarpieces, one finds an even greater emphasis on precious detail: halos might be richly embossed, architectural frames carefully delineated, and gestures of tenderness given pride of place. The size of these works invited closer inspection, and Allegretto took full advantage by incorporating refined touches that hinted at an almost jewel-like quality. Such small panels often circulated beyond the immediate region, carried by traveling merchants or pilgrims, further spreading his stylistic hallmarks to distant locales.

Gradually, as his career advanced, the forms in his paintings became increasingly slender and elongated, revealing a graceful elongation that was a signature of the developing Gothic style. This stylistic evolution was not abrupt but rather evolved organically from his consistent search for elegance and spiritual refinement. Even when the compositions became more crowded or the narratives more complex, Allegretto preserved a sense of poised choreography, ensuring that each figure was both aesthetically balanced and anchored in the unfolding sacred story. In large altarpieces, the coherence of his style was especially evident in the rhythmic interplay among the saints and angels, unified by their shared orientation toward the central holy presence.

He took particular care with color harmonies, selecting hues that harmonized to create a cohesive atmosphere, rather than relying on jarring contrasts that might distract from devotional focus. Warm reds, sumptuous blues, and burnished golds formed a triad that recurred in many of his paintings, granting them a visual continuity that patrons found both beautiful and spiritually enriching. By tempering these vivid hues with delicate shading, he managed to convey a sense of volume and three-dimensionality, albeit within the limits of a pictorial tradition that still prized flat, abstracted backgrounds. His nuanced approach to color, therefore, stood at the forefront of a shift toward greater realism while remaining firmly rooted in Gothic aesthetics.

His later works illustrate a painter more confident in rendering emotive power, as evident in the solemn tears of sorrowful saints or the tender gestures of the Virgin cradling the Christ Child. These nuanced displays of emotion were a testament to Allegretto’s deepening interest in capturing both the human vulnerability and the divine compassion intrinsic to biblical narratives. Although he maintained the gilded surfaces and decorative splendor typical of his age, the emotional register of his figures subtly bridged the gap between the older, iconic tradition and the emergent desire for a more lifelike portrayal of devotion. In this way, his painting style foreshadowed the fuller blossoming of International Gothic that would sweep through Italy in subsequent decades.

## Artistic influences

Over the course of his career, Allegretto Nuzi absorbed and reshaped a variety of artistic influences. One of the primary currents that flowed into his work was the legacy of Giotto di Bondone, whose revolutionary sense of volume and spatial coherence had swept through central Italy a generation earlier. While Allegretto never fully adopted Giotto’s dramatic solidity, the echoes of Giotto’s emotional clarity and narrative lucidity can occasionally be discerned in the more intimate interactions between figures in Allegretto’s compositions. He seemed to admire the human warmth that Giotto brought to religious scenes, even if he chose to maintain the more sumptuous and ornate aspects of Gothic style.
He was also keenly aware of the flourishing Sienese school, where painters such as Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti brothers had pioneered a lyrical approach to line and color, transforming sacred art into an exquisite realm of refined elegance. The sinuous outlines and softly blended pigments of Sienese altarpieces found resonance in Allegretto’s own sensitivity to beauty and grace. Indeed, certain elements in his treatment of drapery and his preference for delicate gestures echo Sienese prototypes, suggesting that he either traveled to Siena or was exposed to Sienese works that circulated through the Marche and Umbria regions.

Florentine painting of the Trecento, though stylistically distinct from Siena’s, also left a mark on Allegretto’s evolving artistic language. The emphasis on structural form and the understated expressiveness championed by masters such as Taddeo Gaddi and Bernardo Daddi may have stimulated Allegretto’s interest in balancing decorative richness with a measure of compositional discipline. Although he never inclined fully towards Florentine realism, he did incorporate more naturalistic touches in the modeling of faces and in the spatial arrangement of figures, especially as he progressed into the later phase of his career.

The influence of local masters within Fabriano and surrounding towns must not be underestimated. Fabriano itself was already fostering a lineage of refined painting, and Allegretto, in his youth, would likely have studied altarpieces and manuscript illuminations crafted by lesser-known but highly skilled regional artists. These local traditions introduced him to sophisticated techniques of gilding and the minute detailing of ornamental borders, which he would refine and adapt to his own style. The synergy of these homegrown methods with broader Italian currents helped solidify a recognizable Fabrianese school of painting, to which Allegretto contributed significantly.

Another powerful influence was the growing culture of illuminated manuscripts, which were prized possessions of both monastic institutions and wealthy secular patrons. The meticulous detail and jewel-like quality of miniature painting found in manuscript illumination had a profound impact on Allegretto’s approach to smaller panels. It contributed to his use of intricate gold tooling, elaborate decorative motifs, and the refined interplay of jewel-toned pigments. Even in larger works, one perceives that sense of preciousness and careful craftsmanship that characterizes the best manuscript illuminations of the period.

## Travels

Contact with traveling merchants and pilgrims, who often carried with them small works of art, further widened his aesthetic horizons. The late medieval period was one of substantial movement along trade routes, and artists benefited from the exchange of ideas and techniques that occurred in such an environment. The cross-pollination of styles that resulted from these encounters broadened Allegretto’s repertoire, enabling him to assimilate Northern Gothic nuances and adapt them to the Italian context. He was not averse to experimenting with fresh compositional possibilities when inspired by foreign designs, though he usually tempered them with the traditions favored by his Italian patrons.

Some measure of influence also arrived via the decorative arts, such as tapestry work and metalwork, both of which were admired in Fabriano’s mercantile culture. The intricacy of woven patterns and the gleam of tooled metal likely fueled his fascination with surface ornamentation, which manifested in the richly clad angels and saints who populate his later works. This inclination toward opulent textures, while partly a response to patron demands, was also a personal artistic choice. He recognized that these sumptuous elements could elevate a painting’s devotional impact, allowing viewers to sense the awe-inspiring realm of the sacred through visual splendor.

Throughout his career, Allegretto maintained a discerning eye for the innovations of his contemporaries, but he never lost sight of the essential role of reverence in his paintings. The wave of spiritual reform and the intense devotion sweeping through monastic circles influenced his focus on contemplative expressions and saintly introspection. He was acutely aware that his art served as a bridge between the earthly congregation and the divine realm, so he filtered whatever aesthetic ideas he encountered through the prism of spiritual propriety, ensuring that each new influence was harnessed to enhance religious sentiment rather than overshadow it.

In his later years, the influences of varied schools and the cumulative lessons of his career converged into a style that was distinctly his own: ornamental and graceful, yet quietly suffused with human feeling. By then, he was regarded as a mature painter whose works commanded respect not only for their technical finesse but also for their ability to speak to the devout imagination. His reputation, shaped by both local admiration and the broader currents of Gothic art, positioned him as a figure who stood at the crossroads of multiple traditions, blending them into paintings that remain moving reflections of fourteenth-century piety.

He undertook several journeys during his lifetime, although detailed records of his movements are sparse. Artists of his generation often traveled to gain insights into emerging styles, secure commissions, or respond to the invitations of distant patrons, and Allegretto was no exception. Fabriano’s position within the network of routes linking the Papal States, the Marche, and other regions of central Italy facilitated relatively easy movement, and Allegretto is believed to have ventured to Ancona, Perugia, and possibly Orvieto in pursuit of opportunities to collaborate or study.

The impetus for these trips often came from direct requests or from the artist’s own curiosity. Visiting religious centers such as Assisi would have allowed him to study the monumental frescoes that had shaped the course of Italian painting, and it is conceivable that the echoes of Giotto’s achievements in the Basilica of Saint Francis found resonance in Allegretto’s deepening appreciation for emotive storytelling. Even brief stays in these cities could yield valuable encounters with fellow artists, monastic libraries, or private collectors, all of which broadened his perspective on contemporary developments in art.

He likely spent time in smaller Umbrian towns, too, where local confraternities or church authorities may have offered him commissions for altarpieces or decorative panels. These smaller centers, though lacking the fame of Florence or Siena, formed a tapestry of artistic fervor in which a painter of Allegretto’s standing could cultivate both experience and income. He would carry back to Fabriano any fresh stylistic insights gained along the way, weaving them into his already rich vocabulary of forms and ornamental motifs.

There is also a possibility, though not conclusively documented, that he journeyed at least once to Florence. The city was a formidable hub of artistic experimentation, and even a short sojourn there would have exposed him to the innovations of masters working under the patronage of powerful guilds and wealthy families. Some scholars posit that certain nuances in Allegretto’s mid-career works—particularly the increased spatial coherence and the discreetly more naturalistic portrayal of the human form—betray a passing familiarity with Florentine achievements. Though no explicit contract has been found to confirm a commission in Florence, the circulation of artistic ideas was fluid enough that even limited exposure could have a lasting impact.

His travels were not solely artistic in motivation; at times, they coincided with religious pilgrimages or the necessity of seeking safer quarters during outbreaks of unrest. Fourteenth-century Italy was often riven by political strife and the lingering threat of the plague. Allegretto may have found temporary refuge in localities that offered stability or that welcomed his talents as part of broader cultural or spiritual renewal projects. Such movements across the region would have deepened his empathy with the diverse communities he encountered, a sentiment later reflected in the tender variety of faces populating his devotional art.

Some accounts suggest that Allegretto’s reputation also led him into the orbit of patrons in the Papal States, possibly requiring travel to nearby enclaves loyal to the Pope. Even if the seat of the papacy was then largely in Avignon, the spiritual authority of Rome endured, and ecclesiastical representatives throughout the peninsula sought artists who could embellish their churches and chapels with suitably impressive works. It was entirely in keeping with Allegretto’s careful balancing of piety and splendor that he might have been summoned to bring his refined altarpieces to such patrons, though records remain fragmentary.

Late in his career, he is said to have revisited some of the sites of his earlier travels to oversee the refurbishment of works that needed restoration or to negotiate final payments for commissions spanning multiple years. These repeated visits forged enduring relationships between Allegretto and the communities he served. They also reinforced his status as a painter who was not merely confined to his place of birth, but who had carved out a reputation extending across a network of towns and ecclesiastical institutions. Each journey enriched his artistic repertoire, allowing him to integrate fresh ideas and maintain a lively engagement with the evolving Gothic idiom.

Yet despite the significance of his movements, Fabriano remained his spiritual and professional anchor. He returned there after each journey, bringing back new experiences and influences, which he distilled into the radiant works that emerged from his workshop. The city’s warm reception and unwavering faith in his creations provided a sense of continuity, a link to the family traditions and local devotions that first shaped his artistic calling. In that sense, his journeys, no matter how far, always ended with a reunion between painter and homeland, ensuring that Fabriano’s deep-seated piety continued to guide the final arcs of his artistic production.

## Death

He died in the year 1373, most likely in his hometown of Fabriano, and although the precise cause of death is unrecorded in the surviving documents, many historians posit that illness of some form, possibly an age-related ailment, claimed him. By this point, the region had been wearied by various hardships, including outbreaks of the plague and shifting political alliances, yet Allegretto Nuzi’s workshop remained active, supported by steady local devotion to his skillful portrayals of the sacred. His legacy was secured through a number of major works that have survived the centuries, carefully preserved in both European and American collections.

## Works of Art

### Saints and angels (1)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 1.jpg" alt="Saints and angels">
  <figcaption>Saints and angels, 1360-70, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 108 × 51 cm, Houston Museum of Fine Arts, Houston.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel is not an independent work but one wing of a dismembered five-part altarpiece, or pentatych. [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico) has argued that because of the prevalence of Franciscan saints across the pentatych's panels, it was most likely made for the high altar of the church of San Francesco in Nuzi's home city of Fabriano, though the church was demolished in 1864 and any reconstruction of the altarpiece's original arrangement remains theoretical. Scholars disagree on dating: Zeri proposed around 1370 with a possible collaboration with Francescuccio Ghissi, while [Alessandro Marabottini](/scholars.html#marabottini-alessandro), noting affinities with Bernardo Daddi, placed it earlier in Nuzi's career.

The panel has the typical ogival, gable-topped silhouette of a lateral element from a Trecento polyptych, set against a continuous burnished gold ground — the field is further worked with tooled and punched decoration in the haloes, a hallmark of Marchigian-Florentine workshops of this period (and one of the features, incidentally, used by scholars to attribute unsigned panels of this kind, since each workshop had its own punch tools). The figures are arranged in four superimposed tiers, an unusual but not unique compositional device that Nuzi and his circle used elsewhere to crowd a large celestial company — saints, angels, and a smaller selection of full-length devotional figures — into a narrow vertical field, building toward a fuller assembly that would have continued across the now-separated central and other lateral panels of the altarpiece.

In the pointed gable at the summit are two youthful heads in profile, fair-haired, inclined toward one another — most likely angels or youthful virgin saints, shown half-length and without identifying attributes, functioning as a celestial chorus rather than as individually named figures.

Below them, a tier of three: a woman frontal at center with reddish-brown hair worn loose, flanked by two figures in profile, one of whom wears a white wimple covering the throat and chin in the manner of a veiled female religious or a married/widowed laywoman — again without clear attributes to fix an identity, though the wimple suggests this is meant to read as a holy widow or a woman in penitential or domestic dress rather than a virgin martyr.

The third tier likewise has three figures: a woman in profile at left; at center a woman wearing an elaborate openwork crown over her veil, a costume detail strongly associated with royal virgin-martyr saints such as Catherine of Alexandria; and at right a tonsured man in a brown habit, his profile and dress consistent with a Franciscan friar — which would support the documentary argument, noted above, for a Franciscan dedication of the original altarpiece.

The lowest and largest register contains four full-length standing saints, rendered at a considerably larger scale than the figures above, each haloed and set on the gold ground without a painted floor or architectural setting (a convention typical of these lateral saint-tiers). From left to right: a woman wrapped in a voluminous red mantle over a paler undergarment, her hair worn long and loose, holding what appears to be a palm frond — the red drapery and unbound hair are conventional markers of female sanctity but, without a more specific attribute visible (no ointment jar, wheel, or tower), the identity cannot be fixed with certainty from the image alone; next, a woman in dark, sober drapery — closer to widow's or penitential dress — holding a shallow plate bearing small round white forms, an attribute that recalls the iconography of Saint Agatha, whose severed breasts are traditionally shown displayed on a salver, though this remains a proposed reading rather than a documented one; then a young, beardless figure vested as a deacon in a white dalmatic with gold-bordered orphreys, holding a palm of martyrdom in one hand and a small book or reliquary box in the other — the dalmatic and palm together are the standard attributes of a deacon-martyr, most often Saint Stephen or Saint Lawrence, and the two are normally distinguished by stones (Stephen) or a gridiron (Lawrence), neither of which is clearly visible here.

### Saints and angels (2)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 2.jpg" alt="Saints and angels">
  <figcaption>Saints and angels, 1360-70, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 107.6 × 51 cm, Houston Museum of Fine Arts, Houston.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the companion wing to the panel you shared earlier — same artist, date, and museum, with the dimensions given here (107.6 × 51 cm) essentially matching the first panel's 108 × 51 cm, confirming these were paired lateral elements of the same dismembered pentatych by Allegretto Nuzi, now divided between two panels in the Houston Museum of Fine Arts' Straus Collection. The format is identical to the first panel: a gabled, ogival panel on a continuous gold ground, with figures stacked in superimposed tiers building from small half-length heads at the summit to two large full-length standing saints at the base.

Top arch: Two half-length figures in profile face inward toward one another. The one at left has long, loose auburn hair falling over the shoulder, typical of an unmarried virgin saint or angel. The one at right holds a piece of patterned red-and-white drapery against the gold ground — possibly a martyr's banner or a fragment of heraldic textile, though the image doesn't give enough to read it with certainty.

Second tier: Three more figures: at left, a saint wearing a distinctive red hood or cap, gesturing with a raised hand in a manner conventionally used for teaching or preaching — this kind of red headcovering is the attribute most often given in Trecento painting to Saint Jerome, anachronistically dressed as a cardinal even though the office didn't yet exist in his lifetime, though I'd flag this as a plausible rather than certain reading. At center, an older man, balding, with grey hair and a sober dark robe, shown without a clear attribute. At right, a younger woman in profile with reddish-gold hair.

Third tier, where the panel widens into its rectangular lower section: at left, a bearded man holding a slender rod or staff-like object close to his shoulder — its shape isn't legible enough in the image to confirm an attribute such as a sword (which would suggest Saint Paul) or another implement. At center, a hermit-type saint with a long, flowing white beard and dark, heavy drapery pulled up around the head like a cowl — a type very commonly used in Franciscan commissions for Saint Anthony Abbot. At right, the edge of a white, gem-studded bishop's mitre is visible, belonging to a third large saint whose body is mostly cut off by the panel's curved frame.

Lower register, the two large standing saints that dominate the panel: At left, a bishop wearing a tall white mitre set with gems, a dark cope patterned all over with gold fleur-de-lis, over what appears to be a coarse brown habit visible at the wrist — and holding a gold crozier in one hand and a red, gem-studded book in the other. This combination of attributes — youthful bishop, fleur-de-lis cope over a Franciscan habit, crozier, book — is the standard iconography for Saint Louis of Toulouse, the Angevin prince who renounced his claim to the throne of Naples to become a Franciscan friar and was briefly bishop of Toulouse before his early death; he is recognized in art as a young bishop usually wearing a brown or grey Franciscan habit under a cope decorated with the French fleur-de-lis. His presence here would reinforce the Franciscan character already proposed for the whole pentatych on the basis of its other saints.

At right, a young woman in a pale gold robe with a dark green mantle draped across her body, holding a book in one hand and what appears to be a green stem or palm in the other. Given the strongly Franciscan cast of this altarpiece, a pairing of Louis of Toulouse with a major Franciscan female saint such as Clare of Assisi would make iconographic sense, but I want to be careful here — the book and the slender green attribute aren't distinctive enough on their own to confirm that identification over other possibilities (a virgin martyr with a palm, for instance), so I'd treat this one as genuinely open.

As with the first panel, these identifications above the lower register are my own attribute-based reading rather than a transcription of the museum's catalogue entry — Carolyn Wilson's 1996 Straus Collection catalogue or Zeri's study of the pentatych would be the place to confirm names with certainty, particularly for the upper-tier heads, which carry no attributes at all in either panel and may simply represent an undifferentiated celestial host rather than individually named saints.

### Madonna Enthroned with Child

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 3.jpg" alt="Madonna Enthroned with Child">
  <figcaption>Madonna Enthroned with Child, 1372, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 167 × 96 cm, Galleria Nazionale delle Marche, Urbino.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel shows the single, canonical subject of the enthroned Madonna and Child, set beneath an elaborately cusped and crocketed Gothic arch, its frame carved with a twisting rope molding and flanking colonnettes with foliate capitals — an unusually rich surviving carpentry ensemble for a Trecento panel of this kind, and one of the reasons the work reads so well as a coherent, integral object today.

The Virgin sits frontally enthroned on a gold-backed bench whose sides are visible at the lower corners, wrapped in a dark blue mantle patterned overall with small gold fleur-de-lis-like foliate sprigs, worn over a red under-robe brocaded with gold floral motifs and lined at the edge with an ochre border. Her head, inclined slightly toward the Child, is framed by a halo tooled and punched into the gold ground, and a veil drapes over her hair and shoulders. Her right hand steadies the Child at his waist while he stands upright on her lap.

The Christ Child, haloed, is dressed in a pale gold, patterned tunic cinched at the waist with a blue cord, with blue sleeves visible at the wrists. He turns toward his mother, one hand raised in a gesture of blessing, his bare feet resting on her knee.

At the base of the panel, along the plinth of the throne, runs a painted inscription band — visible but only partly legible at this resolution — of the type Nuzi regularly used to sign and date his major panels; a related image caption elsewhere records the panel's signature as reading "Hoc opus pinxit Alegrittus Nutii de Fabriano anno..." ("Allegretto Nuzi of Fabriano painted this work in the year..."), consistent with the 1372 date given for this panel. I'd treat the exact wording as needing verification against a photograph of the inscription itself before you publish it, since inscriptions of this kind are often abbreviated or partly effaced.

Unlike the two Houston panels you shared, which come from a dismembered multi-saint pentatych, this work is a single, self-sufficient devotional image — the Madonna and Child are the only figures represented, with no attendant saints or angels, which is consistent with it having been a central panel (as Marcelli proposes) that would originally have been flanked by separate saint-bearing wings rather than incorporating them within the same field.

### The Crucifixion

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 4.jpg" alt="The Crucifixion">
  <figcaption>The Crucifixion, 1355-60, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 43.5 x 20 cm, Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel has a gabled, triangular top rising to a sharp point, evidently once forming one element — likely a pinnacle or a small independent devotional panel — of a larger dismembered altarpiece complex, in keeping with the fragmentary, multi-part structure we've already examined in the two Houston lateral wings. The surface is worked throughout in burnished gold, tooled and punched in the borders and haloes, with passages of a mottled reddish-orange ground surrounding the principal figures — a warm, atmospheric backdrop rather than a neutral field, achieved through a technique of scumbled or abraded paint over the gold that gives the impression of a luminous, almost sunset-like sky behind the cross.

In the triangular apex, set against a dark, foliate mass suggestive of a stylized tree or grove, a bird — likely the pelican in her piety, the medieval symbol of Christ's redemptive sacrifice, shown feeding its young with its own blood — perches above the summit of the cross. This is a conventional Trecento device: placing an emblematic or typological reference to the Passion directly above the Crucifixion scene itself, reinforcing the sacrificial and Eucharistic meaning of the image below.

Christ is shown nailed to the cross at the panel's vertical axis, his body elongated and attenuated in the manner typical of Sienese-influenced Trecento crucifixes, his head inclined to his own right, a halo tooled into the gold behind it. He wears the customary loincloth knotted at the hip, and the wound in his side is marked in red beneath the ribcage. The arms of the cross extend nearly to the panel's edges, and at each extremity, in the upper corners, hover two small mourning half-figures emerging from the gold ground, their hands raised toward Christ in gestures of grief — these are the customary subsidiary figures of Ecclesia and Synagoga, or more simply Sun and Moon personifications, frequently included at the cross's arms in medieval Crucifixion iconography, though at this resolution I cannot confirm which specific pairing is intended here.

Flanking the corpus at chest height are four angels disposed symmetrically around the corpus: two smaller half-length angels at the extremities of the cross's arms, each apparently holding a small chalice or vessel to gather the blood flowing from Christ's pierced hands, and two larger, fully winged angels in flight lower down, at chest height, each reaching toward the wounds in a similar blood-gathering gesture. This is a standard four-angel configuration in Trecento Crucifixion imagery — the angels collecting Christ's blood from the wounds of the hands, feet, and side as a Eucharistic reference.

Below the cross, the panel divides into two groups, separated by the vertical shaft of the cross itself.

To the left stand three grieving figures, tightly clustered: at the rear, a man with a short beard and dark mantle, likely Saint John the Evangelist, his hands raised toward his face in a gesture of lamentation; beside him, a haloed woman in dark robes with her head inclined, and behind her another woman partly obscured, her red mantle visible at the edge of the group — this trio corresponds to the standard Marian cluster of grieving women and the beloved disciple positioned at the Virgin's side in Trecento Crucifixion imagery, though without further inscription I would treat the precise identification of each secondary figure as provisional.

Kneeling at the foot of the cross, in the traditional position of embracing its base, is a haloed figure in a vivid red mantle, hair loose and gaze upturned toward Christ — this pose, and the color and posture together, are the standard iconographic marker for Mary Magdalene, whose penitential embrace of the cross is one of the most consistent devotional motifs in Italian Trecento Crucifixions, deriving ultimately from the Franciscan-influenced imagery popularized in the previous century.

To the right, a solitary haloed standing figure, restrained in expression and gesture, wrapped in a rose-pink mantle over dark green undergarment, hands folded in quiet mourning — most plausibly the Virgin Mary herself, shown in her customary place at Christ's right hand (proper left as we view it), her sober stillness contrasting with the Magdalene's more demonstrative grief at the foot of the cross. It's also possible this figure could instead be a second evangelist or apostle figure, given that the Virgin is sometimes placed among the left-hand cluster instead; I'd flag this as an area to check against the Met's own catalogue entry, which likely names each figure with more confidence.

As with all three panels we've discussed from Nuzi's mature and late career, the gold-ground technique, tooled haloes, and the elongated, Giottesque-Sienese figure type are entirely consistent with his documented style; I was not able to retrieve the Met's full curatorial entry for this specific object, so — as before — I'd recommend cross-checking the individual figure identifications (particularly the two subsidiary half-figures at the cross's arms, and the exact identity of the two right- and left-hand groupings) against the Met's own object page or the relevant volume of Zeri and Gardner's *Italian Paintings* catalogue of the Metropolitan's collection before publishing.

### Busts of five Apostles

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 5.jpg" alt="Busts of five Apostles">
  <figcaption>Busts of five Apostles (fragment from a predella), c. 1360, tempera and gold leaf on panel, 35,3 x 135,5 cm, Musée des Beaux-Arts de Strasbourg.</figcaption>
</figure>

This panel is a predella fragment — a horizontal beam that would once have run along the base of a larger polyptych — showing five apostles in half-length, each set within its own niche beneath a continuous gilded arcade.

The five busts are separated by spiral-turned colonnettes with foliate capitals that support round-headed arches; the spandrels and the arch soffits are worked in tooled and punched gold, and the whole is enclosed within a carved wooden moulding, its lower rail decorated with a twisting rope pattern. Condition is frankly that of a fragment: the gilding is abraded in numerous passages, the frame is chipped and worn along the lower edge, and a small modern metal label plate has been inserted at the base beneath the central figure. Because a full apostolic college numbers twelve, this row of five must be understood as a surviving section of a considerably longer predella — or of a dispersed series across several such beams — with the remaining apostles now lost or separated.

At the far left is an older, balding man with a full greyish-brown beard, wrapped in a blue tunic and ochre mantle, holding a red-bound codex in both hands and turning inward toward the center. The high, bald forehead combined with the long beard is the physiognomic type conventionally used for Saint Paul, though the absence of his customary sword — he carries only a book here — means this identification should be treated as suggested rather than secure.

Second from the left is a youthful, beardless figure with fair, wavy hair, dressed in a rose-red mantle over a paler garment, one hand raised in a speaking or demonstrative gesture while the other holds a greenish-blue book. Youth and beardlessness are the standard markers for Saint John the Evangelist, the youngest of the apostles, but in a series of this kind other young apostles (Thomas, Philip, James the Less) are shown the same way, so again the identification rests on type rather than on a distinguishing attribute.

The central niche holds a bearded man with reddish-brown hair, distinguished from his companions by a notably richer costume: a gold-brocaded tunic patterned with small blue and red devices, worn beneath a lavender-grey mantle. He holds a red book in one hand and, in the other, what appears to be a short blade or hilt. If that object is indeed a knife it would point toward Saint Bartholomew, whose flaying knife is his usual emblem; if it is a sword it would revive the possibility of Saint Paul. I would not want to resolve that on the basis of the image alone — the object is small and partly worn — so I flag it as the single most consequential attribute to verify against the museum's own record. The central and most sumptuously dressed position could also, in principle, indicate that this figure was meant to carry particular weight within the series, but that is an inference from placement, not proof of identity.

Fourth is another young, beardless apostle, fair-haired, in a blue tunic with a coral-red mantle drawn across the shoulder, holding a dark-bound book and turning toward the center. As with the second figure, the youthful type is consistent with several apostles and cannot be narrowed without a further attribute.

At the far right is an older bearded man who is the one securely identifiable figure in the group: he holds a pair of keys, the unmistakable emblem of Saint Peter, and wears a mantle crossed by a band ornamented with small crosses. The keys — signifying the power of binding and loosing conferred on Peter — remove any doubt about this figure, and his presence establishes that the series was a college of apostles rather than a gathering of other saints.

The panel sits comfortably within Nuzi's maturity, when his Florentine training — the solid, Giottesque modeling of the heads — is combined with the Sienese-inflected taste for brocaded textiles and richly tooled gold that is visible here in the central apostle's patterned tunic. The heavy, rounded features, the deep-set eyes, and the punched decoration of the haloes are all characteristic of his workshop in this period.

### Virgin and Child, with Saints Mary Magdalene, James Major, Stephen, and a Bishop Saint

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 6.jpg" alt="Virgin and Child, with Saints Mary Magdalene, James Major, Stephen, and a Bishop Saint">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, with Saints Mary Magdalene, James Major, Stephen, and a Bishop Saint, c. 1346, tempera, silver, and tooled gold on panels with vertical grain, Center panel: 72.1 x 41.9 cm; Side panels [each]: 60 x 29.8 cm, Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia.</figcaption>
</figure>

This polyptych requires very little inference, because the saints are identified by their original tituli painted along the base of the frame — the gilded inscriptions you can read across the plinth: S· MAGDALENA, S· IACOBUS, the Marian invocation AVE MARIA GRATIA PLENA beneath the center, S· STEPHANUS, and S· NICHOLAUS. This makes it one of the most securely legible works in Nuzi's corpus, and it is worth noting at the outset that the Philadelphia Museum of Art's cautious title — "a Bishop Saint" for the rightmost figure — is in fact resolved by the panel itself, which names him Nicholas.

This is one of Allegretto Nuzi's earliest surviving works, generally dated around 1346, the very year he is documented in Florence enrolling in the Compagnia di San Luca. It belongs to the John G. Johnson Collection at the Philadelphia Museum of Art, and scholars regularly cite it as a touchstone for reconstructing the painter's initial phase, in which a Florentine stylistic component — the solid, sculptural modeling ultimately derived from *Maso di Banco* — is fused with an evident familiarity with the Sienese art of the Lorenzetti brothers. The medium is characteristic of the period, the silver typically deployed in ornamental passages and now often oxidized to a darker tone than originally intended.

The work is a five-part polyptych of exceptional decorative richness, its engaged Gothic frame among the more complete to survive from the Marchigian-Florentine ambit. Each of the five compartments is crowned by a crocketed, foliate-cusped gable rising to a fleuron finial, separated and flanked by slender twisted pinnacles. The gables are filled with deep blue grounds diapered with delicate gold foliate scrollwork, and each carries a trefoil or quatrefoil roundel: at the summit of the central gable a half-length figure of the blessing Redeemer, and in the lateral gables small roundels with angels. The picture fields themselves stand against burnished, tooled gold, framed within cusped and pointed arches carried on spiral colonnettes — the whole conceived as a miniature architectural screen.

At the center, larger in scale and set beneath the tallest gable, is the Virgin and Child, glossed by the inscription AVE MARIA GRATIA PLENA — the angelic salutation of the Annunciation, here functioning as a devotional apostrophe to the Madonna. Mary is shown half-length, wrapped in a dark blue mantle bordered in gold over a red gown, her head veiled and inclined tenderly toward the Child she supports on her arm. The infant Christ, haloed, turns toward her and reaches upward, his pale swaddling and rose-colored drapery falling across the Virgin's supporting hand. The intimacy of the exchange — the Child's gesture toward the mother's face, her downcast, contemplative gaze — reflects the affective, humanized Marian imagery that Nuzi absorbed from both Sienese and Florentine sources.

At the far left stands Saint Mary Magdalene (S· MAGDALENA), in a brilliant red robe, her long hair falling in a braid over her shoulder, holding in her draped hands her customary attribute, the covered vessel or pyxis of ointment — the jar of precious unguent with which she anointed Christ, and the emblem by which she is almost invariably recognized.

Beside her is Saint James the Greater (S· IACOBUS), shown as a bearded, mature apostle in a green tunic and rose-pink mantle. He holds a book in one hand and, in the other, the pilgrim's scallop shell — the emblem of Santiago and of the pilgrimage to his shrine at Compostela — which fixes his identity as James Major rather than any other apostle.

To the right of the Virgin is Saint Stephen (S· STEPHANUS), the protomartyr, represented as a beardless youth vested as a deacon in a sumptuously patterned dalmatic with diamond-diapered orphreys. He holds a red book and the green palm of martyrdom; conventionally Stephen is further marked by the stones of his lapidation, and while the palm and deacon's dress here are the primary identifiers, the titulus removes any ambiguity.

At the far right is Saint Nicholas of Bari (S· NICHOLAUS), the only figure in episcopal dress: a bishop in a gem-studded white mitre and a teal-green cope over patterned vestments, raising his right hand in blessing while holding the crozier. Although the museum's own title leaves him as a generic "Bishop Saint," the inscription confirms Nicholas — one of the most widely venerated bishop-saints of the medieval West, and a natural inclusion in a devotional polyptych of this kind.

### Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints Lucy, John the Baptist, and Anthony Abbot, a Female Martyr, and the Annunciate Angel

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 7.jpg" alt="Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints Lucy, John the Baptist, and Anthony Abbot, a Female Martyr, and the Annunciate Angel">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child Enthroned with Saints Lucy, John the Baptist, and Anthony Abbot, a Female Martyr, and the Annunciate Angel, c. 1350, tempera and tooled gold on panel with vertical grain, 43.5 x 23.5 cm, Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a small, portable devotional panel — at 43.5 × 23.5 cm it is domestic in scale, the kind of object made for private prayer rather than a church altar — and it comes, like the 1346 polyptych, from the John G. Johnson Collection at the Philadelphia Museum of Art, where it is likewise catalogued under Allegretto Nuzi's name. The museum's own descriptive title supplies the identifications of all the figures, so as with the Johnson polyptych the names rest on the institutional record.

Before the figures, a word on state, because it conditions everything one can say about the panel. The surface is heavily worn: the gold ground is abraded across much of its extent, revealing the reddish bole beneath, and there are pronounced vertical cracks and paint losses running through the Virgin's lap and the lower field. The engaged, gabled frame — original or early — is itself rubbed down to the gesso and bole in many places. The panel has the steeply pointed, gabled silhouette of a single-compartment tabernacle image, its apex filled by a half-length figure and its main field by the enthroned group, with subsidiary saints tucked into the upper corners and lower flanks.

In the apex of the gable, isolated against the gold, is a half-length angel in a red mantle, the head inclined and one hand raised in a gesture of address or salutation. This is the archangel Gabriel of the Annunciation; in a small devotional panel of this kind the Annunciate angel is frequently placed in the gable as a compressed reference to the Incarnation — the moment that sets in motion the maternity celebrated in the main field below. Its appearance without a corresponding Virgin Annunciate is normal in this reduced format, where the enthroned Madonna beneath effectively answers it.

At the center, seated on a throne whose red back rises behind her, the Virgin holds the Christ Child on her lap. She wears a dark blue (now nearly black) mantle enriched with a fine tracery of gold sgraffito scrollwork over a red gown, her head veiled and haloed with a punched and tooled nimbus. The Child, in a pale yellow-gold tunic, turns toward her, and the intimacy of the pair follows the same affective Marian type we saw in the Johnson polyptych. The throne and drapery are the most elaborately ornamented passages of the panel, the gold scrollwork of the mantle standing as the principal surviving evidence of the original refinement of the surface.

The four flanking saints. The two female saints occupy the upper corners, at the level of the Virgin's shoulders. The one at the upper left holds a small vessel or lamp-like attribute; the one at the upper right, in a coral-pink robe, is shown without a clearly legible emblem. The museum names one of them Saint Lucy and leaves the other as "a Female Martyr," and the distinction almost certainly turns on that small held object — if it is a lamp, it would identify Lucy, whose name (Lucia, "light") is often glossed by a lamp or flame, though Lucy is elsewhere given eyes on a dish or a palm. I would treat the specific assignment of Lucy to the left-hand figure as probable rather than certain from the image alone; the companion remains an unidentified virgin martyr precisely because she carries no distinguishing attribute.

In the lower left stands a gaunt, long-haired and bearded male saint in a rose-pink mantle, his hand raised to his breast, identified by the museum as Saint John the Baptist. The ascetic facial type is consistent with the Baptist, though I'd note that he is not shown here in his usual camel-hair garment, and the identification presumably rests on physiognomy together with a scroll or attribute now hard to make out in the worn surface — a point worth checking against the museum's record if you want to state it firmly.

In the lower right is an elderly, white-bearded monk in a dark monastic habit with a glimpse of red lining, standing in quiet frontality: Saint Anthony Abbot, the Egyptian desert father and father of monasticism, conventionally represented exactly so as an aged, bearded religious in a dark habit. His customary further attributes — the tau cross, bell, or pig — are not clearly present, but the monastic type is unambiguous and fits the museum's identification.

### Virgin and Child

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 8.jpg" alt="Virgin and Child">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child, c. 1360, tempera, silver, and tooled gold on panel with horizontal grain, 43.5 x 34.9 x 3.8 cm (framed), Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel is a half-length *Virgin and Child* set against a gold ground, enclosed in a plain gilded cassetta-type frame with stylized foliate sprigs painted in the upper corners. The condition is poor: the gold is heavily abraded, revealing the reddish bole beneath, and a dense craquelure runs across the whole surface. There are only two figures — no saints, angels, or donors — making this a direct, unmediated devotional image of the intimate half-length type meant to be held close in prayer.

The Virgin fills most of the field, her body turned slightly and her head inclined tenderly toward the Child she supports on her (proper) left arm, the other hand raised to steady him. Her face is of a markedly elongated, Gothic-mannered oval, with heavy-lidded downcast eyes, a long straight nose, and a small pursed mouth — a grave, withdrawn expression. She wears a dark mantle-veil (a maphorion-like covering drawn over the head and shoulders) that is the panel's most distinctive passage: it is modelled not with painted shadow but with dense, parallel gold striations — *chrysography*, gold-line hatching that traces the folds in a calligraphic web of light. The veil's edge over the forehead carries a decorated gold border with a running ornamental motif, and beneath it her gown is worked with a small repeating gold-brocade pattern across the breast. Her halo is a double-ringed, punched and dotted nimbus incised into the gold, now much worn.

The Child, also nimbed with a ringed halo, is held upright against her, his body partly bare and partly wrapped in a reddish-orange garment picked out with gold. He turns outward, one hand drawn toward his chest, his head round and the hair thinly indicated. The modelling here, like the mother's, is badly rubbed, but the same gold-enhanced handling of the drapery is visible.

Inscribed on the reverse (translation):

<p style="text-align: center;">Who will come devotedly to see this sacred image of the Most Holy Name of Mary and will recite there any prayer will gain 100 days of indulgence and reciting the litanies will acquire 50 days of concession called the "S.M." of Pope Pius VI</p>

### Virgin and Child; Man of Sorrows

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 9.jpg" alt="Virgin and Child; Man of Sorrows">
  <figcaption>Virgin and Child; Man of Sorrows, c. 1366, tempera and tooled gold on panel with vertical grain, 39 x 50.8 cm (overall), Philadelphia Museum of Art, Philadelphia.</figcaption>
</figure>

The object is a small portable diptych — two panels of near-equal size, originally hinged to fold together, made for private devotion rather than public display. Its two fields are conceived as a theological pair: on the left the Incarnation, in the image of the Virgin and Child; on the right the Passion and death, in the image of the dead Christ. The diptych thus compresses the whole arc of salvation history into two facing images, inviting the owner to meditate on Christ's beginning and end simultaneously — a devotional logic that the iconography of the left panel, as we'll see, makes explicit through a single prophetic detail. Both fields stand against burnished gold grounds framed by broad borders of dense tooled and punched ornament, with elaborately worked haloes; this punchwork is precisely the kind of workshop-specific evidence used in modern attribution studies of Nuzi and his Fabrianese circle, since each shop's punch tools leave a recognizable signature in the gilding.

The Virgin is shown half-length, her head veiled in a white maphorion and framed by a richly punched halo, inclining tenderly toward the Child she supports on her arm. She wears a dark blue-black mantle over a red gown, the mantle edged with an ornamental gold border of lozenge-and-cross motifs and marked at the shoulder with a gold star — the *stella maris*, the Byzantine-derived emblem of Mary's perpetual virginity that conventionally appears on the shoulder and brow of her mantle. Her features are of Nuzi's characteristic type, the greenish shadows of the flesh arising from the green-earth (verdaccio) underpainting showing through the modeling.

The Christ Child, in a rose-pink tunic and with his own punched halo, turns toward his mother and reaches for a small bird that he holds in his hands — a goldfinch, the *cardellino*, whose traditional association with the Passion (the legend links the red marking on its head to Christ's blood and the crown of thorns) makes it a standard prophetic attribute in Trecento and Quattrocento images of the Madonna and Child. Here the motif does double duty: within this diptych, the goldfinch in the Child's hand on the left panel points directly across the hinge to the dead Christ on the right, binding the two images into a single meditation on the Incarnation as the necessary prelude to the Sacrifice.

The second panel shows the *Imago Pietatis*, the Man of Sorrows — the dead Christ represented upright and half-length, as though rising from the tomb, in a devotional image-type of Byzantine origin that had become widespread in Italian panel painting. Christ's eyes are closed and his head inclines to one side in death; his arms are crossed over his body, and the wounds of the Passion are visible, the side wound and the marks on the hands rendered with trickles of red. The greenish pallor of the flesh is again partly a function of the verdaccio underpaint, but here it is expressively apt, lending the body the livid tone of death. Unlike a narrative Crucifixion or Lamentation, the Man of Sorrows is an atemporal devotional icon — Christ shown neither strictly alive nor merely a corpse, but presented to the viewer as the perpetual object of compassion and Eucharistic contemplation, the wounds displayed for prayerful attention.

This pairing of the affectionate Madonna with the Man of Sorrows is one of the most theologically economical formulas in later medieval devotional art, and Nuzi's version is a particularly refined small-scale example, the sumptuous punched gold and the tender handling of the Virgin's group set in deliberate contrast to the austere, isolated suffering of the facing Christ.

### Madonna and Child with Saint Mary Magdalene, Saint John the Evangelist, Saint Bartholomew and Saint Venantius

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 10.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child with Saint Mary Magdalene, Saint John the Evangelist, Saint Bartholomew and Saint Venantius">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child with Saint Mary Magdalene, Saint John the Evangelist, Saint Bartholomew and Saint Venantius, c. 1360, tempera and gold on panel, 118 x 200,7 cm, Pinacoteca civica Bruno Molajoli, Fabriano.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the *pentittico* (five-part polyptych) in the Pinacoteca Civica Bruno Molajoli in Fabriano, and its provenance is the key to reading it: it comes from the Cattedrale di San Venanzio in Fabriano. That origin accounts directly for the presence of Saint Venantius among the flanking saints, since he is the cathedral's titular and the city's patron — a nice instance of an altarpiece's dedication being legible in its iconographic programme.

The polyptych consists of five gabled compartments, each crowned by a triangular pinnacle enclosing a roundel, the whole conceived as a single architectural screen against burnished gold. The main register presents the enthroned company; the pinnacle roundels above carry a secondary Christological and hagiographic programme centred on the Crucifixion.

- The central compartment

At the centre the Virgin holds the Child on her arm against the gold ground. She wears a red gown strewn with small gold stars beneath a dark mantle marked at the shoulder with a gold star — the *stella maris*, the emblem of Mary's virginity that we also saw on the Philadelphia diptych — and a red fillet binds her hair. The Child, nude but for a pale cloth, turns with a tender, childlike gesture, one hand raised to his own chin and mouth. The type is Nuzi's characteristic fusion of Florentine solidity with the Sienese sweetness he drew above all from Ambrogio Lorenzetti, whose influence pervades his work.

- The four flanking saints, left to right

These are secured by the museum's record and by their attributes. First, Saint Mary Magdalene, holding the jar of ointment in her hands — here the tall covered vessel, wrapped in a white cloth, in a red robe. Next, Saint John the Evangelist with the open book on which are written the opening words of his Gospel: the two pages carry, in Gothic majuscule, the *incipit* of John 1:1 — *In principio erat verbum et verbum erat / apud deum et deus erat verbum* ("In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God"). To the right of the Madonna stands Saint Bartholomew, who holds in his right hand a knife, symbol of his martyrdom — the flaying knife, together with a book. Finally, at the far right, Saint Venantius with the white-and-red banner: the young martyr in courtly-military dress with a jewelled, diamond-patterned mantle, holding the vexillum whose colours are also the civic arms — the cathedral's patron closing the row.

- The pinnacle roundels

Above, the secondary programme unfolds around the Passion. In the central cusp is the Crucifixion; in the others, from the left, Saint Anthony Abbot, the Virgin, Saint John the Evangelist, and, at the far right, a female saint whose name is truncated in the museum's own online text (she appears in the image as a young martyr holding a palm and a slender attribute — a quill or frond). The logic of the arrangement is worth noting: the mourning Virgin and Saint John the Evangelist are placed in the two cusps immediately flanking the central Crucifixion, reconstituting the traditional trio of Calvary across the gables, while Saint Anthony Abbot and the female martyr occupy the outer roundels.

This is one of the anchor works for situating Nuzi in his maturity, around 1360, in that phase where — as the Fabriano scholarship stresses — his Florentine plasticity is increasingly inflected toward Sienese line and ornament, and specifically toward *Ambrogio Lorenzetti*.

### Saint Augustine between Saint Nicholas of Tolentino and Saint Stephen

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 11.jpg" alt="Saint Augustine between Saint Nicholas of Tolentino and Saint Stephen">
  <figcaption>Saint Augustine between Saint Nicholas of Tolentino and Saint Stephen, c. 1360, tempera and gold on panel, 118 x 139 cm, Pinacoteca civica Bruno Molajoli, Fabriano.</figcaption>
</figure>

The ensemble is Augustinian through and through, which its origin explains: it was painted for the Augustinian community of Fabriano — associated with the church of Santa Maria Nova and later recorded in the sacristy of Sant'Agostino. That context accounts for the pairing of the order's founder and lawgiver with its most celebrated friar-saint, and makes the whole a compact statement of Augustinian identity.

Three tall compartments stand beneath a shared arcade of pointed, slightly ogival arches against a burnished gold ground, the lower edge closed by a red basement band; the modern dark wooden frame is a later mounting. The figures are full-length, isolated each within its bay, and — characteristically of Nuzi's late manner — the gold grounds and the vestments are worked with dense tooled and burin-incised ornament, the drapery in effect *trapunta d'oro*, embroidered in gold.

Saint Augustine, at the centre, is shown as bishop of Hippo, in the black habit of his order beneath a sumptuous cope patterned in blue and gold with a broad orphrey band, wearing a jewelled mitre and, per the museum's description, holding the crozier in his right hand while supporting with his left the book of his Rule. The open book is the iconographic heart of the panel: its legible page carries, in Gothic minuscule, the *incipit* of the *Regula ad servos Dei* — *Ante omnia, fratres carissimi, diligatur Deus, deinde proximus, quia ista sunt praecepta principaliter nobis data* ("Before all else, dearest brethren, let God be loved, and then your neighbour, for these are the chief commandments given to us"). To display Augustine presenting the very text that governs the community for which the altarpiece was made is a pointed choice — the founder holding out the constitution of the order to the friars who prayed before the image. A second, rubricated passage follows lower on the page.

Saint Nicholas of Tolentino, at the left. A young, tonsured friar in the black Augustinian habit, haloed, holding — as the museum record specifies — the lily, symbol of purity, and the book of Holy Scripture. Nicholas (d. 1305) was the great model friar of the Augustinian Hermits, and his inclusion beside Augustine is the natural Augustinian pairing. One art-historical nuance worth building into your entry, given your interest in hagiography: Nicholas was not formally canonized until 1446, so an image venerating him with a halo around 1360–70 is evidence of the vigorous *pre-canonization* cult he enjoyed within his own order — the Augustinians promoted him as a saint for well over a century before Rome ratified it, and panels like this are part of that campaign. It's the kind of gap between cult and canonization that repays discussion on a site like yours.

Saint Stephen, at the right. The protomartyr, a young beardless deacon vested in a magnificent pale dalmatic diapered with gold and strewn with blue-and-red stellate motifs, over which fall gold-bordered orphreys and a maniple; he holds the green palm of martyrdom and a red book. Stephen recurs across Nuzi's oeuvre (we met him in the 1346 Philadelphia polyptych), and his deacon's dalmatic here is among the most lavishly ornamented passages in the whole group — a good instance of the "ostentatious luxury" the Fabriano scholarship identifies as the signature of Nuzi's final phase.

On dating, the De Marchi–Mazzalupi exhibition (*Oro e colore nel cuore dell'Appennino*, 2021) dates it *entro il 1362*, i.e. by 1362; while the Treccani *Dizionario Biografico* and others place the Augustine triptych later still, around 1372, calling it in effect Nuzi's last work (*ultima fatica*) shortly before his death in 1373.

This makes a satisfying pendant to the San Venanzio pentittico from the previous panel: both are Fabriano works with recoverable ecclesiastical provenances, one Marian and civic (the cathedral of San Venanzio), this one conventual and Augustinian, and together they let you show Nuzi working for two quite different local patrons in his home city.

### Our Lady of Humility

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 12.jpg" alt="Our Lady of Humility">
  <figcaption>Our Lady of Humility, 1366, tempera and gold on panel, 164,5 x 125 cm, Pinacoteca civica Tacchi-Venturi, San Severino Marche.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the jewel of the group: Allegretto Nuzi's signed and dated *Madonna dell'Umiltà* (Madonna of Humility) in the Pinacoteca Civica "P. Tacchi Venturi" at San Severino Marche. It is a work owned by the Comune di San Severino Marche, and after being lent to the 2021 Fabriano exhibition it was entrusted to the restorer Giacomo Maranesi of Fermo, who set up a working restoration laboratory within the gallery — so the surface you're looking at has recently been conserved.

Along the base runs the artist's signature and date, of which the legible portions read *…[All]egr[ittus de F]abriano me pinxit ano … LXVI* — "Allegretto of Fabriano painted me in the year [1366]." The surviving *LXVI* at the right end fixes the year, and this is one of the securely autographed cornerstones of Nuzi's late career, forming a trio with the Apiro polyptych (also 1366) and the Macerata triptych (1369). Scholars regard it as the most mature production of its author, and [Adolfo Venturi](/scholars.html#venturi-adolfo) held that this panel served as the model for most of the work of *Francescuccio Ghissi*.

The panel belongs to one of the most theologically resonant Marian formulas of the Trecento, the *Madonna of Humility* — the Virgin shown seated not on an elevated throne but low down, on cushions set upon the ground, the posture itself embodying *humilitas* (from *humus*, the earth). The type crystallized around the middle of the fourteenth century and spread rapidly through Italy; Nuzi was one of its principal disseminators, credited with contributing to the diffusion of the Madonna of Humility in the Adriatic region. Here the humble type is fused with a second tender formula, the *Virgo lactans* or nursing Madonna: the Child is drawn to the breast in the act of suckling, so the image unites humility and maternal nourishment in a single affective statement.

What raises this beyond a simple devotional Madonna is its cosmological framing, drawn directly from the *Mulier amicta sole* of Revelation 12:1 — "a woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and upon her head a crown of twelve stars." Around the Virgin's head Nuzi has set a ring of eight-pointed golden stars (the apocalyptic crown, several now abraded but clearly a coronal arc), and at the lower left, beneath the hem of her mantle, a reddish-gold crescent moon. The humble seated mother is thereby identified with the celestial Woman of the Apocalypse — an assimilation that would feed, in later centuries, into Immaculist imagery, but which here functions to exalt the very humility it depicts.

The Virgin sits enveloped in a deep blue mantle, its ground scattered with gold — foliate sprigs, vine-and-grape motifs and star-forms — and edged with a broad gold border, worn over a coral-red gown brocaded with pale gold palmette-and-flower devices. The mantle veils her head; her face, long and grave with the heavy-lidded, gently modelled features typical of Nuzi, inclines toward the Child. The minute all-over flowering of the textiles is exactly the quality the museum singles out as Sienese in derivation — the gold ground, the monumentality of the Virgin, and the mantle minutely decorated with little flowers all point back to Sienese pictorial culture. She is seated on large red-brocade cushions, tasselled at the corners and patterned with gold roundels, resting on a green band that reads as the grassy ground of her humility.

The Child, nude but for a pink cloth across the hips, the infant Christ turns to nurse, one hand raised to the breast. Around his neck he wears a string of coral beads — the red coral amulet that recurs in Trecento and Quattrocento images of the Christ Child, valued as an apotropaic charm against evil and, by its blood-red colour, a quiet prefiguration of the Passion. I'd add a candid condition note: the flesh of the Child reads as markedly darkened relative to the Virgin's, which is characteristic of the oxidation or degradation of the flesh pigments (or the green *verdaccio* underlayer showing through) rather than an intended complexion; the recent restoration may have clarified this, and I'd check the post-cleaning photographs before describing the Child's skin tone in print.

The large single panel is crowned by a polylobed, scalloped arch drawn and tooled in gold against the burnished ground — a cusped Gothic canopy over the figure — with the green ground-band and cushions establishing the low, earthbound register that the humility iconography requires.

### Madonna and Child Enthroned between Saint Michael the Archangel and Saint Ursula

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 13.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child Enthroned between Saint Michael the Archangel and Saint Ursula">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child Enthroned between Saint Michael the Archangel and Saint Ursula, 1365, tempera and gold on panel, 123 x 65.8 cm; left side panel: 91.3 x 41.8 cm; right side panel: 92 x 41.4 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the counterpart to the San Severino *Madonna dell'Umiltà* — the other great signed and dated anchor of Nuzi's maturity.

The Vatican record preserves an unusually full pedigree that also explains the iconographic programme. The triptych was probably commissioned by Nofrio di Santi (Onofrio Santi) for the chapel of Saints Ursula and Michael the Archangel in the church of Santa Lucia at Fabriano; it was still with the Santi family of Fabriano in 1634, and in 1664 they donated it to the church of Saints Leonard and Romuald alla Lungara in Rome, demolished in 1863; by 1800 it was in the collection of Agostino Mariotti, passed by inheritance to Donato Luparelli, and in 1820 was acquired by Pius VII for the Biblioteca Apostolica's Museo Cristiano, finally entering the Pinacoteca under Pius X in 1908.

The two flanking saints are therefore not arbitrary: Michael and Ursula are the co-titulars of the very chapel the triptych was made to furnish. This connects the panel directly to your Fabriano material, because Santa Lucia (Novella) is the same church that houses Nuzi's celebrated fresco cycle in the Chapel of Sant'Orsola, and 1365 is also the year of his Ursula-cycle frescoes (the *Journey of Saint Ursula*) in San Domenico — so the saint recurs across his work precisely in these years.

Along the base of the central panel runs the artist's signature and date, legible as *Alegrittus [Nu]tii me pinxit a[nno] D[omini] MCCCLXV* — "Allegretto di Nuzio painted me in the year of the Lord 1365." A separate, rubricated Latin inscription appears on the face of the throne-base above the kneeling donors, opening with a red initial. The inscription is a block of four lines of Gothic minuscule in brown ink, opened by a rubricated red initial A, set on the ochre face of the throne dais between the two donor groups. The text is a rhyming Marian invocation — the metrical, leonine-verse type extremely common on Trecento thrones — not a dedicatory or documentary inscription naming the patron. The opening is the most legible part, and it reads along the lines of:

"Ave, decus mundi, virgo... / "Hail, glory of the world, Virgin..." — a standard salutation formula.

The Virgin sits enthroned against a red-and-gold brocade cloth of honour, wrapped in a dark blue mantle — marked at the shoulder with the gold star, the *stella maris* — worn over a red gown with a gold border, her head veiled and haloed. The Child, seated on her lap in a pale salmon tunic, raises his right hand in blessing. Here Nuzi is still working in the plastic, Florentine-derived idiom of his central panels, the figures given real volume and gravity, before the more abstract decorativism of his very last phase. At the foot of the throne, in markedly reduced scale, kneel the donors — the family of the commissioner Nofrio di Santi — disposed in two little clusters, the practice of the *ex voto* portrait that places the patrons in perpetual adoration before the enthroned Virgin.

- Saint Michael the Archangel, left wing

The archangel stands as a youthful winged warrior in a full suit of plate armour with a red sash, holding a sword and a shield-like object worked in rose and gold, while beneath his feet coils the vanquished dragon-serpent — the defeated Satan of Revelation 12:7–9, whose overthrow is Michael's defining deed. The martial figure treading the serpent is the standard and unambiguous iconography of Michael as *princeps militiae caelestis*, captain of the heavenly host.

- Saint Ursula, right wing

The saint appears as a young haloed woman in a rose-pink gown and dark mantle, holding in one hand a white banner charged with a red cross — the vexillum that marks her as leader of her company of virgins — and in the other a sheaf of arrows, the instrument of her martyrdom, since according to her legend Ursula was shot with arrows by the Huns at Cologne after her pilgrimage with the eleven thousand virgins. The raised hand and the paired attributes of banner and arrows identify her securely.

Along with the 1366 San Severino *Madonna of Humility*, this 1365 triptych is one of the two firmly signed and dated pillars around which the rest of Nuzi's late chronology is built, and its documented provenance makes it especially valuable: it lets you tie a surviving, movable altarpiece to a specific Fabriano chapel and, through that chapel, to Nuzi's monumental Ursula frescoes in the same city — a rare case where panel and mural production by the same master can be set side by side around a single saint.

### Madonna and Child (and workshop)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 14.jpg" alt="Madonna and Child">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child, c. 1365, tempera and gold on panel, 34,6 x 19,9 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel is a half-length *Virgin and Child* against a richly tooled gold ground, the haloes and border punched with foliate and rosette motifs, and a band of ornament — with what reads as a now-illegible inscription — running along the top edge.

The Virgin dominates the field, her head veiled in a dark mantle whose edge is bordered with a fine X-and-dot ornamental band, worn over a red gown with a gold-embroidered neckline. The single most telling detail is the large gold sunburst star set on her shoulder: this is the *stella maris*, the star of the sea, the emblem of Mary's virginity that derives ultimately from the Byzantine Marian tradition, where stars on the shoulders and brow of the *maphorion* mark the Theotokos — we have met the same star, more discreetly rendered, on the Philadelphia diptych, the San Venanzio pentittico, and the Vatican triptych, so it is something of a signature of Nuzi's Madonnas. Her face is of his characteristic elongated oval, with heavy-lidded eyes and small, grave features; the modelling shows the greenish cast of the underpaint, and the surface carries a pronounced age-craquelure.

The Child is held at the right, standing upright and turning back toward her. He wears a sleeveless cream-and-gold tunic bound with a dark sash, the lower body bare and the small feet exposed, with fair curly hair and his own ringed, punched halo; one hand reaches up toward the Virgin's veil and breast in a tender, characteristically childlike gesture. The Virgin's large hand supports him below — the disproportionately long, elegant hands are again typical of Nuzi.

### Madonna Enthroned with Angels between Saints Catherine of Alexandria and John the Baptist (school of)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 15.jpg" alt="Madonna Enthroned with Angels between Saints Catherine of Alexandria and John the Baptist">
  <figcaption>Madonna Enthroned with Angels between Saints Catherine of Alexandria and John the Baptist, 1380-90, tempera and gold on panel, 36,4 x 28,7 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

The panel is a compact *sacra conversazione* — the Virgin and Child enthroned in the company of saints who inhabit a single, shared space rather than being partitioned into separate compartments as in a polyptych. This unified format, in which the flanking saints stand on the same ground and beneath the same gold sky as the enthroned Madonna, was one of the Trecento's most consequential compositional innovations, and its reduced, portable version — a single small tavola like this one, roughly 36 × 29 cm — was made for private devotion, a domestic altarpiece before which an individual or family could pray. The saints are not merely present; they mediate, presenting the devotee's prayers to the Virgin and, through her, to Christ.

At the centre the Virgin sits on an elaborate Gothic throne conceived as a piece of miniature architecture: clustered colonnettes, a stepped base, and a tall cusped and gabled canopy whose crockets and finials echo the stone tracery of a cathedral. The most telling detail is the canopy's soffit, painted a deep blue and powdered with gold stars, articulated by red ribs — a deliberate evocation of the vault of heaven, the *caelum*, so that the Virgin is quite literally enthroned beneath the firmament as Regina Caeli, Queen of Heaven. The device of the starry vault over the Madonna's throne is one Nuzi and his circle deployed to lift the earthly furniture of the throne into a cosmological register.

Mary is veiled in a blue-green maphorion and enveloped in a dark blue mantle over a red gown, her nimbus punched with a wreath of rosettes — the tooled foliate halo that is a hallmark of the Fabrianese shop. Her long, grave face, with its heavy-lidded eyes and small mouth, descends directly from Nuzi's own Madonnas, themselves inflected by the sweetness of Ambrogio Lorenzetti. On her lap the Christ Child, in a rose tunic and with his own ringed, punched halo, holds a small bird cupped in his hands. This is almost certainly the goldfinch, the *cardellino*: because the bird was believed to feed among thorns, and because a splash of red marks its head, it had acquired by the fourteenth century a fixed association with the Crown of Thorns and the blood of the Passion. In the Child's hands it is at once a plaything and a prophecy — the infant grasping the emblem of his own future sacrifice, so that the Incarnation on view already contains the Crucifixion.

The adoring angels. In the two upper spandrels, above the throne's gable, hover two half-length angels in blue-grey robes, haloed, their arms crossed reverently over the breast. They perform the age-old liturgy of adoration, framing the Virgin as the celestial court attends its queen; the motif of the pair of crossed-arm adoring angels flanking the enthroned Madonna recurs throughout Nuzi's production and that of his followers, and its presence here is one of the surest signs of the workshop's lineage.

At the left stands Catherine, identified beyond any doubt by a triad of attributes: the royal crown of her princely birth, worn over a rosette-punched halo; the palm of martyrdom in her hand; and, most conspicuously, the great spiked wheel at her side. Her legend held that the emperor Maxentius, having failed to defeat her in learned disputation with fifty philosophers — all of whom she converted — condemned her to be broken on a wheel studded with blades, which shattered at her touch before she was finally beheaded. As one of the *virgines capitales* and a sponsa Christi celebrated for her mystical marriage to the infant Christ, Catherine is an especially apt companion to the enthroned Child, and her pairing with the Baptist sets female virgin-martyr wisdom opposite male prophetic asceticism.

At the right is the Precursor, rendered in his standard Central-Italian type: gaunt and weathered, bearded and long-haired, an ascetic of the desert. He wears his penitential garment of camel hair — visible as the shaggy hide about his bare legs and feet — beneath a rose-pink mantle, and unfurls the scroll (the *banderole*) that customarily carries his testimony, *Ecce Agnus Dei*, "Behold the Lamb of God," here reduced to now-illegible characters. As the last of the prophets and the one who baptised Christ and pointed him out to the world, John is the hinge between Old Covenant and New, and his gesture toward his own breast, together with the scroll, marks him as the witness who directs the viewer's attention to the Christ Child at the centre.

Everything in the panel derives from Allegretto Nuzi — the rosette-punched haloes, the Lorenzettian faces, the crossed-arm angels, the starry throne — but the handling is drier and harder than in the master's autograph works, and the architecture of the throne is more fussily elaborate. This is the signature of the Fabrianese epigones active in the 1380s and 1390s, in the orbit of *Francescuccio Ghissi* (Francescuccio di Cecco) and the later workshop that perpetuated Nuzi's formulae for a generation after his death in 1373.

The panel is considerably worn, with pronounced craquelure across the faces and drapery and extensive abrasion of the gold, and the frame is damaged along its edges. The small blurred rectangle at the upper left is a digital redaction of a modern collection label, not an original feature.

### Vir Dolorum (and workshop)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Allegretto-Nuzi/image 16.jpg" alt="Vir Dolorum">
  <figcaption>Vir Dolorum, c. 1365, tempera and gold on panel, 34,8 x 20,3 cm, Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican City.</figcaption>
</figure>

The subject is the *Imago Pietatis* — the Man of Sorrows, *Vir dolorum*, a name drawn from Isaiah 53:3, "a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief," read by Christian exegesis as a prophecy of the suffering Messiah. It is not a narrative scene but a devotional icon of Byzantine origin (the Greek *Akra Tapeinosis*, "utmost humiliation"), in which the dead Christ is shown upright and half-length, as though risen from the tomb, presented frontally to the viewer for prayerful contemplation. The image is deliberately atemporal: Christ is neither alive nor merely a corpse in a narrative, but eternally displayed in his wounded death as the perpetual object of compassion and, above all, of Eucharistic meditation — the body whose sacrifice is re-presented on the altar.

Christ occupies almost the whole field, half-length, the body emerging from behind the lower framing edge in the manner of a figure rising from a sarcophagus. His eyes are closed and his head inclines gently to his own right, the face composed in the "sleep of death" — reclined, at peace, without the contortion of agony. Long wavy hair falls to either side of the shoulders and a soft beard frames the jaw; the modelling of the face, with its heavy lids and long nose, retains the Nuzi type even in this ruined state. The arms are crossed low over the abdomen, the hands laid flat against the ribs on either side — the standard gesture of the dead Christ in this iconography, at once a posture of repose and a display, drawing the eye to the wounds.

The instruments of the Passion are not shown, but their effects are: the lance-wound in the right side is marked by a vivid red gash from which blood trickles downward, and further rivulets of red run across the crossed forearms and the chest, indicating the marks of the nails and the general bleeding of the scourged body. These small red accents — almost the only colour surviving in an otherwise monochrome, abraded surface — concentrate the devotional charge of the image on the shed blood, the matter of redemption and of the Eucharist.

Behind the figure the gold ground is elaborately tooled: a broad border of punched foliate scrollwork frames the field, and behind Christ's head and shoulders a large incised and punched halo (or a rota of radiating ornament) spreads across the gold, its concentric bands of rosettes and dots still legible beneath heavy craquelure. This punchwork, workshop-specific in its tools, is precisely the kind of evidence Mojmír Frinta catalogued for distinguishing Nuzi's shop from his followers — relevant here, since your caption's "and workshop" registers exactly the question of how much of this modest panel is autograph.

The panel is much worn: the flesh has darkened and flattened, the gold is abraded to the bole in wide passages, and a dense age-craquelure covers the whole surface, so that the figure now reads as a brownish monochrome from which only the red of the wounds and the glint of the tooled gold emerge. This darkening is largely the oxidation of the flesh paint and the showing-through of the greenish *verdaccio* underlayer — the same phenomenon we noted on the Christ Child of the San Severino *Madonna dell'Umiltà*, and expressively fitting here, lending the body the true livor of death.

With its Madonna companion, this *Vir Dolorum* completes one of the most theologically economical devices in later-medieval private devotion: the diptych that folds the beginning and the end of the redemptive story — Incarnation and Passion, the Virgin's nurturing life and Christ's sacrificial death — into two facing images, so that opening the little panel set the joy of the Nativity against the sorrow of Calvary in a single glance. It is the same pairing you already have in the Philadelphia *Virgin and Child; Man of Sorrows*, which makes the two diptychs a natural comparative pair for the site: two versions, one in Philadelphia and one in the Vatican, of Nuzi's Incarnation-and-Passion formula.

## Conclusion

Allegretto Nuzi emerges from this dossier not as a peripheral provincial master, but as one of the central architects of Fabriano's Trecento visual culture. Working between inherited Italo-Byzantine forms and increasingly Gothic elegance, he forged a language that remained liturgically legible while opening space for tenderness, rhythm, and devotional intimacy. His paintings preserve the solemn authority expected by confraternities, monasteries, and cathedral patrons, yet they also reveal a painter consistently attentive to the emotional life of sacred images.

The chronology of his career, though still partly reconstructed through stylistic and inscriptional anchors, shows remarkable coherence: a formative phase shaped by local workshop discipline, a mature period of complex polyptych production, and a late phase of heightened refinement culminating in signed and dated milestones. Across these decades, his workshop appears both stable and adaptive, capable of meeting civic, ecclesiastical, and private devotional demand without collapsing into repetition. That continuity helps explain why Nuzi's corpus remains so useful for mapping artistic exchange across the Marche and Umbria in the fourteenth century.

Equally significant is the breadth of his patronage network. The same hand could answer Franciscan spirituality, Dominican didactic clarity, Augustinian identity, and civic-representational needs, recalibrating iconographic emphasis without sacrificing stylistic coherence. This flexibility was not opportunistic in a narrow sense; it was the mark of an artist who understood how visual theology, local politics, and social prestige converged in the commissioning of sacred art. His altarpieces therefore functioned as both devotional instruments and public statements of communal belonging.

On the level of style, Nuzi's achievement lies in balance: gold-ground transcendence paired with increasingly humanized faces; ornamental brilliance tempered by compositional order; inherited hieratic structures enlivened by softer gestures and relational glances. The recurring Marian focus, the disciplined handling of saints' attributes, and the nuanced shifts from monumentality to intimate scale all indicate a painter deeply conscious of audience and setting. Seen together, his works demonstrate that Trecento "tradition" was never static, but continuously reinterpreted through workshop practice, patron expectation, and regional dialogue.

His surviving panels, often fragmentary and dispersed, also remind us that what we now study as isolated masterpieces once formed integrated liturgical environments of woodwork, predella narratives, gables, inscriptions, and ritual use. Reconstructing those original contexts remains essential for interpretation, as does cautious attention to attributional questions in works labeled "and workshop" or "school of." Yet even within these uncertainties, the visual fingerprint of Nuzi's world is unmistakable: refined punchwork, controlled chromatic harmony, and an enduring dialogue between affective piety and formal splendor.

For the history of medieval Italian painting, Allegretto Nuzi represents a crucial hinge figure: rooted in Fabriano, receptive to broader central-Italian currents, and foundational for the later generation associated with Francescuccio Ghissi and related circles. His legacy is not only the individual quality of celebrated panels, but the durable model he offered for combining doctrinal clarity, artisanal sophistication, and emotional accessibility. In that sense, his oeuvre stands as one of the clearest testimonies to how a regional master could shape, and not merely reflect, the artistic and spiritual imagination of the late Trecento.

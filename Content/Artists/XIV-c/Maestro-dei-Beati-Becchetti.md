## Introduction: a name, a place, and a misunderstanding

Some anonymous masters of the Italian Trecento have been built up through decades of connoisseurship, polyptych reconstructions and quarrels over attribution. Others remain modest labels attached to a single ensemble, and the *Maestro dei Beati Becchetti* is one of these. It is easy to assume that the name denotes a late-fourteenth-century painter working in Fabriano and the Marches, and that it derives from a fresco of the Blessed Giovanni and Pietro Becchetti in the church of Sant'Agostino. That assumption has to be corrected at the outset, because a careful reading of the sources does not support it. In the catalogue of the Pinacoteca civica Bruno Molajoli, in the specialist literature on medieval wood sculpture in the Marches, and in the regional encyclopaedic literature, the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti is a sculptor. The museum's own profile describes him as a late-Gothic sculptor "dagli intensi accenti drammatici", active in the second half of the fourteenth century. It attributes to him the polychrome wooden sculptures from the old oratory of the Holy Sepulchre, which the [Augustinians](/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order) Pietro and Giovanni Becchetti built in 1393 and which later took the name of the two friars in local devotion.

The eponymous work, then, is not a fresco of two Augustinian blessed. It is a group of carved and painted figures that once made up a Calvary and a Holy Sepulchre inside an oratory designed as a surrogate for pilgrimage to Jerusalem. The Becchetti in the name are the patrons and founders of that devotional space, not the subject of an image. This reframing matters for more than tidy terminology. Several questions that seem natural for a "painter" of this name have no basis in the literature: his share in the fresco cycles of the chapels of Sant'Agostino, his relation to the [Maestro di Sant'Emiliano](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Maestro-di-Sant-Emiliano.html), his supposed panels in the Vatican Pinacoteca, his handling of the Madonna of Humility, his place in reconstructions attributed to [Federico Zeri](/scholars.html#zeri-federico). As far as I have been able to establish, no scholarly source attaches this name to a painter or to a painting. What follows reconstructs the master as he actually exists in the literature, sets out the limits of what can be said about him, and places his work within the dense figurative culture of Fabriano around 1400. That culture has its own painters, and they have sometimes been blurred with him in popular accounts.

Modern study of this master rests on three contributions. The first is [Giampiero Donnini](/scholars.html#donnini-giampiero)'s work on Fabrianese wood sculpture and on the oratory. It culminated in the 1993–94 exhibition *I legni devoti* and continued in a study of the oratory's Passion iconography published in 2008. The second is [Raffaele Casciaro](/scholars.html#casciaro-raffaele)'s essay, which presents itself as an "ipotesi" (hypothesis) on the master and places him within the problem of the Gothic *crucifixi dolorosi* between the Marches and Umbria. The third is [Andrea De Marchi](/scholars.html#de-marchi-andrea)'s recent involvement with the oratory and with the Fabrianese Trecento, above all through the 2021–22 exhibition on [Allegretto Nuzi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Allegretto-Nuzi.html) and his public call to recover the oratory as a sacred theatre. All other contributions, whether older local historiography, museum and exhibition texts, regional catalogues or university research, are referred to here without naming their authors.

## Sources and method: what counts as evidence

Anonymous masters invite overstatement, so it helps to set out at the start what kinds of evidence are available and how far each can be trusted. There are four layers, and they must be kept apart.

The first layer is documentary. The Becchetti themselves are reasonably well documented as members of the Augustinian Hermits. Their academic careers and final years appear in the order's archival records, and Augustinian hagiography draws on those records. For the sculptor, however, there is no document at all. No contract, payment, signature or inscription is known to name the carver of the Calvary group. The date of the oratory's foundation, usually given as 1393, belongs only in part to this documentary layer. Local historiography transmits it with the telling formula "si tramanda" ("it is handed down"). It is repeated by the Pinacoteca, by the regional encyclopaedic literature and by the hagiographical dictionaries. But it is a date for the pilgrimage and foundation, not for the carving of the statues.

The second layer is the works themselves as described in the scholarly and museum literature. Here the evidence is firm: six polychrome wooden statues from the oratory, now in the seventh room of the Pinacoteca civica, catalogued there under the master's name. Their dimensions, state of preservation and restoration history are partly published. The catalogue records the height of the Crucifix, for example, and notes that restoration revealed details that had been hidden.

The third layer is attribution. The six statues are securely grouped under the master's name, in the sense that they share a provenance and are consistently treated as one ensemble by one workshop. Attempts to widen the corpus beyond Fabriano belong to a more conjectural layer. The most notable of these is the extension of the name to a wooden Crucifix at Gualdo Tadino in Umbria.

The fourth layer is interpretive and speculative: the sculptor's origin, whether local, Umbrian or transalpine; his relation to the painters of Fabriano; his exact chronology. Here the literature offers hypotheses, sometimes conflicting ones, and this essay reports them as such.

A final methodological caution concerns the secondary material that now surrounds the oratory: press coverage of its decay and of campaigns to save it, tourist guides, heritage-organisation pages and image-bank captions. These sources are useful for the building's modern history, but they are unreliable on art-historical detail. One stock-photo caption dates the Pietà to the "second half of the 15th century", a century later than every scholarly source. Some press accounts place the *Lignum vitae* fresco in the fourteenth century, although it is generally held to be an early-fifteenth-century work by Lorenzo Salimbeni. Such slips are more than trivial. They show how easily a thin art-historical profile gets distorted, and they may lie behind the confusion that turned this sculptor into a painter.

## The Becchetti: documented lives, hagiographical accretions, and cult

The two men whose name the master bears were cousins, not brothers as some popular accounts say, and both belonged to the Augustinian community at Fabriano. Augustinian hagiography, drawing on the order's records, gives their careers in some detail. Giovanni had taught at Rimini and gained his bachelor's degree in theology in 1385. He was then sent to Oxford, where he became a master of theology, and after returning to Italy he was appointed regent of the Augustinian house of studies at Perugia. His appointment is placed shortly after his return in 1391. The last documentary trace of him is a letter dated 7 May 1420, preserved in the order's archives in Rome. Pietro is recorded as studying for the lectorate in 1385, at Padua according to some accounts. In 1388 he was teaching at the Augustinian studium in Rimini, where the Prior General made him visitator to investigate complaints about that house. The last trace of Pietro is a letter from the Prior General dated 20 February 1421. The customary death dates, about 1420 for Giovanni and about 1421 for Pietro, therefore mark the last documents rather than recorded deaths.

These are the lives of two learned mendicants, moving within the order's network of studia (Rimini, Padua, Perugia, Oxford) and holding administrative responsibility. That context matters for their patronage. The idea of reproducing the holy places of Jerusalem in a Fabrianese oratory did not come from untutored popular piety. It came from men trained in theology and familiar with the order's international networks, and at least one of them, Pietro, had travelled to the Holy Land himself.

Around these documented facts a layer of hagiographical accretion has formed, and the historian must separate it from the record. One tradition makes the Becchetti descendants of an English family related to Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury. This is a genealogical legend, probably drawn from the surname, and no document supports it. Hagiographical texts also disagree over the date of Pietro's pilgrimage. Some Augustinian popular hagiography gives 1383, while the museum, the local historiography and the regional literature all give 1393. On balance 1393 should be preferred, since it is the date consistently attached to the foundation of the oratory. The 1383 variant may be a slip, or a separate tradition that cannot now be checked. Local historiography adds that Pietro later returned to the Holy Land accompanied by Giovanni. This too is transmitted as tradition rather than document.

The cult of the two blessed grew up in stages, and it is easy to run these stages together. According to the Augustinian tradition, Pietro was venerated from the time of his death, and his images carried the halo of the blessed. The funerary chest painted with his miracles, discussed below, is itself evidence of an early cult in the 1420s. The bodies of the two friars were eventually moved from the friars' common burial to a chest in the church, and in 1565 they were translated into the oratory of the Holy Sepulchre, where a fifth altar, added for the purpose, received them. Formal confirmation of the cult came much later, with a decree of Gregory XVI in 1835, and their feast is kept on 2 July. The museum's short profile of the master compresses these stages. It speaks of the beatification and the translation of the remains as the moment when the oratory took the friars' name. This telescopes events almost three centuries apart. The name "Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti" is a product of the sixteenth-century translation and the cult that followed, and the art-historical name of the anonymous sculptor comes, in turn, from that devotional name of the building.

## The Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre: foundation, arrangement, fortunes

The oratory stands in the cloister of the Augustinian complex of Sant'Agostino at Fabriano. Since the suppression of the religious orders that cloister has been absorbed into the grounds of the town hospital. Descriptions of the site mention a fourteenth-century stone portal in the cloister wall, with a zigzag band and a cross at its apex, and the surviving shell of the chapel that held the Becchetti tomb. The Sacri Monti documentation centre, which lists the oratory among Italian replicas of the holy places, describes it as a commemorative complex that the Augustinian Becchetti created at Fabriano after 1393, on their return from pilgrimage. They placed single figures and groups of painted wood on altars distributed through an oratory dedicated to the Holy Sepulchre. The same description observes that the arrangement and the changes in level might loosely allude to the places they stood for. What prevailed, however, was a "sacra rappresentazione" fixed in images, in which expressive sculpted figures visualised the "mysteries": the Calvary, flanked by the Holy Sepulchre and the tomb of the Virgin, and the Madonna of the Pietà.

Local historiography gives the layout in more detail, and the Pinacoteca's text for its seventh room confirms it independently. There were originally five altars. The first, raised on twelve steps, was dedicated to the Crucifix and called "Monte Calvario". On either side of it stood small chapels, one representing the Sepulchre and the other the tomb of the Virgin. The second altar, which recalled the Virgin's meeting with Christ on the way to Calvary, was called the "Valle di Giosafat". The third, placed lower, was dedicated to the Madonna del Pianto and recalled the tears shed at the Deposition. The fourth was dedicated to the Madonna delle Grazie. The fifth was added later and received the bodies of the two blessed in 1565. The museum places the statues within this scheme. The Crucifix stood on the Monte Calvario with St John at its foot. The Virgin, set apart, was on the Valle di Giosafat altar. The Pietà was on the lowest altar, that of the Madonna del Pianto. The Dead Christ lay in the Holy Sepulchre chapel beside the Calvary, and the Dead Virgin in the chapel of the Virgin's tomb. The museum also suggests, cautiously ("probabilmente"), that Nelli's painted chest for the blessed stood in the chapel below the Monte Calvario.

The result was a small topography of the Passion and of the Virgin's death, arranged over several levels so that devotees could move from station to station. Although the scale was entirely different, the conception belongs to the long tradition of European "Jerusalems" built as substitutes for pilgrimage. Andrea De Marchi called the oratory a "straordinario teatro sacro, che surrogava il pellegrinaggio in Terra Santa" (an extraordinary sacred theatre that stood in for pilgrimage to the Holy Land), gutted by modern neglect after centuries of devotion. The phrase captures what the sculptures were for. They were not isolated devotional images on altars. They were actors in a fixed performance, and each makes sense only in relation to its station, its height and its neighbours.

The later history of the building explains why the sculptures are now in a museum. The oratory was badly damaged in the earthquake of 1768 and then restored, enlarged and altered. It was closed to the public at the beginning of the twentieth century. In 1977, according to local press accounts, the oratory was temporarily reopened to save the surviving wooden statues, which had been found heaped together inside it. They were recovered and restored under the Soprintendenza at Urbino, and have since been shown in the Pinacoteca civica, where a room is devoted to them. The empty oratory, damaged again in the earthquakes of 1997 and 2016, has been the subject of repeated recovery campaigns. In the ninth edition of the national heritage organisation FAI's census "I Luoghi del Cuore" (2018, results announced in February 2019), the oratory collected 5,598 votes, ranking seventy-second in Italy and second in the Marche. None of this bears directly on attribution, but it explains a basic fact of study: for half a century the master has been seen only out of context, in the neutral space of a museum room, cut off from the altars, steps and chapels that gave his figures their meaning.

## The historiographical construction of the Master

The Maestro dei Beati Becchetti is a late creation of art history. The older local historiography of Fabriano, from the early twentieth century and its mid-century surveys of the town's churches, concentrated on the cult of the Becchetti and the history of the oratory rather than on the stylistic identity of the carver. The critical personality emerged only after the statues were recovered and restored in the 1970s, as part of a wider rediscovery of Trecento wood sculpture in the Fabriano area.

The decisive moment was the exhibition *I legni devoti. Sculture lignee del '300 nel territorio fabrianese*, held in the church of San Domenico at Fabriano from 18 December 1993 to 25 September 1994 and edited by Giampiero Donnini. Its contents make clear how the problem was framed. The catalogue opened with an essay on Marchigian sculpture of the Trecento. It then dealt in turn with the Maestro dei Magi di Fabriano, the Maestro della Madonna di Campodonico (a carver, not to be confused with the fresco painter known as the [Maestro di Campodonico](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Maestro-di-Campodonico.html)) and "una bottega lignea fabrianese del secondo '300" (a Fabrianese wood workshop of the later fourteenth century). There followed a section on the Holy Sepulchre with a study of the oratory of the Beati Becchetti, a separate treatment of the oratory's paintings, and a discussion of two further wooden crucifixes in the Fabriano area.

The Becchetti statues were thus placed within a local sequence of anonymous carvers who produced a remarkable body of polychrome wood sculpture in and around Fabriano in the fourteenth century. The oratory was treated as a unit in which sculpture and painting coexisted, but they were studied separately. In the same year Donnini published a study of the oratory and its Calvary group in a volume on a restored portal. In 2008 he returned to the subject with a paper, published on pages 141–150 of the proceedings volume *Di ritorno dal pellegrinaggio a Gerusalemme* (Ponzano Monferrato, 2008) from study days held at the Università della Calabria in May 2005, on the oratory as "un'iconografia della Passione del primo '400 a Fabriano" (an iconography of the Passion of the early fifteenth century at Fabriano). The title's placing of the ensemble in the early Quattrocento, rather than simply "late Trecento", is significant, and I return to it below.

It was also Donnini who first offered, as far as the available summaries show, the observation that has most shaped later discussion. The regional encyclopaedic literature, citing his work, describes the Calvary group as including a Crucifix, a *patiens* with an "extraordinarily serene" face, and "una Pietà dalle linee spezzate che tradisce la matrice nordica del suo artefice" (a Pietà of broken lines that betrays the northern matrix of its maker). This remark, that the angular, fractured drapery of the Pietà points to a transalpine formation, is the seed of the question of origins.

The second contribution is Raffaele Casciaro's essay, presented at a conference on Marchigian wood culture held at Matelica on 20 November 1999 and published in the proceedings, *Nuovi contributi alla cultura lignea marchigiana* (Sant'Angelo in Vado: Editrice Grafica Vadese, 2002), pp. 31–56. The title under which specialists usually cite it is "Ipotesi sul Maestro dei Beati Becchetti e spunti sulla questione dei crocifissi gotici dolorosi tra Marche e Umbria". In the author's own list of publications, however, the essay appears under a slightly different title: "Un'ipotesi sul Maestro dei Beati Becchetti e qualche spunto sulla questione dei crocifissi tedeschi medioevali tra Marche e Umbria". The difference between "gotici dolorosi" and "tedeschi medioevali" (Gothic dolorous crucifixes versus medieval German crucifixes) is revealing, whichever form is the printed one. It shows that the essay approached the master through the problem of German, or German-type, crucifixes in central Italy, a problem that international scholarship on the *crucifixus dolorosus* has long discussed. Casciaro's essay has since become a standard reference for the Umbro-Marchigian branch of that problem. Studies of dolorous crucifixes in the Alto Adige and in Sicily cite it for the Umbro-Marchigian examples, next to the Umbrian surveys.

The exact content of Casciaro's "hypothesis" must be reported with caution. I have not been able to consult the printed text, and none of the secondary sources available summarises its argument in detail. It can be inferred with reasonable confidence that the hypothesis concerns the master's formation and the extent of his oeuvre across the Marchigian–Umbrian border. Two facts point that way. The essay's subtitle frames it within the question of crucifixes between the Marches and Umbria. And the same scholar had written catalogue entries ("Schede", pp. 139–142) for the first volume of the catalogue of the Museo Civico of Gualdo Tadino at the Rocca Flea, published by Electa in 2000, where a wooden Crucifix is attributed, in reports of later restorations, to the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti. Whether Casciaro proposed that attribution, whether he argued for a German-trained sculptor working in the Apennine towns, and whether he proposed a documented name cannot be stated as fact here. On the last point, no identification of the master with a documented artist appears in any source I have been able to consult.

The third contribution belongs to the most recent phase. The 2021–22 exhibition *Allegretto Nuzi e il '300 a Fabriano. Oro e colore nel cuore dell'Appennino*, held at the Pinacoteca civica from 14 October 2021 to 30 January 2022, was co-curated by Andrea De Marchi together with a second curator. It set some thirty works by Nuzi, eleven of them lent from foreign museums, beside a selection of sculptures by other artists of the territory, whose polychromy the organisers described as clearly reflecting the influence of Allegretto and his school. For the present subject, the exhibition matters less for any new attribution (I have not been able to check whether its catalogue discusses the Becchetti master directly) than for the fact that it renewed the case for a unified reading of Fabrianese painting and sculpture. During its preparation De Marchi inspected the oratory with the town authorities, hoped to offer a virtual reconstruction and a chance to visit the site during the exhibition, and described the building in the terms quoted above.

This three-stage history (local rediscovery and exhibition, specialist hypothesis within the international debate on the dolorous crucifix, and recent reintegration into the history of Fabrianese art as a whole) is the whole historiography of the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti. It is a slender one. It contains none of the stages that a painter of this name would require: no reconstruction by Federico Zeri, no Vatican panels, no discussion of a fresco of the blessed, and no proposed identification with the young Ottaviano Nelli or with a documented Fabrianese painter.

## The corpus: the Calvary group from the oratory

The master's secure corpus is the ensemble of six polychrome wooden statues from the oratory, now in the Pinacoteca civica: the Crucifix, the Grieving Virgin, St John the Evangelist, the Pietà, the Dead Christ and the Dead Virgin. The museum catalogues all six under his name, and all are said to have been commissioned by the Becchetti on their return from pilgrimage. Unlike attribution by connoisseurship, the grouping rests first on shared provenance and a shared programme. The statues were made for one space and one devotional scheme, and they have always been treated as the work of one workshop. The claim that they share a single hand, rather than a workshop with several assistants, is a stylistic judgement that no published technical analysis has confirmed, and it should be held with the usual reserve.

The Crucifix is the dominant figure and the best described. The museum records it as polychrome wood, second half of the fourteenth century, 210.2 cm high. It has lost the wooden cross on which it originally hung, and shows Christ at the moment of death. The catalogue notes that careful restoration has recovered the quality of the modelling and brought out details that had been hidden: blue and gold decoration on the loincloth, and bruises and hairs painted on the arms and legs. It stresses the "acceso realismo fisico" (heightened physical realism) of the figure, which gives the death of the Redeemer an intense drama. The body is shown racked by suffering, with a deep wound in the side, and the head is bowed forward, without a crown of thorns. Other descriptions shift the emphasis. Local historiography speaks of a "profoundly human" figure "with gentle features and thin, stunted limbs". The regional encyclopaedic literature, following Donnini, calls it a *patiens* with an "extraordinarily serene" face.

These descriptions are less contradictory than they may seem. They point to a formal tension that seems central to the master's art. The body carries the marks of the Passion with unsparing insistence: bruises, hair, wound, thin limbs. The face keeps a composure that some observers have read as serenity. The question is how such a figure relates to the *crucifixus dolorosus* in the strict sense, the type of German origin with its emaciated body, contorted limbs and often extreme pathos. The descriptions available suggest a figure touched by that type but softer in expression. This fits the regional literature's claim that the Crucifix contrasts with the more pathetic figures around it. That claim, however, rests on published descriptions and photographs, not on first-hand technical study.

St John the Evangelist offers the clearest contrast. Local historiography describes it as the opposite of the Crucifix's calm spirituality: a figure of "intensità patetica" (pathetic intensity), hands clasped at the breast, face inclined onto the shoulder. The Grieving Virgin, placed at the Valle di Giosafat altar and set apart from the Calvary, and the Pietà, on the lowest altar of the Madonna del Pianto, together provide, in the same account, a strong devotional presence within the staging of the whole. The Pietà is the piece most often singled out for stylistic comment. The phrase quoted above, "linee spezzate" betraying a "matrice nordica", refers to it, and it is the image chosen most often to represent the master in the museum. It shows the seated Virgin with the dead Christ across her knees, his body covered by a long loincloth. The type itself, the *Vesperbild*, is one whose northern origins and spread into Italy in the late fourteenth century are well established in the general literature. A Pietà of this kind in a Fabrianese oratory of the 1390s would therefore be, in itself, a sign of transalpine models, whatever the origin of the carver.

The last two figures, the Dead Christ and the Dead Virgin, lay in the side chapels that reproduced the Holy Sepulchre and the tomb of the Virgin. Local historiography describes them as lying on a "humble catafalque", completing the work. Pairing Christ's tomb with the Virgin's, the latter a site in the valley of Jehoshaphat venerated by pilgrims to Jerusalem, fits the oratory's aim of reproducing the pilgrim's circuit rather than a single narrative of the Passion. The recumbent Virgin is also an unusual subject for monumental wood sculpture in Trecento Italy. Its presence here suggests that the programme was shaped by what the patrons had seen, or knew, of the holy places.

The main conjectural addition to the corpus is the wooden Crucifix in the Museo Civico at the Rocca Flea in Gualdo Tadino, across the Apennine watershed in Umbria. A report in the periodical *Vox Militiae* (April 2014) on restorations presented at the Rocca Flea describes it as "la scultura in legno dipinto raffigurante il Crocifisso attribuita al Maestro dei Beati Becchetti". The attribution may well come from the specialist literature on the Marchigian–Umbrian crucifixes discussed above. Until its origin and arguments can be checked, however, it should be treated as a proposal, not as settled fact. If accepted, it would matter a good deal: it would turn the master from the carver of one commissioned ensemble into an artist working across the Apennine route between Fabriano and Umbria, a route along which painters, carvers and devotional models are well known to have moved in the fourteenth century.

A last element of the oratory's decoration must be kept firmly outside the master's corpus. The 1993–94 exhibition treated "the paintings" of the oratory separately from the sculptures. The painted works associated with the oratory, the *Lignum vitae* fresco and the funerary chest of Blessed Pietro, carry other attributions, as discussed below. Nothing in the literature suggests that the sculptor himself painted anything beyond the polychromy of his own statues, which may in any case have been the work of a painter-collaborator, as was common practice.

### The Serene Patiens: The Becchetti Crucifix in the Pinacoteca of Fabriano

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Maestro-dei-Beati-Becchetti/maestro-dei-beati-becchetti-01.jpg" alt="Polychrome wooden statue of the crucified Christ without its cross, arms raised diagonally. The emaciated body shows carved ribs, a bleeding side wound with painted blood running down the right leg, and red scourge marks on the limbs. The bowed head with closed eyes and brown hair is serene; a long grey-green loincloth with gilded borders reaches the knees">
  <figcaption>Christ Crucified, second half of the 14th century, polychrome wooden statue, height 210.2 cm, Fabriano, Pinacoteca civica "Bruno Molajoli"; from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre (Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti), Sant'Agostino.</figcaption>
</figure>

Of the six figures that survive from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre at Sant'Agostino, the Christ Crucified is the largest and the most commanding, and it was the pivot of the whole devotional theatre: this is the image that stood on the "Monte Calvario", the first of the oratory's altars, raised on its flight of twelve steps above the surrounding stations. Carved in the second half of the fourteenth century and standing 210.2 centimetres high, the figure has lost the cross to which it was originally fixed, and it is precisely this loss that gives the museum photograph its strange, arresting quality. Deprived of its support, the body seems suspended in mid-air, arms flung diagonally upward, so that the modern viewer confronts not a crucifix but the crucified body itself, isolated as a piece of sculpture in a way its maker never intended.

The pose repays close attention. The arms do not extend horizontally, as on the standard Latin cross, but rise at a steep diagonal from the shoulders, tracing a broad V above the head. Whether this reflects the form of the lost cross cannot be stated with certainty, but the configuration is at least consistent with the raised-arm mounting familiar from the Gothic *crucifixi dolorosi*, the "dolorous" crucifixes of transalpine origin whose branch-like or Y-shaped crosses pulled the arms upward and stretched the body into an image of extreme tension. The observation must remain at the level of morphology — no source documents the original cross — but the silhouette alone places this figure within the orbit of that northern type, and it is on such features that the long-standing question of the master's "matrice nordica", his possible transalpine formation, has turned.

The anatomy carries the argument further. The body is drawn out and emaciated, the ribcage articulated rib by rib beneath taut skin, the sternum a sharp ridge, the abdomen sunken above a gently swelling belly whose navel is precisely marked. The legs hang almost vertically, thin and faceted, the knees barely flexed, terminating in the superposed feet pierced by a single nail hole — the three-nail scheme that had become canonical in the Trecento. The gash of the lance wound opens in the right side of the chest, carved in depth rather than merely painted, and from it the polychromer has drawn long rivulets of blood that run down the flank, stain the loincloth, and continue in thinning threads down the length of the right leg to the ankle. Across the arms, torso and shins run the short red strokes of the scourging, the painted bruises and weals that the restoration carried out under the Soprintendenza recovered from beneath later accretions, together with the fine painted body hair on the limbs that had likewise been hidden. It is this recovered surface that justifies the museum's insistence on the figure's "acceso realismo fisico": the realism belongs as much to the painter's brush as to the carver's gouge, and the two are inseparable.

Against this insistent record of suffering, the head strikes a different note. Bowed forward and inclined toward the right shoulder, framed by hair painted in warm brown that falls in carved tresses onto the shoulders, the face is composed rather than contorted: the eyes closed, the brow smooth, the short beard neatly ordered, and no crown of thorns interrupting the calm oval of the skull. Here lies the tension that has divided the descriptive literature, in which the same figure has been called both dramatically realistic and "extraordinarily serene", a *patiens* of gentle features. The photograph shows that both readings are true, and that their coexistence is the work's essential statement: the body testifies exhaustively to the Passion while the face already belongs to death's repose, the *Christus patiens* subsiding into peace at the very summit of the oratory's ascent. One may reasonably suspect a deliberate calibration, for the surviving companions — the St John with clasped hands and cheek sunk to the shoulder, the Pietà with its broken, angular folds — carried the pathos that the Crucified himself withholds.

The loincloth is the passage in which the sculptor and polychromer worked most closely together, and it is unusually ample for the type. Rather than the short, agitated *perizoma* of many dolorous crucifixes, it falls nearly to the knees in a long skirt of greyish-green cloth, knotted and folded over itself at the right hip, its surface traversed by curving carved folds and enriched with gilded hems and bands of gold ornament that the restoration brought back to legibility — the blue and gold decoration the museum's catalogue singles out. This sumptuary note, gold thread on humble cloth, tempers the figure's asceticism and speaks the pictorial language of Fabriano around 1400, a town whose panel painters had made the marriage of gold and colour their signature; whether the polychromy is owed to a local painter's workshop remains, as for the whole ensemble, an open and important question.

Seen in the round, the figure's proportions confirm that it was made to be read from below. The slight elongation of the legs, the forward pitch of the head, the deep undercutting of the loincloth's overfold all anticipate a viewer standing at the foot of the twelve steps, looking up at a body silhouetted against the "Monte Calvario". In the museum the statue hangs at eye level against a neutral wall, and something of its rhetoric is inevitably lost; but the photograph, taken frontally and evenly lit, at least allows the modern eye to recover the carver's syntax — the great V of the arms answering the narrow vertical of the legs, the diagonal of the bowed head breaking the symmetry at exactly the point where devotion was meant to fasten.

Within the master's small secure corpus this Crucifix is the anchor: the work from which the drama of the Dead Christ in the Sepulchre chapel, the grief of the Virgin at the "Valle di Giosafat", and the fractured lament of the Pietà all took their measure. And within late-Trecento Marchigian sculpture it is one of the principal witnesses to the arrival of the Gothic dolorous type in the Apennine towns, standing at the head of the comparative series that the specialist literature has traced between the Marches and Umbria. That it was commissioned by two learned Augustinian theologians for a surrogate Jerusalem only sharpens its interest: this is northern pathos disciplined by local devotional intelligence, a body of suffering placed, quite literally, at the top of a pilgrim's climb.

### A Corpse for Pilgrims: The Dead Christ of Fabriano's Holy Sepulchre

  <img src="/Images/Maestro-dei-Beati-Becchetti/maestro-dei-beati-becchetti-02.jpg" alt="Life-size wooden statue of the dead Christ lying on his back, seen foreshortened from the feet, whose soles show carved nail holes. The forearms are crossed over the lower chest, the bearded head rests with closed eyes, and a deeply folded cloth wraps the hips. The painted surface is almost entirely worn away, exposing bare brown wood">
  <figcaption>Dead Christ, second half of the 14th century, polychrome wooden statue, length 188 cm, Fabriano, Pinacoteca civica "Bruno Molajoli"; from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre (Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti), Sant'Agostino.</figcaption>

If the Crucifix crowned the oratory's ascent, the Dead Christ was its destination. This recumbent figure, 188 centimetres long and carved in the second half of the fourteenth century, lay in the small chapel flanking the "Monte Calvario" that reproduced the Holy Sepulchre itself — the chamber that gave the whole building its dedication and its purpose. Pietro Becchetti had knelt at the tomb in Jerusalem; on his return, this statue was to allow his fellow townsmen to do the same. Of the six surviving figures it is therefore the one most literally bound to the pilgrimage that generated the ensemble, and the one whose function was least that of an image and most that of a body: a corpse to be approached, mourned, and kept vigil over in a chamber built to the measure of the original.

The museum photograph, taken from the foot of the display platform, restores something of the devotional viewpoint that the neutral gallery otherwise cancels. The visitor to the oratory's sepulchre chapel would have encountered the figure much like this, feet first, in a compressed space that forced the body into steep foreshortening — the soles of the feet looming large in the foreground, the head small and distant beyond the long horizon of the shrouded torso. It is the perspective that Trecento and Quattrocento painters would explore for the dead Christ, achieved here not through pictorial artifice but through the simple physical fact of a life-size corpse laid out at the height of a bier. The carver evidently anticipated this address: the soles are fully worked, and each is pierced by the nail wound, the holes still clearly legible in the photograph, so that the first thing the approaching devotee confronted was the proof of the Crucifixion itself — the wounds of the feet presented like the *ostentatio vulnerum* of the risen Christ, but in the register of death.

The pose is one of composed finality. The body lies supine and nearly straight, the legs extended with the knees very slightly flexed so that the feet fall a little apart, tilting outward in the natural slackness of death — an observed detail, not a formula. The forearms are crossed over the lower chest, each hand resting against the opposite side, in the gesture of the laid-out dead that the same workshop repeated on the effigy of the Dead Virgin in the facing chapel, and that Ottaviano Nelli's painter would echo, a generation later, on the lid of Blessed Pietro's funerary chest, where the friar lies with arms crossed on his breast. The head, bearded and framed by long tresses falling to the shoulders, rests level, the eyes closed, the mouth slack within the beard; there is no torsion, no arrested agony. As on the Crucifix, the master reserves his violence for the body's surface and lets the face subside into stillness — here carried to its logical conclusion, since this Christ is not dying but dead.

The drapery is handled with unexpected amplitude. A long cloth — reading either as the loincloth retained from the Cross or, more suggestively, as the burial shroud in which Joseph of Arimathea wrapped the body — is gathered about the hips and thighs in thick, rolling folds that break against the flanks in deep undercut pockets. The channels are carved broadly and deeply, calculated for the raking light of candles in an enclosed chapel rather than for even museum illumination; even in the photograph they gather shadow along the hips and give the inert body its one passage of formal turbulence. Whether these folds show the "linee spezzate", the broken angular line that the literature has read in the Pietà as the sign of the master's possible northern formation, is harder to say from this state of preservation: the rhythm here seems rounder and more continuous, though the deep drilling of the fold-pockets belongs to the same sculptural intelligence.

Preservation is indeed the crux of this figure. Unlike the Crucifix, whose restoration recovered an eloquent painted surface of bruises, blood and gilded hems, the Dead Christ survives in a severely abraded state: the polychromy is worn through across almost the entire figure, leaving the bare wood exposed in warm, streaked browns, with only scattered residues of preparation and colour — traces of lighter flesh tone and what appear to be remnants of gilding on the drapery — clinging in the hollows. The photograph shows this ruined surface honestly, and it should be read for what it is: the consequence of the ensemble's documented misfortunes, the earthquake damage, the long abandonment, and the statues' recovery from the closed oratory in the 1970s, when they were found heaped together before their rescue and restoration under the Soprintendenza at Urbino. Nothing about the present nudity of the wood belongs to the sculptor's intention; the figure must be imagined with a painted body bearing, like the Crucifix, the full inventory of the Passion.

Yet the loss has an unintended eloquence of its own. Stripped to the wood, the figure reveals the carver's construction with unusual clarity — the confident massing of the thighs and calves, the blocking of the crossed arms, the way the chest is built up in broad planes beneath the drapery's edge — and it acquires, for the modern viewer, the pathos of the relic: a body twice dead, once in iconography and once in matter. That second death is not the master's work, but it is now part of the object's history, and any presentation of the piece owes the reader an account of it.

Within the oratory's programme the Dead Christ formed a deliberate pair with the Dead Virgin lying in the chapel of the Valley of Jehoshaphat on the other side of the Calvary — Christ's tomb and Mary's tomb, the two sepulchres of the Jerusalem pilgrim's circuit, reproduced side by side in an Apennine cloister. The pairing is among the most remarkable features of the whole ensemble, for the recumbent Dead Virgin is a rarity in Italian Trecento sculpture, and it confirms that the programme was dictated not by narrative convention but by the topography the Becchetti had walked. Between the two effigies, the visitor did not follow the Passion as a story; he retraced a journey. This figure, the goal of that journey, is the plainest and in its ruined state the humblest of the six — and precisely for that reason the one in which the oratory's founding idea, the substitution of image for place, is most nakedly visible.

### Broken Lines: The Pietà of the Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti and the Question of the North

  <img src="/Images/Maestro-dei-Beati-Becchetti/maestro-dei-beati-becchetti-03.jpg" alt="Polychrome wooden Pietà: the Virgin, seated in a dark hooded mantle with a pale, still face, holds the rigid pale body of the dead Christ horizontally across her lap.   <figcaption>Pietà, second half of the 14th century, polychrome wooden statue, height 131.8 cm, Fabriano, Pinacoteca civica "Bruno Molajoli"; from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre (Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti), Sant'Agostino.</figcaption>

Among the six figures recovered from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre, the Pietà occupies a place apart. It stood on the lowest of the oratory's altars, that of the Madonna del Pianto, at the foot of the ascent that culminated in the "Monte Calvario" — the station of tears, where the devotee paused before the Virgin's grief prior to climbing toward the Cross. And it is the work around which the critical fortune of the anonymous master has crystallised: it was of this group, with its drapery of "broken lines", that the foundational scholarship first spoke of a "matrice nordica", a northern matrix betraying the maker's formation. The photograph, showing the group fully restored on its veined marble base, allows that judgement to be tested against the object itself, and the object largely sustains it.

The composition belongs unmistakably to the *Vesperbild*, the devotional image of the Virgin with the dead Christ on her lap that German workshops had developed in the early fourteenth century and that spread south across the Alps in the decades around the group's likely date. All the type's structural decisions are present. The Virgin sits frontally and hieratically, her knees forming the horizontal shelf on which the corpse is laid; the body of Christ is carried transversally, almost at a right angle to her axis, rather than sinking diagonally into her embrace as the later Italian tradition would prefer; and the corpse itself is rendered with the board-like rigidity of the northern examples — stretched nearly straight from the shoulders to the knees, stiff with the rigor of death, so that the Virgin does not so much cradle her son as present him, laid out across her lap as on a bier. The head alone escapes this rigidity, and violently: it falls sharply backward over her right knee, unsupported, the bearded face inverted toward the approaching viewer, the mouth open. That drop of the head, with the right arm dangling free below it and the hand open in the void, concentrates the group's entire pathos into its left-hand third. Nothing in the serene Crucifix or the composed Dead Christ prepares one for it; here, at the station of weeping, the master permitted the corpse the eloquence of abandonment.

The drapery confirms the transalpine grammar. The long cloth that wraps Christ's body from the waist to below the knees — reading as shroud as much as loincloth — is scored into tight, parallel, sharply crested folds that run diagonally across the thighs like the flutes of a shell, angular in section and almost metallic in rhythm: this is the "linea spezzata" of the literature, a fold-language of ridges and fractures foreign to the rounder, more fluent drapery of contemporary central Italian carving. The Virgin's mantle answers it in a graver register. Descending from the head in a deep cowl that shadows her face, it breaks over the knees and cascades between the legs in heavy V-shaped and stepped folds, pooling at the base in angular pockets — a system of fractures rather than flows, calculated, like the drapery of the Dead Christ, for the raking candlelight of the oratory. The mantle's present near-black colour is striking and demands caution: whether it preserves an original dark mourning polychromy or reflects darkened varnishes, later repainting, or the vicissitudes the ensemble is known to have suffered cannot be decided from the photograph, and I have found no published technical analysis that settles it. What can be said is that the chromatic effect as it stands — the pale, almost ivory corpse and the pale oval of the Virgin's face emerging from an expanse of darkness — is of extraordinary devotional efficacy, close in feeling to the stark contrasts of the German *Vesperbilder*, and that the light strands of the veil framing her face and the white cloth glimpsed at Christ's shoulder mediate between the two registers.

The two faces are studies in contrasted death and grief. Christ's inverted head, its reddish-brown hair and beard framing sunken features, shows the marks of the Passion in the painted wounds and abrasions that the restoration recovered across the torso — the lance wound legible in the left flank as the body is presented, red scourge traces on the chest and arms, blood at the ankles and the back-turned hand. The Virgin's face, by contrast, is a mask of contained sorrow in the master's characteristic mode: broad, pale, and still, the small mouth barely parted, the eyes wide and fixed rather than contorted — grief rendered as stunned vigilance rather than lament. It is the same restraint observed in the Crucifix's serenity, and it suggests again that the workshop distributed emotion deliberately across the ensemble, reserving open pathos for the St John and entrusting the Pietà's anguish to composition — the fallen head, the dangling arm — rather than to physiognomy. If the carver was indeed trained in the north, he tempered the expressionism of his models with a distinctly Italian economy of the face; if he was an Italian working from imported examples, he absorbed their structure more deeply than their grimace. The group, in other words, embodies precisely the unresolved alternative that the specialist literature has debated, and it does so in a single object.

At 131.8 centimetres the group is under life size, scaled to intimacy rather than to the monumental address of the Crucifix above it. Placed low, at the altar of tears, it met the devotee at the beginning of the circuit almost at eye level, the inverted face of Christ tilted directly toward the approaching worshipper — an orchestration of viewpoint entirely consistent with the sophistication observed throughout the oratory's staging. It is worth insisting, finally, on what this object represents historically: a fully-fledged *Vesperbild*, structurally faithful to its northern type, installed in a Marchigian Augustinian complex within a Jerusalem-surrogate programme conceived by two university-trained theologians, at a date when the type was still a novelty south of the Alps. Whatever the carver's passport, the Pietà of the Beati Becchetti is one of the earliest and most coherent witnesses to the arrival of this devotional invention in central Italy, and the keystone of any future argument about the master's origins.

### Grief Contained: The St John of the Oratorio dei Beati Becchetti

  <img src="/Images/Maestro-dei-Beati-Becchetti/maestro-dei-beati-becchetti-04.jpg" alt="Polychrome wooden statue of a young, beardless St John the Evangelist standing with hands tightly clasped at his chest. Of the six figures from the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre, the St John the Evangelist had the most exposed and most demanding station: he stood on the "Monte Calvario" itself, at the foot of the Crucifix, the sole carved witness beneath the dying Christ at the summit of the twelve steps. The arrangement was a Calvary reduced to its essentials — and an unusual one, for the Grieving Virgin, John's canonical counterpart under the Cross, was set apart on the second altar, the "Valle di Giosafat", so that the young apostle mourned alone. Whatever dictated that separation, its effect was to make this figure carry, by himself, the entire human response to the Crucifixion at the oratory's climactic station. The descriptive literature has always recognised as much, singling out the St John as the counterweight to the Crucifix's calm: where the Christ is a *patiens* of extraordinary serenity, the John is the ensemble's declared figure of "intensità patetica".

The photograph, showing the statue fully restored on its low octagonal plinth, both confirms and refines that characterisation. The pathos is real, but it is achieved with the same economy that governs the whole ensemble. The figure stands 171.6 centimetres high — near life size, subordinated by a calculated margin to the 210-centimetre Christ above him — in an almost columnar pose, feet together on the small base, the body a single closed silhouette without contrapposto or stride. All movement is gathered into two points: the hands and the head. The hands are pressed together tightly at the breast, in the knot of grief that the local historiography describes, held against the sternum as if to contain what cannot be spoken. The head inclines from the vertical toward its left shoulder, the face lifted — a tilt rather than a collapse, gentler in the object than the literature's phrase "face sunk onto the shoulder" might suggest — and the whole force of the figure concentrates in the face: the brows drawn up and knotted at their inner ends, the eyes rolled upward beneath heavy lids, glassy and brimming, fixed on the crucified body that originally hung above. The small mouth is closed. This is not lamentation but its suppression — the *dolore trattenuto*, the swallowed grief that the Byzantine and Italian traditions had long assigned to the beloved disciple, here rendered with a directness of physiognomy that stops just short of the contorted weepers of the northern Calvaries. Once again the workshop's expressive strategy is legible: emotion displaced from gesture into face, and in the face compressed to the eyes and brows alone.

The drapery is the figure's second voice, and its most sculpturally ambitious passage. John wears the long tunic proper to his iconography, painted in a warm grey-brown and closed at the throat by a broad gilded collar, with gilded hems at the cuffs and along the vertical front fall, where the cloth breaks over the feet in stacked, angular pockets. Over it sweeps a great mantle in deep green, gold-bordered along its every edge, which climbs over both shoulders, envelops the arms to the wrists, and crosses the body in a heavy diagonal cascade from the left hip to the right knee — a swag of thick, parallel, sharp-crested folds whose rhythm immediately recalls the fluted shroud of the Pietà and the fractured fall of the Virgin's mantle in that group. The "linea spezzata" is here again, though tempered: the folds of the diagonal swag are deep and crisp, angular in section, but their overall movement is more continuous, more fluent, than the Pietà's system of fractures, as if the carver had bent his northern fold-language toward the graver, longer line that a standing figure demands. One dangling tip of the mantle, falling free below the left hand with its gilded border twisting in space, is a small bravura passage of undercutting, carved for the flicker of candlelight on the Calvary. The colour scheme itself deserves note: the pairing of green mantle and gold border, over the neutral tunic, is a pictorial choice fully at home in Fabrianese painting of the later Trecento, and it strengthens the suspicion — unproven but increasingly consistent across the ensemble — that the polychromy of these statues came from a local painter's workshop conversant with the town's panel tradition.

The state of preservation is good, closer to the Crucifix and the Pietà than to the ruined Dead Christ. The polychromy of mantle and tunic survives extensively with its gilding; the flesh tones of face and hands are intact enough to preserve the painted modelling around the eyes on which the expression depends; losses and abrasions, visible along the mantle's ridges and at the collar, remain within the normal wear of the ensemble's hard history. The hair, painted a light reddish brown, falls in a smooth cap to the jaw, framing the youthful, beardless face that the iconography requires — the eternally young John, here aged only by grief.

Read against its European kin, the figure takes its place in the long tradition of the *Johannesfigur* under the Cross — the clasped-hand mourner familiar from transalpine Calvary groups as much as from Italian painted Crucifixions — while its restraint of gesture and its columnar stillness keep it distinct from the more agitated northern examples. It is, in this sense, the complement of the Pietà in the argument over the master's formation: where the Pietà displays the imported type at its most structurally faithful, the St John shows the same fold-language and the same expressive temperament absorbed into a figure of essentially Italian composure. And within the oratory's staging it played the most generous role of all: the devotee who had climbed the twelve steps to the Calvary found at its summit not only the dead Christ but this witness, eyes raised, hands knotted — a figure whose grief modelled the response the whole building was constructed to elicit. John under the Cross was always the surrogate of the faithful; here, in a chapel that was itself a surrogate Jerusalem, he was the pilgrim's own mirror.

</figure>

The Grieving Virgin occupied the strangest station in the Oratory of the Holy Sepulchre. She did not stand where every Calvary convention would place her, opposite St John at the foot of the Cross; she stood alone on the second altar, the one the sources call the "Valle di Giosafat", which commemorated the Virgin's meeting with her son on the road to Calvary. The separation was deliberate and, once recognised, illuminating. Detached from the Crucifixion group, the figure ceased to be a member of a narrative tableau and became a station in her own right — a solitary image of the mother's grief encountered by the devotee in mid-circuit, between the tears of the Pietà below and the consummation of the Calvary above. The oratory, which reproduced a pilgrim's topography rather than a Passion narrative, could afford this liberty: the Virgin here is less a participant in an event than a place, the *mater dolorosa* as a destination on the road to Jerusalem's holy sites, standing in the valley that bore her own tomb.

The figure's formal language answers this function with an austerity unmatched among the six statues. At 178.5 centimetres she is taller than the St John, and her silhouette is even more strictly columnar: a single closed volume rising from the small polygonal base to the hooded head, without contrapposto, without gesture toward an absent Christ, without any of the torsion that animates her counterpart. The great mantle — now reading as near-black, with the same caution required for the Pietà's, since darkened varnish or later intervention cannot be excluded without technical examination, though its consistency across the two Marian figures may equally support an original dark mourning habit — descends from the crown of the head in an unbroken fall, closes over both shoulders, and opens down the front to reveal the dark violet dress gathered at the throat. From beneath the hood, the white veil descends in two long, flat bands that frame the face and fall the length of the chest, their squared ends marked with delicate painted or incised ornament — a border of fine patterning that constitutes, with the dress's muted violet, almost the only chromatic incident in the entire figure. The carver has concentrated his fold-language in the dress rather than the mantle: down the front runs a dense sheaf of thin, parallel, sharp-crested pleats, tight as the flutes of a column, breaking at the ground in small angular pockets — the familiar crisp, ridged carving of the workshop, here disciplined into pure verticality. The mantle by contrast falls in long, sparing folds, broad planes barely inflected, so that the whole figure reads as a dark monolith traversed by one bright vertical of veil and one fluted core of drapery.

All expression, once again, is gathered into the extremities — and here the master's economy reaches its furthest point. The hands are the figure's single gesture, and it is a gesture of no gesture: both arms hang at the sides, and the bare, pale hands emerge from the mantle's sleeves open and empty, palms turned back, the long fingers extended downward and slightly spread. It is the ancient attitude of helpless grief — hands that can do nothing, displayed as such — and its effect, on a figure otherwise sealed within its dark cocoon, is quietly devastating. The northern Calvary tradition knew the wrung hands, the clasped hands, the hands pressed to the breast; this workshop gave those to the St John and reserved for the Virgin the emptier, older eloquence of arms simply dropped. The face completes the statement. Framed by the hood, the veil bands, and a fringe of hair rendered in fine parallel striations across the brow, it is a broad, pale oval of extreme stillness: the eyes heavy-lidded and lowered, not raised to any cross; the small mouth closed; the features neither contorted nor slack, but fixed in the stunned composure that this carver gives all his mourners. What appear to be traces of painted tears descend the cheeks below the inner corners of the eyes — an observation the photograph suggests but that only autopsy of the surface can confirm — and if original they would be the figure's sole concession to demonstrative grief, threads of water on a face of stone.

The state of preservation is substantially good and consistent with the Pietà: the dark polychromy of the mantle survives as a continuous glossy skin with scattered losses, the flesh tones of face and hands are intact with their painted modelling, the veil retains its ornamented borders, and glimpses of a deep green underside at the mantle's lower edges — matching the green noted elsewhere in the ensemble — hint at a richer original dialogue between the garments than the present darkness discloses. Here as throughout, the unresolved question of the polychromy's authorship and its relation to Fabrianese painting presses; the refinement of the veil's painted borders, in particular, is the work of a practised painter's hand.

Set against her companions, the Grieving Virgin defines one pole of the workshop's expressive system. The ensemble runs a gamut from the Pietà's compositional violence through the St John's knotted pathos to this figure's near-total suppression of incident, and the gradation is too consistent to be accidental: each station was assigned its temperature, and the "Valle di Giosafat" was assigned the coldest and deepest. Within the wider landscape of Trecento sculpture the figure is also the ensemble's closest approach to the standing *Addolorata* of the German Calvary groups — the mantled, columnar Virgin familiar from transalpine Crucifixion ensembles — while its planar severity and the stillness of its face keep it, like the St John, on the Italian side of the expressive frontier. And it holds, finally, a quiet structural correspondence that the oratory's programme surely intended: the woman standing in the Valley of Jehoshaphat is the same woman whose entombed effigy lay in the chapel of that valley's tomb beside the Calvary. The devotee who met her here, upright and grieving on the road, would find her again a few steps further on, laid out in death — the two Marian images bracketing the circuit as the two sepulchres bracketed the pilgrim's Jerusalem.

With the Dead Virgin the oratory's programme reached its most audacious invention. The figure, 187 centimetres long — almost exactly the length of the Dead Christ, a correspondence that can hardly be accidental — lay in the second of the small chapels flanking the "Monte Calvario", the one that reproduced the Tomb of the Virgin in the Valley of Jehoshaphat. Nothing obliged the Becchetti to include it. A Calvary with a Sepulchre would have satisfied every devotional convention of the age; the addition of Mary's tomb, with a full-length recumbent effigy of the dead Virgin inside it, is explicable only by the logic of the pilgrimage itself. Pietro Becchetti had visited not one holy tomb but two, for the church of the Virgin's sepulchre at the foot of the Mount of Olives was a fixed station of the Jerusalem circuit; and his oratory reproduced what he had walked, not what iconographic habit prescribed. The result is one of the rarities of Italian Trecento sculpture: a monumental carved Dormition effigy, isolated from the apostolic assembly that frames the Virgin's death in painting, presented simply as a body in a tomb — the *Koimesis* stripped to its funerary core. Any future census of the subject in fourteenth-century Italian wood sculpture will have to begin, or very nearly begin, with this object.

The photograph, taken like that of the Dead Christ from the foot of the platform, restores the intended address. The devotee entering the low chapel met the effigy feet first and steeply foreshortened: the drapery breaking over the feet looms in the foreground, the long fluted expanse of the dress recedes like a furrowed field, and the small pale face lies distant at the summit of the perspective, tilted back in death. The two tomb chambers thus mirrored one another across the Calvary — mother and son, each laid out at full length, each approached from the feet — and the carver bound the pair still more tightly through construction: the same near-equal lengths, the same level recumbency, the same crossing of the arms over the body. Here, as on the Christ, the forearms are crossed over the breast, one hand laid flat over the other arm with the long fingers extended — the gesture of the composed dead that this workshop repeated across the ensemble and that Nelli's painter would echo on the lid of Blessed Pietro's chest a generation later. The head, framed by hair falling in thick waves to the shoulders — worn now to the bare wood, but clearly carved in the parted, striated manner of the standing Virgin's fringe — rests level, the eyes closed beneath heavy lids, the nose sharp, the small mouth set: in the shrunken planes of the cheeks the carver has registered, with the same observational honesty he brought to the Dead Christ's slack mouth, the specific stillness of an aged face in death. It is the face of the Grieving Virgin of the "Valle di Giosafat" — the same broad oval, the same composure — carried across the threshold she was, at that station, still approaching.

The drapery is the figure's great formal statement, and it is handled with a rigour that surpasses even the standing Virgin's. The entire body, from the collar to the ankles, is sheathed in a dress carved into an unbroken system of thin, parallel, sharp-crested pleats running the full length of the figure — the fluted core of the standing Addolorata's dress here extended to the whole recumbent form, so that the body reads almost as a single ribbed vessel, a *gisant* rendered in pure fold. Anatomy is deliberately suppressed beneath this system: no swell of the body interrupts the pleats' run, and only at the lower end does the carving break, where the cloth collapses over the upraised feet in the deep, angular, tent-like pockets — the "linea spezzata" at its most emphatic — that dominate the photograph's foreground. The contrast is surely calculated: the long calm of the parallel flutes, then the sudden fracture at the feet, exactly at the point of the devotee's approach. One may note how profoundly this conception differs from the Dead Christ's, where the body's anatomy — chest, thighs, calves — remains fully legible beneath and beside the drapery: the son is presented as a corpse, wounds and flesh; the mother as a form already sealed, vested, almost liturgical. Whether that distinction reflects theological tact — the Virgin's body, awaiting assumption, withdrawn from the viewer's scrutiny as the crucified body could not be — or simply the decorum owed her sex, it belongs to the same distributive intelligence that assigned each figure of the ensemble its own register.

The state of preservation is the poorest of the six, fully comparable to the Dead Christ's and evidently the product of the same history: the two recumbent effigies, lying in their open chapels through the oratory's long dereliction, suffered what the standing figures on their altars partly escaped. The polychromy is abraded across virtually the entire surface, leaving the wood exposed in streaked browns; yet the losses are here unusually informative, for within the hollows of the pleats survive extensive residues of the original surface — passages of colour and what appear, even in the photograph, to be traces of patterned decoration or gilding ghosting through the channels of the dress. These remnants suggest that the Virgin's funeral garment was originally ornamented, perhaps richly so — which would sharpen the liturgical, vested character of the conception noted above — but the observation must remain provisional: only technical examination can establish what the pleats preserve, and the figure is, of all six, the one that would most repay it.

Seen whole, the Dead Virgin completes the ensemble's symmetries with an exactness that speaks for a unified design. Each Marian figure answers a Christological one — the Grieving Virgin to the Crucifix across the oratory's ascent, the Dead Virgin to the Dead Christ across its summit — and the pleated fold-language binds the two Virgins as the laid-out gesture binds the two effigies. The devotee who completed the circuit had thus performed, in an Apennine cloister, the essential double pilgrimage of Jerusalem: Calvary and the two tombs. That the tomb of the Virgin was, in doctrine, an empty one — vacated by the Assumption as Christ's by the Resurrection — only deepens the image's resonance: both effigies presented bodies that the faith proclaimed to be no longer there, death exhibited precisely as the thing overcome. It is a conception worthy of the two theologians who commissioned it, and this battered, magnificent figure — the least known of the six and the most singular — is its boldest surviving witness.

## Style, technique and the question of origins

The central interpretive question about the master is where his style comes from. Two answers circulate, and it is worth being clear that they are not strictly exclusive.

The first answer, going back at least to Donnini's work of the early 1990s, reads the angular, fractured drapery of the Pietà as the sign of a northern formation, a "matrice nordica". This fits the wider picture of the *crucifixus dolorosus* and the *Vesperbild* as transalpine inventions that reached Italy through the movement of works and of craftsmen, including German carvers documented in several Italian centres in the fourteenth century. The discrepancy in the title of Casciaro's essay suggests that this line of enquiry was pursued in the specialist literature. In one version of the title the problem is explicitly that of "crocifissi tedeschi medioevali" between the Marches and Umbria. The comparative literature has since treated the Becchetti Crucifix as a reference point for central Italian examples of the type. A study of a fourteenth-century wooden crucifix in Basilicata, for instance, compares it with the Becchetti Crucifix as an exemplar of Tuscan-Umbrian-Marchigian crucifixes.

The second answer, found in encyclopaedic summaries that are less carefully sourced, suggests the statues were probably carved and painted in neighbouring Umbria by artists of Giottesque culture. This statement is unsourced in the text where I found it and should carry little weight on its own. It nevertheless points to something real. The Fabrianese carvers of the fourteenth century worked in close contact with Umbrian workshops and with the painters of their own town. The polychromy of Fabrianese wooden statues, as the 2021 exhibition argued, often shows the influence of the local painters, above all Allegretto Nuzi.

These two answers can be reconciled if two things are kept apart: the typological and formal models the master drew on, and the workshop setting in which he worked. A sculptor could adopt transalpine types (the dolorous Crucifix, the *Vesperbild*, the broken-fold drapery of the late fourteenth century) whether he was himself a German émigré, an Italian trained in contact with German carvers, or a local craftsman working from imported models. His figures could then be painted in a manner close to local panel painting. The evidence now available does not allow a choice among these possibilities. No document names the carver. No technical study of the statues' construction has been published to my knowledge. Such a study, of the joinery, the way the head is attached and the use of canvas or cord, would be the kind of evidence that has helped separate German from Italian workmanship in other cases of dolorous crucifixes. In its absence, the most defensible statement is that the master's figures combine types and formal features of transalpine origin with a restraint of facial expression. That restraint is noticeable in the Crucifix, and the contrast between it and the pathos of St John and the Pietà seems deliberate. It gave each station of the oratory its own emotional register.

Technically, the Crucifix's surface shows the importance of polychromy to the master's effects. The blue-and-gold loincloth, and the bruises and hairs painted on the limbs, recovered in restoration, show that the realism the museum text stresses belongs as much to the painted surface as to the carving. This is typical of Trecento wood sculpture, where carver and painter often worked in partnership. It is one reason why sculpture of this period in Fabriano cannot be understood without the town's painters. If the polychromy of the Becchetti statues was done by a local painter, as seems likely though unproven, it would link the ensemble to Fabrianese painting around 1400 in a concrete way, and future technical and stylistic study should give this priority.

## Chronology

The master's chronology is anchored at one point only, the foundation date of the oratory, and even that anchor is softer than it looks. The date of 1393 is the consistent date for Pietro Becchetti's pilgrimage and for the building of the oratory on his return. The Pinacoteca states that the oratory was "eretto nel 1393". The Sacri Monti documentation more cautiously says the complex was put together "dopo il 1393", after 1393. Local historiography introduces the date with "si tramanda". The variant date of 1383 in some Augustinian popular hagiography has already been discussed and is best set aside.

On this basis the museum dates the statues to the second half of the fourteenth century, and local historiography places the carving "sul finire del XIV secolo" (towards the end of the fourteenth century). Donnini's 2008 study, however, calls the oratory's scheme an iconography of the Passion "del primo '400", of the early fifteenth century. This need not contradict the museum dating. It may reflect a view that the oratory was built and furnished over several years after the pilgrimage, perhaps into the early 1400s, and that its programme should be read as a whole, sculpture and painting together. The *Lignum vitae* fresco, generally dated to the early fifteenth century, belongs to that programme. The later tradition that Pietro returned to the Holy Land with Giovanni some years after 1393 would, if true, add a further possible moment of commission or addition.

The prudent chronological conclusion is therefore a range, not a date. The sculptures were probably carved in the 1390s or around 1400, after the pilgrimage of 1393, and in any case before the translation of the blessed in 1565 gave the oratory its present name. Any narrower dating, such as placing the statues in the 1380s or tying them to a particular year, would go beyond the evidence.

## The painted companions: the *Lignum vitae*, Nelli's chest, and the chapels of Sant'Agostino

Much of the confusion around the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti comes from the fact that the oratory, and the church it belongs to, contain important paintings by other hands. These must be described precisely so that they are not attributed to the sculptor.

The first is the great fresco on the altar wall of the oratory, a *Lignum vitae*. It shows a large stylised tree with a medallion on each branch containing the figure of a saint. It is painted in monochrome, described in local accounts as executed "a punta di pennello" (with the tip of the brush). It is generally attributed to Lorenzo Salimbeni of San Severino Marche and dated to the early fifteenth century. The national heritage organisation's page calls it a work of the painter's maturity. The Pinacoteca's documentation of Sant'Agostino places it at the beginning of the Quattrocento. Lorenzo's career is usually taken to have ended around 1418–19, which provides a rough end date. The attribution is stylistic, not documentary, and is sometimes given in the press as "attribuito". One point of iconography supports an early-fifteenth-century date within the oratory's programme. The *Lignum vitae*, which the museum describes as standing for the Cross itself, sat behind or above the Monte Calvario with its carved Crucifix. The painted tree of the Cross and the carved Crucified thus formed a single statement, and the fresco may have been added to an existing sculptural installation some years after the statues were carved.

The second is the funerary chest of Blessed Pietro Becchetti, now also in the Pinacoteca. On its front, in painted panels, are four miracles attributed to the blessed, two performed in life and two after death. In the first he visits and blesses a leper. In the second he heals a sick girl brought by her parents. In the last two his soul descends from heaven to heal sick people lying in their beds, attended by relatives and religious. Two crosses are painted on the sides, and on the lid lies the figure of the blessed, arms crossed on his breast, which the museum attributes to a hand different from that of the Gubbio painter. The museum attributes the front to Ottaviano Nelli and dates the chest to the 1420s. The national catalogue of cultural heritage gives a date range of 1400–1424 and speaks more cautiously of the "school" of Ottaviano Nelli for the front, distinguishing it from the lid. The biographical dictionary entry on Nelli accepts the chest as his. It links it to his repeated work for Augustinian patrons, from the Pietralunga polyptych onward, and suggests a particular connection with some leading member of the order.

A simple inference narrows the range. Since two of the miracles are posthumous and Pietro was still alive in February 1421, the front cannot have been painted before 1421. The museum's dating to the 1420s is therefore preferable to the broader catalogue range. The chest matters to the present subject in a negative way. It is the only securely Becchetti-related painting of the period, and it is by a documented painter, Nelli, not by an anonymous Fabrianese. If an identification with the young Ottaviano Nelli has any footing at all, it is this: Nelli did paint for the cult of the Becchetti. But he did so around 1421 or later, as a mature master, and his work is an image of Pietro's miracles, not of the two blessed together, and it is a painted chest, not a fresco. No source identifies Nelli with the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti, who is a sculptor.

The third body of painting is the fresco decoration of the Gothic chapels of Sant'Agostino itself. The chapels flanking the high altar were frescoed in the first decades of the fourteenth century and came back to light in 1933, when the partitions that had hidden them were demolished. Descriptions of them are neither complete nor consistent. One tradition assigns the Stories of [St Augustine](/endnotes.html#fn-augustine-of-hippo) to the right-hand chapel and scenes from the life of Christ and of the Magdalen to the left. Another account, from an Augustinian source, lists in the left-hand chapel Stories of St Augustine, a Triumph of St Ursula, a Stoning of St Stephen and an Incredulity of St Thomas, and in the right-hand chapel, formerly dedicated to Augustine, a Crucifixion, figures of saints, and episodes of Augustine entering a city and freeing prisoners, the death of Augustinians and Augustine's triumph, all badly damaged. The general literature agrees that different hands were involved but that the cycles share a stylistic denominator from the Riminese-Assisi tradition. The only documentary anchor is the sale of a house and land in 1311, to meet the expenses of building and decorating the church, then Santa Maria Nova. The Pinacoteca presents the most important painter of the early Fabrianese Trecento, the anonymous Maestro di Sant'Emiliano, as active in the first half of the century.

These frescoes are therefore the better part of a century earlier than the Becchetti oratory. Nothing connects them with its sculptor. The question of the master's share in these chapels, beside the Maestro di Sant'Emiliano, does not arise, because the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti did not paint. The chapels are relevant only as context: they show that the Augustinian community of Fabriano had been a significant patron of painting for two generations before the Becchetti built their oratory in the same convent.

## Fabriano around the Master: Nuzi, Ghissi, the carvers, and Gentile

The Becchetti Calvary was made in a town with a notably rich figurative culture, and some sense of that culture is needed to place the master without inventing links that do not exist.

The dominant figure of Fabrianese painting in the mid-fourteenth century was Allegretto Nuzi, a native of Fabriano trained in Tuscany, who worked there continuously from 1347 until his death in 1373. He produced everything from small altarpieces for private devotion to large polyptychs and fresco cycles. His successor in the town's painting was [Francescuccio di Cecco Ghissi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Francescuccio-Ghissi.html), active in the second half of the century. Ghissi's *Madonna of Humility* from San Domenico, now in the third room of the Pinacoteca, is among the best-known Fabrianese images of that devotional type. It is presumably this image, or others like it, that lies behind the notion that the Becchetti master worked in the Madonna of Humility type. The type belongs to Nuzi's and Ghissi's circle, not to the sculptor.

Confusions with Nuzi's workshop, with Ghissi or with the Maestro di Staffolo would all be confusions among painters. None can apply to a sculptor. Any real relationship between the Becchetti master and Nuzi's tradition would lie in the polychromy of his statues. The 2021 exhibition argued in general terms that the colouring of Fabrianese wooden sculpture was deeply influenced by Allegretto and his school. Whether this holds for the Becchetti group specifically has not, as far as I know, been shown. The statues were carved some twenty years after Nuzi's death, so any link would pass through his followers.

The sculptor's closer peers were the other anonymous carvers of Fabriano. The 1993–94 exhibition grouped them into a local tradition that included the Maestro dei Magi di Fabriano, whose wooden St Nicholas of Bari survives in San Domenico, the Maestro della Madonna di Campodonico, and a Fabrianese wood workshop of the later fourteenth century. The Pinacoteca's collection of Trecento wood sculpture is described in regional guides as one of its strengths, with the Maestro dei Magi and the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti as its two leading names. Seen in this company, the Becchetti master stands out for his transalpine elements. The Maestro dei Magi and the other carvers appear more closely tied to the local painting of Nuzi's generation. The Becchetti Calvary, with its dolorous Crucifix, its *Vesperbild* and its broken folds, seems to bring into Fabriano a sculptural language whose roots lay outside the town, and very probably outside Italy.

The young Gentile da Fabriano also grew up in this town. Born around 1370, he was in his twenties when the Becchetti built their oratory. As for the master's relationship to Gentile's formative context, the honest answer is that the Becchetti oratory was part of that context in a general way, as one of the most ambitious devotional commissions in Fabriano during Gentile's youth. No source connects Gentile with the sculptor or the oratory, and suggesting a specific influence either way would be speculation. It is legitimate to observe that the Fabriano of Gentile's youth was a place where transalpine sculptural types such as the dolorous Crucifix and the *Vesperbild* could be seen alongside the Tuscan-derived painting of Nuzi's followers. That is a remark about context, not a claim of influence. It is also worth noting that the painters later active in the oratory, Lorenzo Salimbeni and Ottaviano Nelli, both belong to the late-Gothic generation that the biographical literature on Nelli describes as close to Salimbeni and to Gentile. The oratory thus became, within a generation of its foundation, a small meeting place of the Marchigian-Umbrian late Gothic.

## Errors, confusions and myths: a critical inventory

It is now possible to address directly the errors that surround the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti, including those that turned him into a painter.

The most basic error is to treat the master as a painter. I have found no scholarly source that does so. The museum, the specialist literature on Marchigian wood sculpture, the comparative literature on dolorous crucifixes and the regional encyclopaedic literature all describe him as a carver of polychrome wooden statues. The confusion probably arises because his name appears in the lists of "artists" of a picture gallery alongside painters, and because the oratory that gives him his name contains paintings by others.

The second error is to derive the name from a fresco depicting the two blessed. No such fresco by this master is known. The name comes from the oratory, which carries the name of the blessed because of the 1565 translation and the later cult. The two principal painted works connected with the Becchetti are Salimbeni's *Lignum vitae*, which does not depict them, and Nelli's chest, which depicts Pietro's miracles. Neither is by the master.

The third error concerns dating. A dating to around the 1380s–1390s for a supposed Becchetti fresco combines the foundation date of the oratory with an imagined painting. The statues are best placed after 1393, in the 1390s or around 1400. The *Lignum vitae* is early fifteenth century. The chest belongs to the 1420s. Popular texts place the Becchetti themselves in the fifteenth century, date the *Lignum vitae* to the fourteenth, or date the Pietà to the second half of the fifteenth. All of these are demonstrable slips.

The fourth group of errors involves confusions with painters: Nuzi's workshop, Ghissi, the Maestro di Staffolo, the Maestro di Sant'Emiliano. Because the master was a sculptor, none of these can arise. The only legitimate question about painters is how the statues' polychromy relates to Fabrianese painting, and that question remains open.

The fifth concerns proposed identifications. I have found no proposal in the literature identifying the Maestro dei Beati Becchetti with a documented artist, whether the young Ottaviano Nelli, a Fabrianese painter or a named sculptor. Nelli's documented connection with the Becchetti cult is real, but it concerns a different object, a different date and a different medium. Casciaro's essay is called an "ipotesi", and its content could not be verified here. It is therefore possible that it contains a proposal of this kind, but nothing in the sources that cite it suggests so, and none should be assumed.

The sixth concerns supposed works outside Fabriano in the Vatican Pinacoteca or in American and European collections, and supposed reconstructions by Federico Zeri. I have found no such attributions. The only work outside Fabriano linked to the master's name in the sources I could reach is the Gualdo Tadino Crucifix, a wooden sculpture in an Umbrian civic museum, and that attribution should still be treated as provisional.

## Open problems and the Master's place in late-Trecento Marchigian art

The Maestro dei Beati Becchetti is therefore a real but narrowly defined artistic personality: the anonymous carver, or perhaps the workshop, of the Calvary and Holy Sepulchre figures of the Becchetti oratory. Many problems remain open, and it is more useful to state them precisely than to cover them with conjecture.

The first is the content and status of the specialist hypothesis. Casciaro's essay needs to be read closely against the objects. The key questions are whether it argues for a German or German-trained carver, whether it extends the oeuvre to Gualdo Tadino or elsewhere, and whether it proposes a date or an identity. These are the questions on which the master's profile most depends.

The second is technical. A study of the six statues' construction, wood species, joinery and polychrome layers would allow the "northern matrix" to be tested with the methods that have proved decisive in other cases of dolorous crucifixes. It would also show whether the polychromy is contemporary with the carving and whether it can be linked to a particular painter or workshop of Fabriano.

The third is contextual. A full reconstruction of the oratory's original arrangement, now made possible by the survey and digital rendering carried out by the University of Florence architecture department and by De Marchi's call for a virtual reconstruction, would let the statues be read again as a sequence of stations. The relation between the carved Crucifix and the painted *Lignum vitae* would then become visible, and the question of whether the sculptures and the fresco belong to one campaign or to successive ones could be addressed.

The fourth is chronological: whether the statues belong to the immediate aftermath of the 1393 pilgrimage or to a longer campaign extending into the early Quattrocento, as the title of Donnini's 2008 study implies. Here the only reliable progress would come from new documents, perhaps from the Augustinian archives, which preserve letters to and from both Becchetti, or from the Fabrianese notarial records.

Within these limits, the master's place in late-fourteenth-century Marchigian art can still be stated with some confidence. He is not a figure of the Nuzi tradition in painting, and not a precursor of Gentile. He is the maker of the most ambitious surviving ensemble of devotional wood sculpture in late-Trecento Fabriano, an ensemble created for a precocious Italian replica of the holy places of Jerusalem. His figures bring into an Apennine town, and join to a learned Augustinian programme, the types of the dolorous Crucifix and the *Vesperbild*. They alternate calm and pathos from station to station, and within a generation they were set beside paintings by two of the leading late-Gothic masters of the region, Lorenzo Salimbeni and Ottaviano Nelli. That is a more interesting historical position than the one a painter of this name would have held. The Becchetti master is important not as a minor painter caught between Nuzi and the International Gothic. He matters as a sculptor through whose work the devotional imagery of northern Europe entered, very physically, the sacred topography of a Marchigian convent. Returning his figures to that topography, even virtually, is the most useful thing future scholarship can do for him.

## Conclusion

The Maestro dei Beati Becchetti is a sculptor, not a painter, and the correction is not a matter of detail. It removes, at a stroke, a whole set of questions that the label of painter seemed to leave open: the master's share in the chapels of Sant'Agostino, his relation to the Maestro di Sant'Emiliano, his Madonnas of Humility, his panels in foreign collections, his identity with the young Ottaviano Nelli. None of these has any footing in the literature. What remains after that subtraction is smaller than the painter's profile promised, but it is solid. There is a named ensemble, a known provenance, a documented pair of patrons and a devotional building whose arrangement can still be reconstructed. An anonymous master is only as secure as the objects that define him, and in this case the objects are few, coherent and well preserved enough to be read closely.

The name itself turns out to be a layered historical product. At its base are two documented Augustinian friars, cousins rather than brothers, whose careers can be followed through the studia of Rimini, Padua, Perugia and Oxford until their last letters of 1420 and 1421. Above them sits the tradition, transmitted rather than documented, of Pietro's pilgrimage and the foundation of the oratory in 1393. Above that again is the translation of their bodies into the oratory in 1565, which gave the building its devotional name, and the confirmation of their cult in 1835. The art-historical label came last of all, borrowed from the name of the building after the statues were recovered and restored in the 1970s. The sculptor is therefore named after a building that took its name from two men, and it took that name almost two centuries after the statues were carved. Keeping these layers apart is the first condition for saying anything reliable about him.

The six statues, read one by one, show a design more deliberate than their scattered museum presentation suggests. The Crucifix and the Dead Christ, the Grieving Virgin and the Dead Virgin answer one another in pairs. The two recumbent effigies are almost exactly the same length, 188 and 187 centimetres, and were approached in the same way, feet first, in two facing chapels. Emotion is distributed across the ensemble rather than repeated in each figure. The Crucifix keeps a composed face above a body marked with every wound of the Passion. St John carries open, knotted grief at the foot of the Cross. The Pietà concentrates its pathos in composition, in the fallen head and dangling arm, while the Virgin's face stays still. The Grieving Virgin, with her empty hands, marks the coldest point of the scale. This gradation is too consistent to be accidental, and it is the strongest argument that the ensemble was conceived as a single programme, whatever the number of hands that carved it.

That programme only makes sense in the oratory for which it was made. The statues were not devotional images placed on altars but stations in a small, stepped topography of Jerusalem: the Calvary raised on its twelve steps, the Sepulchre and the Virgin's tomb on either side, the altars of the meeting on the road and of the tears at the Deposition below. The devotee did not follow the Passion as a story but retraced a pilgrim's route. This is why the Dead Virgin, a rare subject for monumental wood sculpture in Trecento Italy, is there at all: the programme followed the places the Becchetti had visited, not the conventions of the Calvary group. It is also why the modern museum room, however necessary for the statues' survival, gives only a partial view of them. Heights, distances, levels and candlelight were part of the work. The master's figures were made to be looked up at, walked towards and come upon, and much of their meaning was lost when they were heaped together in the closed oratory and later set in a line against a gallery wall.

The question of the master's origins remains open, and this essay has not tried to close it. The types he used, the dolorous Crucifix and the *Vesperbild*, are transalpine inventions, and the angular, broken drapery of the Pietà is the feature on which Giampiero Donnini's "matrice nordica" was first founded. Yet the faces throughout the ensemble show a restraint that sets them apart from the more contorted northern models. The Pietà and the St John, taken together, sum up the alternative: one is structurally faithful to its imported type, the other absorbs the same fold-language into a figure of Italian composure. Whether the carver was a German émigré, an Italian trained beside German carvers, or a local craftsman working from imported examples cannot be decided on present evidence. Raffaele Casciaro's hypothesis, which placed the master within the question of German-type crucifixes between the Marches and Umbria, awaits a close reading against the objects, and the attribution of the Gualdo Tadino Crucifix must stay provisional until its arguments can be checked.

Where the master does meet the painters of Fabriano, he meets them through colour rather than through attribution. The blue and gold of the Crucifix's loincloth, the green and gold of St John's mantle and the ornamented borders of the Virgin's veil belong to the pictorial language of a town whose panel painters had made gold and colour their speciality. If, as seems likely but is unproven, the statues were painted by a local workshop, that is the real link between the Becchetti Calvary and the tradition of Allegretto Nuzi's followers. Within a generation the oratory also held work by Lorenzo Salimbeni and Ottaviano Nelli, so that it became a small meeting place of the Marchigian and Umbrian late Gothic. The young Gentile da Fabriano grew up in the same town while it was being built. These are facts of context, not claims of influence, and they should be stated no more strongly than that.

The case also has a lesson of method. A thin art-historical profile is easily distorted. A caption that moves the Pietà a century later, a press account that pushes the *Lignum vitae* back into the Trecento, a museum text that compresses three centuries of cult into a single moment: each slip is small, but together they show how a sculptor could turn, in popular accounts, into a painter with a fictitious fresco and an invented oeuvre. The remedy is to keep the kinds of evidence apart: documents, objects, attributions and interpretations, each weighed on its own terms. Here that meant accepting a short historiography of three contributions, from Donnini's exhibition and studies, through Casciaro's hypothesis, to Andrea De Marchi's call to recover the oratory as a sacred theatre, and saying plainly where each stops.

What can be said, then, is modest but clear. The Maestro dei Beati Becchetti was the carver, or the workshop, of the most ambitious surviving ensemble of devotional wood sculpture in late-Trecento Fabriano, made in the 1390s or around 1400 for a precocious Italian replica of the holy places of Jerusalem. Through his figures the devotional imagery of northern Europe entered the life of an Apennine convent, joined to a programme devised by two learned Augustinians. The work that remains is concrete: a technical study of the six statues and their polychromy, a close reading of the specialist hypothesis, a search of the Augustinian and notarial archives, and a reconstruction, even a virtual one, of the oratory's stations. Until then the master is best known through the building that gave him his name. His figures were made to be met along a pilgrim's route, and their meaning will only be fully recovered when they can be seen in that setting again.

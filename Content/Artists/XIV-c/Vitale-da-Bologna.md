## Introduction: A Painter with a Horse in His Name

Vitale di Aimo degli Equi, known since the seventeenth century as *Vitale da Bologna*, is the painter around whom the history of Bolognese painting in the fourteenth century is written. He is documented in Bologna from 1330, worked in Udine in 1348–1349 and at Pomposa around 1351, signed a contract for a polyptych in 1353, made his will in December 1359, and was dead by 31 July 1361. Between those dates lies a body of work that includes one signed and dated panel, a second signed panel, one panel signed with a monogram, a contracted polyptych that survives in its original frame, and several fresco campaigns whose extent and authorship are still argued over.

The family name is the first clue to his signature. *Equi* means "horses" in Latin, and Vitale exploited the pun. On the panel of *Saint George and the Dragon* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna, the letters of his name are worked into a monogram branded on the horse's hindquarters. The same device recurs in a decorative band in the church of Santa Maria dei Servi. A painter who signs on the flank of a horse is announcing both his name and his family, and the joke tells us something about the confidence with which he worked.

The modern Vitale is largely a twentieth-century construction, whose stages are traced at the end. The archival picture was assembled in the second half of the twentieth century and is summarised in the entry of the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (2020), which is the main documentary source for the biographical record.

What follows separates three levels of knowledge. The first is what the documents and inscriptions establish. The second is what scholarship infers from style, provenance and chronology, with a broad consensus. The third is what remains hypothesis or open debate. Several statements that circulate widely belong to none of these levels and are corrected in the section on disputed and rejected attributions.

## Documents and Chronology

Vitale is one of the best-documented Italian painters of his generation outside Florence and Siena. About twenty records span three decades. None of them gives his date of birth, and none describes a surviving work in a way that would let us identify it with certainty, except the contract for the San Salvatore polyptych.

The documents call him *Vitalinus Aimi de Equis* (1331) and *Magister Vitallis pintor fillius Aymi de Equis* (1343): Vitale, son of Aimo degli Equi. The diminutives *Vitalinus* and *Vindalinus* appear in the early records.

No document records his birth. His name appears for the first time in 1331 in the *venticinquine*, the lists of men liable for armed service, for the parish of Santa Maria Maggiore in the quarter of Porta Stiera; it is absent from the list of 1330. Enrolment came at about twenty, so a birth around 1310 is the usual inference.

He had a son, Francesco, named as his universal heir; a daughter, Giovanna, already dead in 1367; and a sister, Franceschina.

The earliest record of his activity is a payment of 7 March 1330, when *Vindalino pictori* received 40 lire<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lira-bolognese" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> for the chapel of Filippo Odofredi in San Francesco, Bologna. From 1331 to 1333 he is enrolled in the *venticinquine* of Santa Maria Maggiore, in the quarter of Porta Stiera, which establishes his residence in the city and underlies the inferred birth date of about 1310. On 2 and 3 August 1334 he witnessed two acts at the convent of San Francesco, the first sign of a lasting link with the Franciscans<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, and two payments of 1340 continue that connection: 18 lire on 11 November "pro toto complemento capelle sancti Laurentii", for a chapel now completed, and 6 lire on 9 December "pro picturis foresterie", for paintings in the guest quarters of San Francesco.

On 14 August 1343 he signed a contract with Guido, bishop of Ferrara, for four wooden statues for Ferrara Cathedral, to be painted white and gold "ad modum figurarum marmorearum" for 50 lire, rising to a maximum of 60 — proof that he also worked as a painter of sculpture. In 1345 he signed and dated the *Madonna dei Denti*, his only securely dated panel, and on 5 May 1347 he is named, as *Vindalinum pictorem*, in a Bologna lawsuit for homicide, the last Bolognese record before the Friulian journey. On 28 June 1348 he witnessed a will in San Francesco at Udine, and payments of 1348 and of 4 October 1349 for the chapel of the confraternity of San Nicolò dei Fabbri in Udine Cathedral — where "Vitalis pictor" is named in 1349 — document the Udine commission.

The date 1351 on the apse of Pomposa belongs to this sequence only if the inscription is authentic. Back in Bologna, on 6 July 1353 he contracted with Raniero Ghisilieri, prior of San Salvatore, for a polyptych for the altar of Saint Thomas Becket, to be delivered by Christmas against an advance of 100 lire; on 3 October of the same year Balduccio di Francesco bound himself to work with him for a year, the first documented assistant. On 2 July 1357 Vitale witnessed a contract between Balduccio and Bishop Giovanni da Gallarate, and on 11 November of that year he sold a house in his own name and in that of his son Francesco.

On 4 June 1359 he witnessed a deed at the chapter of the Servites together with other painters, the documentary link with Santa Maria dei Servi. His will was registered on 8 December 1359, with Francesco as universal heir — the last record of him alive — and on 31 July 1361 Francesco is named "q(uondam) magistri Vitalis pictoris": Vitale was dead.

This record is taken from the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry (2020), with the archival publications it cites, and from the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry (2000).

Taken together, the record allows a plain summary. Vitale was active as an independent master by 1330, when he was about twenty. He worked for the Franciscans of Bologna throughout the 1330s and in 1340. In 1343 he was trusted with a contract outside the city and with polychrome sculpture. He left Bologna at some point after May 1347 and was in Udine by June 1348. He was back in Bologna by 1353 at the latest, now with an assistant under contract. He died between 8 December 1359 and 31 July 1361.

## The Fixed Points: Signed, Dated and Contracted Works

Five works anchor everything else. Three carry Vitale's name, one carries a date on a wall he decorated, and one survives together with its contract. Four of them are treated in this section; the third of the signed works, the *Madonna dei Battuti* now in the Pinacoteca Vaticana, is catalogued below with the Ferrarese works, where its provenance places it. Every other attribution is built outward from these.

The first is the *Madonna dei Denti* in the Museo Davia Bargellini in Bologna (inv. 129), the only work that once carried both Vitale's name and a year; it came from the oratory beside the church of Sant'Apollonia at Mezzaratta, outside the city walls.

The inscription runs along the foot of the panel, below the throne and between the two donors. Only part of it can be read today: the letters "…ALIS FECIT" and "OPUS", followed by a damaged run of Roman numerals, are visible in the photograph. The full reading recorded in the literature, *Vitalis fecit hoc opus MCCCXLV*, depends on earlier transcriptions made when more of the paint survived. With that reading the panel gives the one fixed year in Vitale's career between the Franciscan payments of 1340 and the Udine documents of 1348–1349.

The Virgin sits on a white throne of Gothic design whose side posts end in pinnacles and crocketed finials, a piece of painted architecture rather than furniture. Red cushions show at either side. Behind her hangs a gold cloth of honour tooled with a lattice of lozenges, which fills the whole round-headed field and turns the gold ground into a textile. Her mantle, now much darkened and abraded, was originally a deep blue covered with gold motifs; scattered fragments of the gold decoration, among them small creatures, still catch the light across her lap. A transparent white veil falls from her head over her shoulders. The figure is large in relation to the panel, and the mantle spreads in a broad cascade from knees to footstool, giving the image the mass of a cult statue.

Against this monumental Virgin, the Child is entirely in movement. He stands naked on her lap, his weight on one leg, and reaches up to touch her chin with his right hand while his left arm swings out towards the viewer. The Virgin supports him with both hands, one at his chest, the other at his legs, and inclines her head towards him with a downward, inward gaze. The gesture of the Child touching his mother's chin belongs to the repertory of tender devotional types that spread in Italian painting in the fourteenth century; Vitale gives it a physical immediacy, with the Child's plump body, curly golden hair and open stance, that is characteristic of his work.

Two small figures kneel at the foot of the throne, at the bottom corners of the panel. The one at the left wears red and joins its hands in prayer; the one at the right wears a dark, hooded garment and raises its hands towards the Virgin. Their scale, far smaller than the Virgin's, follows the convention by which patrons appear in the presence of the holy figures. They are not named, and no document identifies them. Since the panel came from a confraternity's oratory at Mezzaratta, they may represent members of the company or private donors who paid for the image; neither reading can be confirmed.

"Madonna dei Denti", "Madonna of the Teeth", is a devotional name, not a title given by the painter. The panel stood in an oratory beside the church of Sant'Apollonia, and Saint Apollonia, whose teeth were broken in her martyrdom, was invoked against toothache. The name most probably passed from the cult of the saint to the image venerated in her precinct.

Malvasia, writing in 1678, describes the panel in its oratory as part of a polyptych. The tall, round-headed format and the self-contained composition, with donors included, are compatible with a central panel whose lateral compartments are lost. Nothing is known of the missing parts, and the surviving panel shows no trace of them.

The panel has suffered. The paint of the Virgin's mantle is heavily worn, with the dark preparation and craquelure showing through; the gold of the upper field is cracked and damaged at the top of the arch; and the inscription has partly flaked away. The flesh of the Virgin and Child and the faces of the donors are comparatively well preserved, and they carry the qualities on which Vitale's reputation rests: soft modelling, warm flesh tones, and faces of individual character.

The date 1345 also makes the panel the point of comparison for undated works such as the *Saint George*. Compared with the expressive vehemence of the *Saint George*, the *Madonna dei Denti* shows the other side of Vitale: the devotional image, in which movement is confined to the Child and the dignity of the cult figure is preserved. Both sides were present in his work by the mid-1340s.

The second fixed point is the *Saint George and the Dragon* in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna (inv. 6394), which carries the painter's name in the form of a monogram.

The panel carries no inscription. Vitale signed it instead with a monogram painted on the horse's hindquarters, where a real horse would carry its owner's brand. The letters are interlaced, one set inside another; the Pinacoteca reads them as the name VITALIS. The placing is a pun on the painter's family name, *de Equis*, "of the horses": the brand marks the horse as Vitale's, and the picture with it. At Santa Maria dei Servi, where the device recurs, the *Dizionario Biografico* records a second, earlier Saint George signed in the same way.

The scene is built on one long diagonal. The lance, held high in the saint's right hand, runs from the top edge of the panel down to the lower right corner, where its point enters the dragon's open jaws. Every other element is set in motion around it. The horse rears and twists, its forelegs over the dragon, its neck bent back so sharply that its head points up and away from the action, mouth open and teeth showing. George leans forward over the horse's neck, his head lowered along the line of the lance, his hair and red cloak blown back. The figures fill the field almost to its edges; there is no landscape beyond a strip of red rock, and no space behind them except the flat, dark ground.

George wears chain mail rendered in gold, a red tunic and a red cloak lined in yellow that flies out behind him in angular folds. A pale blue cap or helmet band covers his brow, and his gold halo is tilted with the forward thrust of his head. His face is calm and concentrated, in contrast to the violence of the horse. The gilded details of mail, spurs and halo were applied in relief and still catch the light, as the Pinacoteca notes, giving the armour a physical presence on the surface of the panel.

The horse is the most striking part of the picture: pale grey, almost white, with sinuous legs and a long, curving neck, its body turned so that the viewer sees the monogrammed flank. Its gesture, head flung back and mouth open, is one of terror as much as of attack. Beneath it the dragon is a tangle of claws, wings and scales in golden brown, its head raised at the right edge to receive the lance. The two animals are painted with a fluency of line that recalls drawing more than the modelling of Florentine painting.

At the upper right, half outside the picture, the crowned princess turns to look back over her shoulder at the combat. Only her head and shoulder are shown. Her placing at the edge, cut by the frame, makes her a witness rather than a participant, and directs the viewer's attention back into the struggle.

The ground is a dark blue-green, not gold, which is unusual for a panel of this date. A geometric decorative band runs along the left and right edges but not along the top or bottom, which suggests that the panel has been cut down at the top and bottom. The Pinacoteca also raises the possibility that it was originally set into a frescoed wall. Its first destination is not known. The paint surface shows wear, particularly in the dark ground and in the red rocks, and the princess's figure has been reduced by the cutting of the panel.

No document dates the panel. The Pinacoteca places it in the early years of Vitale's activity, and most scholars put it in the later 1330s or early 1340s, before or close to the *Madonna dei Denti* of 1345; later dates, around 1350, have also been proposed. The caption here gives c. 1340 as the midpoint of the prevailing range.

The *Saint George* is the work most often used to define Vitale's art. It shows the qualities that distinguish him from the Florentine followers of [Giotto](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html): movement across the surface rather than construction in depth, line and colour used for expressive force, and an attention to precious detail in the armour and halo. It also shows his wit, in the signature branded on the horse.

The third anchor is the apse of Pomposa, with its date of 1351. The Benedictine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-benedictine-order" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> abbey of Santa Maria at Pomposa stands on what was once an island in the Po delta, near Codigoro in the province of Ferrara. Its church was consecrated in 1026 and its bell tower begun in 1063; by the fourteenth century the abbey had lost much of its eleventh-century power, and the surrounding marshes were making the site unhealthy. It was in this phase of slow decline that Abbot Andrea of Fano, who governed the house from 1336 to 1361, had the whole interior of the church repainted. Vitale's apse is the head of that campaign.

The semidome shows Christ blessing, enthroned in a mandorla and surrounded by angels. On one side the Virgin leads a company of virgins and martyrs; on the other Saint Michael weighs souls<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-psychostasis" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> with a balance. Abbot Andrea kneels at the Virgin's feet, without halo or episcopal insignia, presenting himself as the patron of the work. Below, in a middle band, stand Saint John the Baptist and Saint Martin among the Evangelists and the Doctors of the Church. The lowest register narrates the *Stories of Saint Eustace*, the Roman general converted by the vision of a stag bearing the crucifix between its antlers, separated from his wife and sons, reunited with them, and martyred.

The programme combines three themes. The semidome is a Last Judgement in compressed form: Christ in glory, the Virgin as intercessor, Michael with the scales. The middle band sets the authority of Scripture and of the Fathers beneath it. The Eustace legend at eye level offers the monks and the faithful a narrative of conversion, loss and restoration. Why Eustace should occupy the apse of Pomposa has not been fully explained; the choice is usually connected with a relic or an altar dedication in the abbey, but no document has been published that settles it.

The year 1351 is read in an inscription in the apse. The inscription is damaged, and the *Dizionario Biografico* accepts the date only on condition that it is authentic. If it is, the apse follows Vitale's return from Udine and precedes the San Salvatore contract by two years, a placement that most scholars find compatible with the style of the semidome.

The hand of Vitale is most evident in the semidome. The middle band and the Eustace stories are more uneven, and most scholars recognise assistants working to his designs. For that reason the Eustace scenes are credited here to Vitale da Bologna and workshop, unless a specific study assigns a scene to the master. [Benati](/scholars.html#benati-daniele)'s essay of 2017 on the fourteenth-century painters and programmes at Pomposa is the current reference for these distinctions.

Abbot Andrea's campaign did not stop at the apse. The nave walls carry three superimposed cycles that read from the apse towards the entrance: Old Testament scenes in the upper register, from Noah to Joseph and Jacob, and the Vision of Ezekiel; New Testament scenes in the middle register, from the Annunciation to the Ascension; and, in the spandrels of the arcades, an extended cycle of the Apocalypse. The entrance wall carries a *Last Judgement*. These paintings are generally dated later than the apse, in the 1350s, and attributed to Bolognese painters of Vitale's circle rather than to the master himself. The Apocalypse is the most unusual of the three: a full illustration of the Book of Revelation in the nave of an Italian church of this date is rare, and the choice of subject is credited to the abbot rather than the painters.

Taken together, the apse and nave make Pomposa the most complete programme connected with Vitale that can still be seen in its original building. Mezzaratta has been detached and reassembled; Udine survives in fragments and in a copy; the Franciscan chapels in Bologna are lost; the Servite<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-order-servants-of-mary" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> frescoes are a single cycle. At Pomposa the visitor stands where Abbot Andrea stood.

The whole semidome is best read from its centre outwards, and the detail that follows isolates that centre: the enthroned Christ within his mandorla, the angels nearest to him, and the two inscriptions that state the meaning of the image — the words on the pages of his open book and the verse that runs beneath the point of the mandorla. Seen at this scale, the figure shows what the distance of the apse conceals: the starry ground of heaven inside the mandorla, the colour of the vestments, the modelling of the face, and the letters of the texts, which are the starting point for any reading of the programme and of its date.

Christ sits at the centre of the semidome, enthroned within a tall pointed mandorla. The mandorla is built of concentric bands of red, white and blue-grey, which give it the look of a rainbow or a gateway of light rather than of a solid frame. Inside, the ground is a dark blue scattered with gold stars: Christ is shown in heaven, above the firmament. He is seated frontally on a throne covered with a gold cloth, his bare feet resting at the foot of the mandorla. He wears a white tunic with red panels at the chest and sleeves and a red mantle lined in green, drawn across his knees in broad, heavy folds. His right hand is raised in blessing; his left holds an open book turned towards the viewer.

The pages of the book carry a text in large Gothic letters, divided across the two pages: *PA-CEM / MEA[M]* on the left and *DO VO-BIS* on the right. The reading *Pacem meam do vobis*, "my peace I give unto you", is the words of Christ to the apostles at the Last Supper (John 14:27). At Pomposa, in a monastic church, it addresses the community gathered in the choir below.

Along the base of the semidome, directly under the point of the mandorla, runs a hexameter: *ASTRA POLI XPS NITET CUM CARNE BEATA*, "Christ shines in the stars of heaven with his blessed flesh". The verse states the theology of the image: the Christ enthroned among the stars is the risen and ascended Christ, present in heaven in the body he took on earth. Its source has not been identified. Below, on the frieze that divides the semidome from the wall of the apse, a second inscription survives in fragments: only isolated letters and words, among them A D M and TEMPORE, can be made out in the photograph. This lower inscription is the one that carries the date 1351 and the abbot's name in the readings published by scholars; its damaged state is the reason the date is accepted with reserve.

Around the mandorla the semidome is filled with the court of heaven. In the upper corners, angels crowd together in dense clusters, their haloed heads overlapping in rows. At the foot of the mandorla, on each side, smaller angels kneel or bow towards Christ; those at the right wear tunics marked with a dark crossed band. On the left side of the semidome, at Christ's right hand, a procession of tall female figures advances towards him, richly dressed in robes lined with ermine, the foremost holding an unfurled scroll. This is the company of virgins and martyrs led by the Virgin, as the abbey's own description identifies it. On the right side, at Christ's left, stands the armoured and winged Saint Michael, the weigher of souls; beyond him much of the plaster is lost, and the figures that once completed the right side of the composition survive only as faint traces.

A frieze of roundels containing heads, perhaps prophets or saints, runs along the rim of the semidome, interrupted at the top by a scroll. The arch that frames the apse is painted with ornamental bands, and above it a large roundel, now damaged, marks the crown of the arch. Ornamental frames of this kind were usually left to assistants, and there is no reason to think otherwise here.

The kneeling figure of Abbot Andrea, recorded in the abbey's own description, is not clearly distinguishable among the kneeling angels at the lower left, and its exact position has not been verified on site.

The semidome is the part of the Pomposa decoration most generally accepted as Vitale's own work. The broad, calm figure of Christ, the heavy fall of the mantle and the dense, animated groups of angels belong to the monumental phase of his art after the return from Udine. The difference from the narrative vehemence of the Saint George is a difference of subject: here Vitale painted a hieratic image of Christ in majesty for the most important wall of an abbey church, and he subordinated movement to symmetry and order. The faces of Christ and the angels, with their soft modelling and individual features, are closest to his panel paintings.

The fresco has suffered losses, particularly on the right side of the semidome, where large areas of plaster are missing, and along the frieze below. Colours have faded in places, and the blue ground has darkened. The inscriptions are partly illegible. A modern lighting fixture now hangs in front of the left side of the fresco, as the detail shows.

The fourth fixed point, the polyptych of Saint Thomas of Canterbury at San Salvatore, is the only work by Vitale that survives together with its contract, the document of 6 July 1353 recorded above, published in 1909. It remains the one case in the painter's career in which a surviving object, its patron, its price, its deadline and its destination can all be read together.

The altarpiece is still in the church of the Santissimo Salvatore in Bologna, in the chapel of the right transept, in its original carved and gilded frame. The catalogue record of the Italian state gives an overall size of about 105 × 225 cm and a central panel of about 100 × 50 cm (units are not stated in the record). It came from Santa Maria di Reno, the original seat of the canons, and was moved to San Salvatore around 1580; it was restored in 1976–1979.

The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* describes its carpentry as Venetian in inspiration, a sign of how far Bologna looked towards the Adriatic for the format of its altarpieces. Within that carpentry the programme is laid out with complete clarity: the tall central field, raised under its own gable, carries the Coronation of the Virgin; two standing saints flank it under their arches, the altar's titular Thomas Becket at the left, with the kneeling commissioner, and John the Baptist at the right; and at the outer edges four small arched scenes in two tiers — the Nativity and the Martyrdom of Saint Catherine among the narratives, Saints Benedict and Scholastica and the writing doctors Augustine<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustine-of-hippo" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> and Ambrose<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ambrose-of-milan" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> among the images of the religious life. The seven painted fields are catalogued below.

#### The Coronation of the Virgin

The central field — at about 100 × 50 cm the one panel whose dimensions the state's record singles out — carries the Coronation of the Virgin, raised above its companions under a gable of its own. For an altarpiece ordered by the canons of Santa Maria di Reno, the subject is a dedication made visible: the titular Virgin of their mother church brought to the end of her story, crowned in heaven at the centre of their altar.

The scene is set against a cloth of honour that fills the whole field, gold patterned with dark quatrefoils — textile doing the work of space, as on the Mezzaratta wall and in the panels of the 1340s. There is no architecture beyond the bench throne; the event is placed nowhere but in heaven, and heaven is a woven thing. Christ sits at the right in a rose mantle, turned in profile towards his mother, both hands raised to set the crown; the Virgin bows from the left, hands crossed on her breast, in the gesture of the Annunciate carried into glory. Her pale mantle pours over the seat and down across the floor in the long, unbroken folds of Vitale's late manner.

In the cusp above, a row of half-length angels stands against a band of red, a compressed court of heaven answering the full choirs of Pomposa; the painted arch beneath the carved one doubles the framing, picture and carpentry working as one Gothic object.

The gentleness of the two protagonists — inclined heads, lowered eyes, the crown descending without drama — belongs to the same years as the added episodes at Mezzaratta and the late Madonnas: the vehemence of the *Saint George* has become ceremony. If the apse of Pomposa shows Vitale's public monumentality, the Coronation shows its domestication for the altar table. The subject, too, had travelled down the Adriatic with the carpentry. The Coronation of the Virgin was the favoured crowning theme of Venetian altarpieces in just these decades, and its appearance at the centre of a Bolognese polyptych framed in Venetian-inspired woodwork shows format and iconography arriving together — Vitale working, in his last years, inside a lagoon convention and making it speak his own soft language.

#### Saint Thomas Becket with the Commissioner

The inner left panel holds the altarpiece's titular: Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury, to whose altar the contract of 6 July 1353 expressly destined the work. He is the reason the polyptych exists in the form it does, and the panel beside the Coronation is his place of honour.

Becket's cult had crossed Europe within a generation of the martyrdom of 1170, and Bologna had a claim of its own: tradition made the young Thomas a student of law in the city, and a university that lived by the law could honour in him both the martyr and, after a fashion, an alumnus. An altar of Saint Thomas in a Bolognese canonry therefore needs no special pleading — but it carried a local pride all the same. The contract's deadline reads like liturgy. Delivery was set for Christmas, and the feast of Saint Thomas falls on 29 December, within the octave: the finished altarpiece, installed on time, would have stood ready precisely for the first feast of its own titular — the notary's calendar and the church's calendar pulling in the same direction.

The saint stands frontal under his arch: a pale mitre, a rose-red cope over white, the right hand raised in blessing, the left holding the cross-headed staff of his rank. Nothing marks the martyrdom; he is shown not murdered but vested, the archbishop as the canons wished to be patronised by him.

At his feet, at the familiar reduced scale, kneels a figure in the white habit of the canons under a dark cloak, hands joined, turned towards the central Coronation: the commissioner — presumably Raniero Ghisilieri, the prior who signed the contract, or the community he stood for. The white of the habit is the brightest small accent on the left wing, the donor made visible precisely by his plainness. The pairing repeats the circuit of patronage that runs through Vitale's confraternity images, from the Battuti standard to the Viterbo panel: the great intercessor standing, the client kneeling, the glance and the prayer binding the two. Here the circuit is notarised — the kneeling figure answers to a name in a surviving document, a rarity in the painter's œuvre.

#### Saint John the Baptist

The inner right panel answers Becket with the Baptist: the first and the most recent of the martyred witnesses, flanking the Coronation as a matched pair. The literature records a donor at his feet as at Becket's, so that the two wings mirrored one another in devotion as in design.

The pairing is eloquent. John denounced a king and died for it; Thomas resisted a king and died for it — the parallel was a commonplace of Becket's cult from the first generation, and an altarpiece that sets the two side by side around the court of heaven states it without a word. The Baptist is given his full ascetic type: gaunt and dark, wild-haired, the long beard falling over the camel-hair garment, a rose mantle slung so as to leave shoulder and arm free. The raised hand across the body is the preacher's gesture, witness carried in the wrist.

The gesture also has an address. Raised across the body towards the centre of the altarpiece, it points where the Baptist always points — to Christ — here enthroned in the Coronation beside him: the precursor turned into the polyptych's own index finger, directing the eye from the wing to the heart of the programme. Against the courtly Becket opposite — vested, mitred, blessing — the Baptist is deliberately the other kind of holiness: the desert against the cathedral, hair shirt against cope. Vitale had painted the desert's saint before, in the Anthony storiette; here the type is compressed into a single standing figure.

The figure's silhouette, all angles where the Virgin's is all curves, shows how much of the painter's expressive repertory survives inside the late gentleness; the wiry line of the *Saint George* years is kept in reserve for the bodies that need it.

#### The Nativity

The four corner fields turn the polyptych from an assembly of saints into a small narrative machine. The first of them, at the upper left, is the Nativity.

The scene is built, as at Mezzaratta, from the shed outwards: an open trussed roof drawn in pale lines against the dark interior, the carpentered minimum of roof, posts and shadow. Beneath it the haloed group of Virgin and Child occupies the left of the little field, with attendant figures gathered at the right.

It is the *Presepe* of the entrance wall compressed to the span of a hand: the painter who spread the subject across five metres of plaster here folds it under an arch a few tens of centimetres wide, keeping the shed and the huddle and letting the rest go. Even at this scale the panel observes the series' rule of textures — thatch, truss, halo, gold — and the dark interior of the shed gives the corner its depth, a pocket of night set into the gilded machine.

The position is calculated. The upper left is where the reading eye begins, and the programme begins there too, with the Incarnation: the narrative corners open the story whose heavenly conclusion, the Coronation, fills the centre at full scale.

#### Saints Benedict and Scholastica

The upper right corner holds the monastic pair recorded among the polyptych's subjects: Benedict, the father of western monasticism, and his sister Scholastica — the founder of the rule and its contemplative wing, set side by side as the altarpiece's image of the life lived under vows.

The legend's defining scene for the two is a meeting: the yearly colloquy outside the monastery walls, at the last of which Scholastica's prayer called down the storm that kept her brother talking of heaven until morning. A composition of standing figures about a tower — the monastery between and behind them — belongs to that tradition of the siblings met outside the enclosure. In the little field a tall figure in a long red robe stands at the left, a pale tower rises at the centre, and companions gather at the right: the conversation staged, as the legend requires, in the open, with the house that separates the two present as architecture between them.

Benedict and Scholastica on an altarpiece of Augustinian<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-augustinian-order" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a> canons are not out of place: the pair stood for regular life itself, exemplars for any community living under a rule, whichever rule it was — and their corner stands above the panel of the rule-writing doctors on the same side of the altarpiece. That division is the programme's quiet armature. Infancy and martyrdom — the Nativity, the Catherine — run on one axis; the religious life — the monastic siblings, the writing bishops — on the other.

It is the most intimate of the seven fields: no throne, no execution, two religious talking outside a wall. On an altarpiece built from a notarised contract, the corner holds the one scene whose subject is simply conversation about God.

#### The Martyrdom of Saint Catherine

The lower left corner carries the Martyrdom of Saint Catherine of Alexandria, the virgin martyr recorded among the polyptych's subjects: the crowned Maxentius enthroned at the left, the saint standing before him at the right — the confrontation on which her whole passion turns. In the legend the audience is the martyrdom's beginning and its meaning: the emperor commands, the virgin argues and refuses, and wheel and sword follow from her refusal. Vitale chose the moment of speech over the moment of blood — a debater's martyrdom.

The emperor is wrapped in dark blue-green, crowned, the sceptre carried against his shoulder, the commanding hand raised towards the saint; she stands slight and upright before him, the rose of her dress the warmest note in the field, answering force with presence. For Bologna the choice of moment had a special fitness. Catherine, who out-argued the philosophers of Alexandria, was the learned virgin par excellence, a patroness of scholars and of the disputation itself; in a city that lived by the law and the quaestio, her martyrdom-by-argument was almost a civic subject.

Catherine runs through Vitale's work — holding her wheel among the virgins of Mezzaratta, standing beside the Poldi Pezzoli Madonna, raised again in the heavenly court of the *Guarigione* — and her presence on the canons' altar extends a devotion the painter had served for a decade. With Becket standing full-length beside her, the altarpiece holds two martyrdoms, one told as a story, one worn as a vestment.

#### Saints Augustine and Ambrose

The lower right corner closes the programme with the two doctors: Augustine and Ambrose, mitred and enthroned face to face, each at his desk, writing. For the canons the choice is a signature. They were canons regular of Saint Augustine; the author of their rule sits at the foot of their altarpiece, in the act — writing — by which he governs them still. Ambrose beside him is his baptiser and his peer among the Latin doctors: the bishop who made the bishop.

The two are shown as working scholars, not icons: seats turned inward, desks between them, books open, the furniture of the studio rendered with the same respect Vitale gives to beds, cribs and biers elsewhere. In a university city the image of two men writing needed no gloss. The symmetry of the little field — mitre answering mitre, desk answering desk — gives the altarpiece's lower corner a kind of institutional calm, the counterweight to the narrative corners above and beside it.

Within the programme the panel completes a set: Becket gives the canons their altar's martyr, the Coronation their titular, the monastic siblings their vocation's exemplars, and the doctors their rule — the polyptych as the canons' self-portrait in saints. It is also the painter's last word on a theme that runs through his city's art: Bologna's painting grew up beside the book trade of the university, and Vitale, whose formation lay close to the illuminators, ends the canons' altarpiece with the image of writing itself — two authors at work, framed in gold.

To these four should be added the documented but fragmentary campaign at Udine. The payments of 1348 and 1349 concern the chapel of the confraternity of San Nicolò dei Fabbri, the smiths, in Udine Cathedral. Of this decoration a whole wall of *Stories of Saint Nicholas* survives, catalogued below, with the *Funeral of Saint Nicholas* in its lunette the best-preserved scene. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* considers the Fabbri chapel more workshop-driven than the larger campaign in the high chapel of the cathedral, consecrated in 1335 by Patriarch Bertrand of Saint-Genès, whose fragments are given to Vitale on stylistic grounds. The lost parts of the high-chapel cycle are known in part through a copy in the cathedral of Spilimbergo, which Benati dates to about 1358. The Udine stay is the only well-documented period Vitale spent outside Emilia.

The wall of the Fabbri chapel is the one documented decoration by Vitale's workshop that can still be seen outside Emilia, and its payments give it a precision that even Mezzaratta lacks. The chapel now forms part of the Museo del Duomo of Udine. What the wall shows is the shop at full stretch, on the master's designs, a year or two after his departure from Bologna.

The wall is organised like a page: a lunette and two registers, separated by broad ornamental bands of rosettes and lozenges. In the apex Christ blesses within a roundel, an open book in his hand, angels flying at either side; directly beneath, a small praying figure rises between two angels over the funeral — the soul of Nicholas carried to heaven above his own obsequies, the same double register of body and soul that governs the age's tomb sculpture.

The lunette holds the *Funeral of Saint Nicholas*, the best-preserved scene and the heart of the programme. The saint lies in pale vestments on a bed beside a bier draped in yellow, his head sunk in the pillow; around him the city gathers — clergy in white, a fur-trimmed dignitary in red, a nun in black, laymen pressing in from both sides, one in a tall hat — before a long wall of pink architecture. Painted perhaps five years after the funeral of the Anthony storiette, the scene turns the same subject from romance to civic ceremony: fewer prodigies, more mourners, the confraternity's own patron buried as a bishop of their city might be.

The middle register strings the saint's miracles between domed aedicules whose skyline has an Adriatic, almost Byzantine flavour. At the left the famous rescue at sea: a boat crowded with struggling sailors, sail bellying, riding a dark swell — seafaring observed by a painter of landlocked Bologna with the same appetite he gave to camels. At the centre a woman kneels in prayer before a shrine; at the right a bishop-saint in a red cope stands over a naked youth rising from a tub, steadied by a man in oriental dress — the register of resurrections and restitutions, the boy restored that made Nicholas the protector of children. The lower register has fared worst: a scene of dignitaries at a table survives at the left — a banquet of the legend — amid wide losses, and a modern doorway cuts the corner.

For the catalogue the wall matters twice. It anchors the Udine journey in something visible — the only place where the documented payments of 1348–1349 and surviving painting meet — and it is the seedbed of the Vitalesque tradition in Friuli, discussed below among the painter's followers.

## Style and the Wider Catalogue

No document names Vitale's master, and no work can be dated before 1330 with confidence. His formation has therefore to be read from the works themselves, and the readings differ. What follows distinguishes the components that most scholars recognise from the claims that rest on a single proposal.

Bologna in the 1320s and 1330s was a city of the university and of the book. Its painters worked alongside a large production of illuminated legal and liturgical manuscripts, and the boundary between panel painter and illuminator was porous. Two local currents are usually named as the background against which Vitale emerged: the expressive, sharply characterised manner associated with the anonymous painter called the Pseudo-Jacopino, and the related work grouped under the name of the Master of 1333. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* reads Vitale's early *Crucifixion* in Philadelphia as his answer to this expressive current.

Among the earliest frescoes now given to Vitale is the *Last Supper with Saints Catherine, Louis of Toulouse<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-louis-toulouse" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a>, Anthony of Padua and the Archangel Raphael* from the convent of San Francesco, a long, fragmentary frieze (211 × 937 cm) rediscovered in 1935, since detached and now in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna (inv. 10032). The *Dizionario Biografico* connects it with the payment of December 1340 "pro picturis foresterie", but the connection is an inference from subject and place, not a stated identity, and the Pinacoteca's own record gives its provenance as the convent's Refettorio Nuovo and dates it before 1340 — possibly still within the 1330s, on account of its echoes of the older Giotto-Riminese manner. Benati sees in it the paratactic arrangement of figures typical of the painters of Rimini, who had dominated Emilia and Romagna in the previous generation.

Of the fresco itself barely a third survives. What remains is the upper zone of a frieze more than nine metres long: a row of heads and upper bodies along the back of a table whose surface, like the whole lower register, has fallen away, leaving bare plaster, scattered traces of colour and a faint red circular mark towards the base. The irregular islands of surviving paint are set into a neutral modern ground, and a strip of the painted red border remains at the foot. The ensemble is mounted on the large front wall of Room 6 of the Pinacoteca, among the detached fourteenth-century frescoes.

Even in ruin the scene can be read. The apostles were ranged in a single line behind one long table stretching across the full width of the field — no perspectival room, but a frieze of figures presented frontally to the room below. At the centre the blond, curly-haired young apostle slumped forward over the table is John, asleep, the detail that fixes the subject at a glance. To his right a figure in a red mantle, its head lost, points along the table with an extended forefinger, a gesture that belongs to the announcement of the betrayal; at the left a dark-haired apostle in red reaches across the table with a long, outstretched arm. At the far right a balding figure in a pale rose mantle, seen from behind, bows towards a standing bearded figure in a dark robe. The losses are too great for the remaining heads to be named with confidence, and of the four saints recorded in the title — Catherine, Louis of Toulouse, Anthony of Padua and the Archangel Raphael — who accompanied the supper, not all can now be identified; the haloed figure in the yellow mantle at the extreme left presumably belongs to them.

What survives carries the qualities of the young Vitale: heads of marked individual character, softly modelled, each turned at its own angle, and drapery in strong reds and blue-greens handled with the density of his panel painting. A Last Supper is the traditional subject for a refectory wall, which accords with the Pinacoteca's provenance from the Refettorio Nuovo. The frieze is the measure of Vitale's starting point: Riminese in its arrangement, Bolognese in its expressive force.

Giotto's presence in the background is less direct than older accounts suggest. Giotto's signed polyptych for Santa Maria degli Angeli in Bologna dates from c. 1330–1335, and the Riminese painters themselves were heirs of his work at Rimini. Vitale certainly knew Giottesque solutions of space and volume. But the description of Vitale as a "Giottesque" painter, common in general histories, misses what distinguishes him: he took little interest in the stable, measured space that Florentine followers of Giotto were developing in the same years.

The qualities on which modern readings agree are visible in *Saint George* and in the Mezzaratta *Nativity*. Figures are set in motion across the picture plane rather than placed in depth. Bodies arch and twist; Benati compares their curved postures to French Gothic ivories. Drapery is thrown into sweeping, independent curves. The paint itself is dense and handled with visible impasto, and flesh is modelled softly.

Vitale was also attentive to precious surfaces. The early fresco in San Francesco uses inserts of glass and metal foil, and the *Saint George* retains raised gilded details on armour, shoes and halo. The commission of 1343, to paint four wooden statues white and gold "in the manner of marble figures", shows that he was trusted with the polychromy of sculpture, a field in which the imitation of materials was the point.

[Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto) made Vitale the protagonist of a Bolognese painting that he described as "brutally sincere and impulsive", and he saw in him a precursor of the courtly culture of late Gothic painting. Both halves of the formula have shaped later criticism. The first emphasises the expressive, almost violent energy of works like *Saint George*; the second the elegance of the late Madonnas, where, in Benati's account, Vitale returns to a Gothic ideal of beauty. The two are not contradictory. They describe a painter whose range ran from narrative vehemence to refined devotional images, and whose late work shifted towards the second.

Much, however, remains open. The sequence of Vitale's works before 1345 is not fixed by any date. *Saint George*, the Philadelphia *Crucifixion* and the first Mezzaratta campaign are ordered by style, and different scholars order them differently. The claim that Vitale trained as an illuminator is plausible in the Bolognese context but has no documentary or securely attributed basis.

Around the fixed points stands a catalogue of attributed works. Most are accepted by the majority of specialists; a few remain contested. They are grouped here by place and by degree of certainty.

The frescoes of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta are the largest ensemble connected with Vitale and the largest surviving cycle of fourteenth-century painting in Bologna. They are also the most often misdescribed. Understanding them requires separating the building, the confraternity that used it, the several campaigns of painting, and the modern history of detachment.

The church, also known by its later dedication to Sant'Apollonia, stands on the slope of the hills south of Bologna, outside the Porta San Mamolo; the name is usually explained as *a mezza ratta*, "halfway up the climb". It was the seat of a lay confraternity of *disciplinati*<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-disciplinati" class="footnote"><sup>10</sup></a>, devotees who practised penitential discipline and, among other works of charity, assisted pilgrims and comforted the condemned. The complex included the church and two oratories, one of which held the *Madonna dei Denti*, and the decoration was paid for by the confraternity over several generations, from the mid-fourteenth century into the fifteenth.

Within this long campaign, Vitale is credited with the first phase, on the entrance wall. The scenes most often given to him are the *Annunciation*, the *Nativity*, the *Dream of the Virgin*, the *Descent into Limbo* and the *Baptism of Christ*. The Pinacoteca's own room text assigns him the first three; Benati adds work in the apse and a return, after an interval, to paint the *Baptism*. These differences are not trivial for captions: the *Nativity* is universally accepted as Vitale's, while the *Baptism* and the *Descent into Limbo* depend on a particular reconstruction of his involvement.

  <figcaption>The entrance wall of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, general view of the reassembly. Detached frescoes by Vitale da Bologna, c. 1338–1355. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, Room 8.</figcaption>

Before the single fragments, the ensemble. In Room 8 the detached frescoes of the entrance wall are remounted on a neutral ground in the wall's original configuration — a broad gable pierced by the oculus, the main field beneath it, and two wings at the base — so that the visitor can still read the counter-façade of Mezzaratta as one composition, at the cost of the height and light the painters planned for. The modern infill knits the surviving islands of plaster together, and the blank circle at the summit stands where the window once let in the light of the day.

Read from the top: in the gable, Gabriel kneels at the left of the oculus and the slight figure of the Virgin Annunciate answers him at the right, the window itself standing between salutation and assent. A patterned band separates the gable from the main field, where the Nativity holds the centre — the Virgin amid golden rays, the midwife's bath at her side, the great wicker crib with the ass and the ox beneath the striped thatched roofs. At the left a crowd of women gathers around a tall bowing angel; at the right, figures sit among the rocks with their animals. Beneath the central scene run the choir of haloed maidens and the frieze of kneeling, adoring angels. The two lower wings carry the completing episodes: at the left, the *Miraculous Healing* beside the largely ruined *Dream of the Virgin*; at the right, the *Annunciation to the Shepherds* with its flock.

The view settles what the details can only suggest: on this wall the scenes were not framed apart but flowed into one another around the window, the gable alone set off by its band, and the additions of the 1350s were grafted onto the field of the 1340s without a seam the eye can catch at this distance. Each part is examined in the sections that follow.

The *Nativity* is the scene by which Vitale is best known. Its many figures and incidents are spread across the whole width of the field rather than arranged in depth, the figures are agitated and the colours strong. It is the clearest example of what Longhi meant by the sincerity and impulsiveness of Bolognese painting.

### Nativity ("Presepe")

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-09.jpg" alt="Colour detail of the centre of Vitale da Bologna's Mezzaratta Nativity: the haloed Virgin stands upright at the heart of the scene in a pale blue-white mantle, golden rays bursting around her, the paint before her breast abraded; at the left a midwife wrapped in a great ochre-yellow mantle bends over a brown basin and tests the water with spread fingers, while a companion in grey-green pours from a small jug and two fair-haired women look on; at the right the dark head of the ass and the golden-brown ox lean side by side over the plaited wicker rim of the crib, two fair-haired angels flying in above them; below, against dark ground, runs a frieze of kneeling angels in white, rose and green, hands joined or raised in adoration.">
  <figcaption>Nativity ("Presepe"), detail of the central group. Early 1340s; the lower episodes added c. 1353–1355. Fresco, detached and transferred to canvas, 495 × 320 cm (the full field). Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 6346. From the entrance wall of the church of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Pinacoteca's record fixes the data. The fresco, detached and transferred to canvas (495 × 320 cm, inv. 6346), is the oldest part of the whole Mezzaratta cycle, painted probably not long after the construction of the church in 1338, at the beginning of the 1340s; the museum supports the dating with the stylistic affinities to the master's earliest works, beginning with the *Saint George* panel and the slightly later *Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot*. The two episodes that complete the field below, the *Dream of the Virgin* and the *Miraculous Healing*, are judged a later intervention by Vitale himself, around 1353–1355 — so the two campaigns that Benati reconstructs for Mezzaratta, a first phase and a return after an interval, are written into this single wall.

Nothing in the scene stands still. The general view above shows the whole field: no built stable but a thatched roof striped in ochre and a rocky ground, an angel plunging from above, and no masonry manger but a great plaited wicker crib. The photograph takes the central group close. The Virgin stands at the very centre amid a burst of golden rays, a cult image set within the narrative; the paint before her breast is now too damaged to say how she held or presented the Child.

At her side the bath is prepared with domestic exactness — the midwife in her great ochre mantle testing the water with spread fingers while a companion pours from a small jug — and at the right the dark head of the ass and the golden-brown ox, painted with the same expressive licence as the horse of the *Saint George*, push side by side over the plaited rim of the crib, two angels flying in above them. Beneath runs the frieze of adoring angels — kneeling, praying, looking up — painted with the variety of a crowd observed one by one, in Vitale's saturated rose, green and ochre. This is the "animated and perspectivally free" climate that the Pinacoteca's own description names: each figure caught in its own motion, with colour and gesture doing the work that measured space does in Florentine painting.

The fresco has suffered: large losses of plaster interrupt the upper zone, the paint is abraded throughout, and the lower register, with the added episodes, is fragmentary; a geometric border survives along the right side. Even so, it remains the most vivid single image of Vitale's narrative manner.

### The Miraculous Healing ("Guarigione miracolosa")

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-10.jpg" alt="Fresco fragment by Vitale da Bologna in two zones: in the lower scene a sick woman lies in a bed under a white sheet and a yellow-patterned coverlet, her capped head on a scrolled bolster at the right beneath a gilded gabled canopy; behind the bed an elderly hooded man and a woman in a dark mantle pray with joined hands while a third woman in grey-blue reaches towards the patient; in the foreground a woman in a rose mantle sits on a stool with her head sunk in her hands, and at the far left a seated figure in ochre reads from a book; above, beyond a mottled red wall, the upper zone holds the Virgin and Child among haloed young women, Saint Catherine with her wheel among them, while from the right an angel with red wings and a red pointed cap flies down trailing a long inscribed scroll towards the bed; bare plaster and dark gaps interrupt the edges, with fragments of patterned border at the corners.">
  <figcaption>The Miraculous Healing ("Guarigione miracolosa"), c. 1353–1355. Fresco, detached and transferred to canvas. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inventory not verified against the printed catalogues. From the entrance wall of the church of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, Bologna, lower left wing.</figcaption>
</figure>

The *Guarigione miracolosa* is the first of the two episodes of Vitale's second intervention that completed the Nativity field below. Detached separately and transferred to canvas, it hangs in the same Room 8 among the details of the entrance wall. The museum's online page that carries the older photograph of this fragment gives inventory 6347 and 114 × 155 cm under the title *Sogno della Vergine*; the pages for this wall pair photographs and titles in ways that do not always agree, as at the *Annuncio ai pastori*, and the number 6347 most probably belongs to the *Dream of the Virgin* itself, the ruined scene beside this one, catalogued below. This fragment's own inventory and measurements have not been verified against the printed catalogues.

The scene is a sickroom. The patient lies in the bed under a white sheet and a coverlet patterned in yellow, her capped head sunk into the scrolled bolster at the right, her face drained; above the bed's head rises a gabled canopy worked in gold. The household is gathered around her in distinct attitudes of distress: behind the bed an elderly hooded man and a woman in a dark mantle pray with joined hands, a younger woman in grey-blue bends to tend the patient, and at the far left a seated figure in ochre reads from a book — prayers, presumably, read over the sick. Most striking is the woman in the rose mantle seated on a stool in the foreground, her head sunk entirely into her hands: grief given a whole body.

Above the mottled red wall that closes the sickroom, the upper zone holds the court of heaven: the Virgin and Child amid a throng of haloed young women, Saint Catherine among them with her wheel, while from the right an angel with red wings and a red pointed cap flies down trailing a long inscribed scroll towards the bed. It is this descent that gives the scene its name: the healing comes from above, the scroll carrying the answer to the prayers below. In the softer modelling of the faces and the gentler rhythm of the drapery the episode shows the manner of Vitale's last decade, set against the vehemence of the Nativity.

### The Dream of the Virgin ("Sogno della Vergine")

  <figcaption>The Dream of the Virgin ("Sogno della Vergine"), c. 1353–1355. Fresco, detached and transferred to canvas; 114 × 155 cm and inv. 6347 according to the museum's online record, not verified against the printed catalogues. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale. Here is the *Dream of the Virgin* itself: the ruined scene of the lower left wing, beside the *Guarigione*. Even in this condition the identification is secure — the haloed sleeper can be no one else. Like its companion, the episode is part of Vitale's additions of about 1353–1355.

The Virgin lies asleep across the picture, her cheek laid on her joined hands — the immemorial gesture of sleep — beneath a halo of radiating lines, her blond hair loose against the white pillow; her body, in a pale blue robe, stretches along a red bed of which little more than the colour survives. What she dreams is almost entirely lost, the plaster above her bare.

What survives around her is nonetheless remarkable: a walled town seen from above, a rose tower and church front at the upper left, clustered white houses and tiled rooftops running beneath the bed and reappearing at the right edge, drawn with the minute care of an illuminator. A Virgin asleep above a townscape belongs to the visionary strain of the subject — the dream that holds a city, whether the Bethlehem of the Nativity or the Jerusalem of the Passion foreseen — but with the upper zone gone, the content of this dream cannot be named. A fragment of patterned border survives at the lower right.

The face is among the best-preserved passages of the whole wall, and its soft modelling and gentle inflection are those of Vitale's last manner, close to the late Madonnas. Between this sleep and the grief of the sickroom beside it, the two added episodes set side by side two bodies given over — the dreamer at rest, the mourner undone — with an observation that survives even this ruin.

  <figcaption>The Archangel Gabriel ("L'Arcangelo Gabriele"), early 1340s. Fresco, detached and transferred to canvas, 252 × 249 cm. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 6349. From the entrance wall of the church of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The fragment is named for its principal figure, the tall winged angel who bows at its centre. Like the other details of that wall, the museum's record (252 × 249 cm, inv. 6349, detached and transferred to canvas) gives only the cycle's generic bracket and refers back to the *Presepe* for the whole. Which angel is meant, however, is less simple than the title suggests: the Annunciation proper stood higher on the wall, in the gable around the oculus (see below), and the angel of this fragment belongs to another episode.

The angel stands in the open, against the same dark, tree-scattered ground as the Nativity, and inclines towards a young woman in rose-pink who kneels with clasped hands. Read beside the bath of the Child, which fills the right of the photograph — the midwife wrapped in a great ochre mantle bent over the basin, with her attendants and, cut by the fragment's edge, the haloed Virgin of the central group — the women and their angel belong to the apocryphal midwife narrative that runs through this side of the Nativity.

The general view of the wall shows the group within the main field, at the left of the central scene, which places it in the first campaign of the early 1340s; the *Guarigione miracolosa* proper is the bedside scene of the lower left wing, catalogued above, and the exact subject of this group has not been verified against the printed catalogues. The surrounding figures are harder to place: a woman in white with folded arms, another bending deeply forward, and, in the upper left corner, two women embracing cheek to cheek, a motif that recalls the Visitation; the museum's bare record identifies none of them. Above, a flying angel leans over a lacuna holding up a basket, and below survive the choir of haloed maidens and the kneeling angels already met in the other details — a reminder that on this wall the scenes ran into one another without frames.

The condition is that of the rest of the wall: oval losses of plaster in the upper zone, abrasion throughout, and white gaps where the detached fragments meet in the museum's reassembly. What survives best — the angel's long-limbed figure and the kneeling woman's profile — shows the same fluent, drawing-like line as the *Saint George*, bent here to a scene of address and assent rather than combat.

### The Annunciation to the Shepherds ("Annuncio ai pastori")

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-13.jpg" alt="Fragmentary fresco by Vitale da Bologna: at the upper left a haloed angel in white leans down out of a pale aureole, red wing-tips showing at the corner, towards a shepherd who kneels in profile at the centre, raising joined hands, dressed in an ochre tunic and a dark speckled sheepskin with a white pointed hood; at the right a second shepherd in a rose-red mantle bends away, a yellow horn at his belt; below, on dark ground, two sheep with striated fleeces graze head-down beside a third animal that sits upright with raised head and pricked ears; a red border strip closes the scene at the foot, a fragment of decorative border survives at the upper right, and heavy cracks and white gaps between the detached pieces run through the field.">
  <figcaption>The Annunciation to the Shepherds ("Annuncio ai pastori"), early 1340s. Fresco (detached), 114 × 155 cm. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 6348 — catalogued online under the title "Discesa di Cristo al Limbo". From the entrance wall of the church of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Pinacoteca's online record pairs this photograph and this inventory number (6348, 114 × 155 cm, fresco) with the title *Discesa di Cristo al Limbo*, the Descent into Limbo, and gives for it the same generic note as for the other details of the entrance wall, referring back to the *Presepe*. What the photograph shows, however, is unmistakably the annunciation to the shepherds: the angel leaning out of its aureole, the shepherds, the flock. A Descent into Limbo by Vitale did exist at Mezzaratta — it is among the scenes listed above whose attribution depends on the reconstruction of his campaigns — so the online pairing of title, number and image looks like a slip of the web catalogue, and the data have not been verified against the printed catalogues. The measurements, identical to those given for the episodes of the lower left wing, mark the fragments as details of the same order cut from the wall.

The scene itself is the episode that completes the Nativity at the side. The angel, haloed and dressed in white, leans bodily out of a pale aureole at the upper left, as physically present as the dreams and visions elsewhere on this wall. The nearer shepherd has fallen to his knees in profile, joined hands raised, his ochre tunic half covered by a dark, speckled sheepskin and his head in a white pointed hood; his companion in a rose-red mantle turns away, bending, a yellow horn at his belt — caught mid-movement, as if the light had struck him from behind. Below them the flock goes on grazing: two sheep with striated fleeces, heads down, painted with the same linear relish as the ox of the *Presepe*, while a third animal — the shepherd's dog, to judge by its place in the scene — sits upright with pricked ears, the only creature besides the kneeling shepherd to attend to the apparition.

The fragment belongs to the main Nativity field, and so to Vitale's first campaign of the early 1340s. Its condition is poor even by the standards of the wall: long cracks cross the plaster, the surface is abraded, and white gaps separate the detached pieces in the museum's reassembly; a strip of the red painted border survives at the foot, with a fragment of the geometric frame at the upper right. What remains — the rustic dress, the animals, the shepherd's open-air amazement — is Vitale's narrative observation at its most direct.

### The Annunciation ("Annunciazione")

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-14.jpg" alt="Gable-shaped detached fresco by Vitale da Bologna mounted on a dark wall, built around a large circular oculus ringed by a red-and-white patterned border, its opening now filled with a blank panel: at the left the Archangel Gabriel kneels on one knee facing the window, blond and haloed, in a rose mantle over a gold-patterned white tunic, with wings of green and russet and a partly legible inscribed band arcing beside his halo; at the right of the oculus the Virgin stands slight and inward, a grey-violet mantle drawn over her head, hands crossed on her breast and head bowed, beside an ochre wooden lectern and the trace of a slender Gothic aedicule; vertical bands of acanthus scroll frame the outer edges, a rosette medallion survives low on the right, a patterned band runs along the base, and wide bare losses of plaster open above the oculus and through both figures' fields.">
  <figcaption>The Annunciation ("Annunciazione"), 1340s–1350s. Detached fresco, in its gable-shaped field around the oculus of the entrance wall. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, Room 8. From the entrance wall of the church of Santa Maria di Mezzaratta, Bologna; inventory number and measurements not verified against the printed catalogues.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the Annunciation that the Pinacoteca's room text names among the scenes Vitale painted on the entrance wall: not a framed field within the wall but its crown, the triangular gable around the circular oculus, remounted in Room 8 with the empty window ringed by its patterned border. No dedicated record for it was found in the museum's online catalogue, whose records for this wall's details are tangled, as noted above; its inventory number and measurements have not been verified against the printed catalogues. The sequence of Vitale's Mezzaratta inventories (6346–6351, 6356) leaves 6350 unaccounted for, a possible candidate, but this is a conjecture.

The window itself is the heart of the composition. Gabriel kneels at its left on one knee, blond and haloed, an inscribed band — now only partly legible — arcing beside his halo with the opening of the salutation; he wears a rose mantle over a white tunic patterned in gold, and his wings open in green and russet. Across the void of the oculus the Virgin stands small and inward, a grey-violet mantle drawn over her head, hands crossed on her breast, head bowed, her ochre lectern before her and the trace of a slender Gothic aedicule behind. The real light of the window thus stood between the angel's greeting and the Virgin's assent, separating and joining the two figures — an idea of some wit on a wall otherwise crowded edge to edge. Bands of acanthus scroll close the field at the outer edges, with a rosette medallion low on the right and a patterned border along the base; wide losses of plaster open above the oculus and through both figures' fields.

In manner the gable is gentler and more ornamental than the *Presepe* below: the slender, inward Virgin and the soft curls and courtly fall of Gabriel's mantle sit closer to the late Madonnas than to the *Saint George*. Whether that means Vitale's own late manner, as in the episodes added below around 1353–1355, or the hand of a close collaborator working to his design, the bare records do not say; the room text gives the scene to Vitale.

After Vitale's campaign the work passed to other hands. The side walls carried two long cycles, the stories of Joseph and of Moses on one side and the stories of Christ on the other, painted partly in collaboration with him and partly after his death. The Pinacoteca distinguishes a painter who signed *Jacobus*; Simone di Filippo, called [Simone dei Crocifissi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Simone-dei-Crocifissi.html), who worked with *Jacobus* on the *Adoration of the Magi* and the *Healing of the Paralytic*; the anonymous Master of the Well, to whom seven stories of Joseph are given; the Master of the Adulteress; and in a later generation Jacopo Avanzi, Jacopo di Paolo and Cristoforo da Bologna, the last associated with scenes of Moses and a fragment of the *Gathering of Manna*. A Passion cycle in the lowest register was attributed by the sources to the fifteenth-century Ferrarese painter Galasso; only a fragment survives on the wall.

Mezzaratta is therefore not a cycle "by Vitale" but a long campaign begun by him and continued for some forty years by painters who looked to his example. Its value for the history of Bolognese painting lies precisely in this: it shows, on the walls of a single building, how Vitale's manner was taken up, varied and eventually left behind.

No document dates Vitale's frescoes. The 1345 date of the *Madonna dei Denti*, which came from the adjoining oratory, has long served as an anchor; a document of 1338 concerning the confraternity has been read as a possible start of the works. Proposals for Vitale's own campaign therefore range from the late 1330s to the 1350s, and a bracket of about 1338–1355 is often cited for the ensemble of his work there.

Between 1949 and the 1960s the frescoes were detached, with their sinopie, to protect them from water infiltration; the operation was controversial at the time, as were many detachments of those years. They were shown at the Pinacoteca in Longhi's exhibition of 1950 and are now reassembled in Room 8, in the order they had in the church, with the sinopie displayed in the adjoining room (invv. 6346–6351, 6356 for Vitale's scenes). The church itself is deconsecrated. The installation lets the visitor read the cycle as a whole, but the scenes are seen at a different height and in a different light from those the painters planned for. The monograph devoted to Mezzaratta in 2005 remains the fullest study of the cycle and its painters.

In Bologna itself, the frescoes of the *Stories of Mary Magdalene* in Santa Maria dei Servi are placed among his latest works; the 1359 deed at the Servite chapter gives a documentary link with the house.

### Madonna of the Embroidery

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-15.jpg" alt="Detached fresco by Vitale da Bologna with irregular plaster edges and strips of red border at the sides: against a blue-grey ground scratched with small stars, the Virgin, in a rose-mauve mantle lined in green and drawn over a white veil, inclines her head towards the Christ Child seated at the right; her raised right hand pinches a thread at her breast above a white, gold-patterned cloth spread across her lap, and her left hand rests on the Child's arm; the Child, golden-curled and haloed, in a rose tunic, looks up at her open-mouthed and raises his left hand towards her; the lower corners of the plaster are lost.">
  <figcaption>Madonna of the Embroidery (Madonna del Ricamo), 1330–1340. Detached fresco, 118 × 79 cm. Bologna, Museo della Storia di Bologna (Palazzo Pepoli), Fondazione Carisbo collection. From the basilica of San Francesco, Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The *Madonna del Ricamo* adds a third surviving witness to Vitale's long Franciscan employment, beside the *Last Supper* and the documented chapels: a fresco of 118 × 79 cm from the basilica of San Francesco itself, dated in the literature between 1330 and 1340, the decade of the Odofredi payments. Its modern history reads like a novella. Detached from the wall as early as 1801, by Giacomo Beccadelli and Violante Bovio, it then disappeared from view for the better part of two centuries, until in 1978 the Soprintendenza found it in an abandoned oratory at Monte San Pietro, in the hills west of Bologna. Acquired by the Fondazione Carisbo — which devoted an exhibition to it at Casa Saraceni in 2008 — it is now shown in the Museo della Storia di Bologna in Palazzo Pepoli, and it has travelled widely since its recovery: to *Le Madonne di Vitale* in 2010, to Padua for *Guariento e la Padova Carrarese* in 2011, to Autun in 2012, to *Da Cimabue a Morandi* in 2015.

The name records the humblest of its inventions: the Virgin is sewing. Across her lap lies a white cloth patterned in gold — the embroidery — and her raised right hand pinches the thread at her breast, caught between one stitch and the next; her left hand rests on the arm of the Child, who sits at her knee in a rose tunic, golden-curled, and looks up at her open-mouthed, raising a hand as if to interrupt the work. The ground is a blue-grey scratched with small stars, with strips of the red border surviving at the sides; the plaster's irregular edges and the lost lower corners record the fresco's rough passage through the centuries. The Virgin's rose-mauve mantle, lined in green and drawn over a white veil, falls in the long, soft folds of the early work, and her full, gently inclined face belongs to the same family as the *Madonna dei Denti*.

The invention matters beyond its charm. A Virgin at her needlework, interrupted by her son, is a domestic Madonna of a kind rare anywhere in the 1330s, and it shows how early the intimacy of Vitale's late panels was already in place; the apocryphal tradition of Mary as weaver and embroiderer of the Temple veil gives the homely motif its doctrinal footing.

### Fragment of a Crucifixion: the Head of Christ (San Martino Maggiore)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-16.jpg" alt="Stone funerary monument set against a pier in San Martino Maggiore, Bologna: a tall rectangular frame of carved stone with a moulded border, three quatrefoil medallions along the top — a larger one at the centre with a figure in relief, two smaller ones at the sides with carved emblems — enclosing a glazed recess in which an irregular fragment of fresco survives on a pale ground: the head of the dead Christ, eyes closed, inclined towards his right shoulder, long hair falling, within a large gold halo with ornamented cross-arms, set against the brown beam of the cross and the dark ground above it, with the edge of the shoulder below; beneath the recess a stone tablet carries a six-line epitaph in Gothic majuscule, flanked by two carved quatrefoil panels.">
  <figcaption>Fragment of a Crucifixion: the Head of the Dead Christ, c. 1355–1360. Fresco, set within the tomb monument of Stefano Ayguani. Bologna, basilica of San Martino Maggiore, fifth pier on the right. Attributed by Cesare Gnudi (1962).</figcaption>
</figure>

In the Carmelite basilica of San Martino Maggiore, against the fifth pier on the right, a tomb monument of carved stone frames, behind glass, all that survives of a Crucifixion by Vitale: the head of the dead Christ. The attribution is [Cesare Gnudi](/scholars.html#gnudi-cesare)'s (1962), and a study of the Trecento presences in San Martino places the fragment in the painter's last phase, about 1355–1360, comparing it with the Crucifixion of the Fabbri chapel at Udine, with the Thyssen panel and with the Vitalesque cycle of the cathedral of Spilimbergo. The ensemble is a later recomposition: monument and fresco were moved to their present place in 1899 from the first cloister of the friars, beside the door of the chapter-house, where seventeenth-century documents already record them; a local historian's statement that the painting came from the wall closing the friars' choir has no known source.

The epitaph on the tablet below, a verse inscription in Gothic majuscule spoken in the first person and transcribed in full below, names the dead and his family: Stefano of the Ayguani house, his wife Iacoba Sereni and their sons Michael and Bernardus; its last line records his death on the feast of the Magi, 6 January. The elder son is Michele Aiguani, the Carmelite theologian — master of theology at Paris in 1364–1365, prior of San Martino, and from 1381 prior general of the Order — and the younger the Bernardo who entered the Carmelites with him; their mother's name, Giacoma Sereni, is confirmed by Michele's biography in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*. A father's tomb in the convent where his son would rise to lead the Order is thus also a family monument of the house's most famous friar.

The whole transcription follows:

> HIC · EGO · QUI · IACEO · HAYGUANIS · STIRPE · ORIOR (?)
> STEPHANUS · IN · UTERO · GENUI · MEMBRA (?) · DUORUM (?)
> IACOBE · SERENIS · MAIOR · EXTAT · MICHAEL · …
> AT (?) · MINOR · BERNARDUS · AMBO · NOS · EX · FONTE (?) · DOCTOR(UM) (?)
> HIC · PETRA · SIC · TEGIT · … · CENTUM (?) · ET · L · …
> (IL)LUSTRIS · TER · NOVEM · … · IN · FESTO · MAGORUM

"Here I lie, Stephanus, of the Ayguani line. In the womb of Iacoba Sereni I begot two sons: the elder is Michael, the younger Bernardus… This stone covers me…"

What survives of the painting is enough to show why Gnudi gave it to Vitale. The head hangs to Christ's right, the eyes closed, the long hair falling forward; the face is drawn with the gentle, elongated features of the late Madonnas, the grief of the subject expressed not by contortion but by the weight of the inclined head. The halo is large and richly worked, its cross-arms ornamented; behind it the brown beam of the cross runs across the field against a darker ground. The fragment's rough edges, cut from the plaster that held the rest of the Crucifixion, make it a relic in the literal sense, preserved for the sake of the face alone.

The setting gives the fragment its last meaning. Whatever its first location, it now presides over a burial: the dead Christ above the dead man, the image of the redeeming death placed where the epitaph speaks of a private one. For Vitale's catalogue it adds a late work in a Carmelite house to the Franciscan, Servite and confraternity commissions already known, and it connects the painter's final years with a family whose son would become one of the leading theologians of the Trecento Church.

### Christ in Pity between Saints Anthony Abbot and Christopher

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-17.jpg" alt="Square gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna: at the centre the dead Christ is shown half-length, rising from the edge of his tomb, his greenish body naked, his head with long brown hair fallen onto his right shoulder and his eyes closed; his arms are crossed low over his body, the wounds in his hands and the wound in his side bleeding in thin red streams, and a faint halo is incised in the gold behind his head; at the left, much smaller, Saint Christopher in a yellow tunic over red strides forward, leaning on a dark palm-staff and carrying the Christ Child, in pink, on his shoulder; at the right Saint Anthony Abbot, bearded and hooded in a dark brown habit, stands with his hands joined in prayer.">
  <figcaption>Christ in Pity between Saints Anthony Abbot and Christopher ("Gesù Cristo in pietà fra i Santi Antonio Abate e Cristoforo"), c. 1355. Tempera on panel, 53.5 × 54 cm. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 6994. Probably from the suppressed convent of San Giovanni Battista, Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Pinacoteca Nazionale owns a second Bolognese image of the dead Christ, complete where the San Martino fragment is a ruin. The almost square panel, 53.5 × 54 cm in tempera (inv. 6994), is thought to have stood in the suppressed convent of San Giovanni Battista in Bologna; the gallery dates it close to 1355, pointing to its relations with the frescoes of Udine Cathedral (1348–1349) and, a little later, with those of Pomposa (1351), whose delicate handling of colour it shares.

The type is the *imago pietatis*, the dead Christ shown half-length rising from his tomb, without the figures who hold him up in the Fondazione Longhi panel. His body, painted in the greenish flesh tones Vitale reserves for the dead, fills the centre of the field; the head, with its long brown hair, has fallen onto the right shoulder, the eyes closed; the arms are crossed low over the body, so that the wounds of both hands are shown together with the wound in the side, each bleeding in thin red streams. Only a faint halo, incised in the gold, marks his divinity. The edge of the sarcophagus shows at the bottom of the field, and the gold ground is the only setting.

The two saints flank him at a much smaller scale, as intercessors before a devotional image rather than witnesses to an event. At the left Saint Christopher strides forward in a yellow tunic over red, leaning on a dark palm-staff and carrying the Christ Child on his shoulder; at the right Saint Anthony Abbot, bearded and hooded in his dark habit, joins his hands in prayer. The pairing is a choice of protectors: Christopher, whose image was believed to guard against sudden death on the day it was seen, and Anthony, invoked against the plague and the fire that bears his name. In a panel made a few years after the Black Death, the two saints stand beside the dead Christ as guarantors of a good death and of healing.

The gallery's dating places the panel in the same years as the San Salvatore polyptych and the late Madonnas, and its treatment of the dead body shows the gentleness of that phase applied to the Passion. Where the early Crucifixions of Philadelphia and Madrid set the dead Christ in a crowd and a drama, here the body is isolated and still, offered for meditation. With the San Martino fragment and the Longhi Pietà it shows how often, in his last decade, Vitale returned to the image of Christ's dead body.

Ferrara contributes a second group, headed by the *Madonna dei Battuti*, which is not in Ferrara but in the Vatican.

  <figcaption>Madonna dei Battuti ("Stendardo (?): Madonna dei Battuti"), 1345–1350. Tempera and gold on polylobed panel, frame in gilded pastiglia, 95.6 × 68 cm. Signed at the foot. The Vatican's record identifies it, with a question mark, as a *stendardo*, a processional standard — a plausible function for the image of a flagellant confraternity, made to be carried as well as venerated — and dates it 1345–1350. Its provenance is unusually well documented: it comes from the church of the Ospedale dei Battuti Bianchi at Ferrara, is recorded in 1801 in the hospital's upper hall, was bought in 1841 by the Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, and entered the new Pinacoteca of Pius X, in Sala I, on 2 January 1909 (inv. MV.40017.0.0). The panel is polylobed, tempera and gold, 95.6 × 68 cm, in its frame of gilded pastiglia worked with foliage.

Along the foot, on a white band, runs the signature

> .VITALIS. DE BONONIA. F(ECIT).

The inscription is accepted as original, which makes the Vatican panel the third surviving work to carry the painter's name, beside the signed and dated *Madonna dei Denti* and the monogrammed *Saint George*: a fixed point in its own right.

The image is built on a diagonal of glances. The Virgin, wrapped in her near-black mantle with gold-worked borders, turns her long, heavy-lidded eyes down to the left; the Child, half-naked in a pale rose wrap on her arm, looks the same way and raises his right hand in blessing. What they look at kneels in the lower corner: the Battuti themselves, a cluster of small figures in the white hooded habit of the company, one hood marked with a cross, hands raised in prayer, the foremost holding the dark scourge of their discipline, a slender processional crucifix rising behind them. The blessing, the gaze and the prayer close a circuit between the image and the men who carried it — the portrait of the patron as a group noted below among the confraternity commissions. The Virgin's spread right hand, impossibly long-fingered across the Child's body, is one of the most elegant passages in all of Vitale.

Between the *Madonna dei Denti* of 1345 and the late Madonnas of the 1350s, the panel shows the devotional image turning towards the dark, grave elegance of the final decade; it is also the essential reminder that his Ferrarese patrons were lay penitents, not only bishops.

The fragmentary *Stories of Saint Maurelius*, found in 1949 in a chapel at the base of the bell tower of Santo Stefano in Ferrara and now in the Museo di Casa Romei, were attributed to Vitale on their discovery; a difficult detachment in 1950 left the paint surface in poor condition.

The wooden statues contracted for Ferrara Cathedral in 1343 have not been identified.

Several panels are now outside Italy, beginning with two early Calvaries in Madrid and Philadelphia.

### The Crucifixion (Madrid)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-19.jpg" alt="Tall gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna with the three crosses of Calvary: at the centre Christ hangs on a high yellow cross, his grey-green body sagging, while small angels fly about his wounds; the two thieves hang on lower crosses at the sides, their arms bent back over the crossbeams, a tiny angel rising by the thief at the left and a small dark winged devil perched on the cross of the thief at the right; below, at the left, the Virgin in a bright blue mantle collapses into the arms of the holy women as the long-haired Magdalene grasps the shaft of the cross, with soldiers in pale hats and lances behind; at the centre and right a troop of cavalry crowds in — a horse masked in black netted mail with gilt ornaments, a white and a brown horse, an armoured centurion raising his hand — while bearded elders argue, a bare-backed man in green twists away, and in the lower corner soldiers crouch to cast lots over a white garment; blood runs down the cross to the rocky mound, beneath which a complete skeleton lies in a dark cavity at the foot of the panel.">
  <figcaption>The Crucifixion, c. 1335. Tempera and gold on panel, 93 × 51.2 cm. Madrid, Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, inv. 425 (1930.122).</figcaption>
</figure>

The Thyssen Crucifixion is the larger and the more crowded of the two early Calvaries: a tall panel of 93 × 51.2 cm, tempera and gold, dated by the museum about 1335 and accepted — like its Philadelphia companion — among the earliest works in the catalogue. Its elongated vertical format has been connected in the literature with the window-pierced back wall of the high chapel of Udine Cathedral, a reminder that the panel's original setting, like its early history before the Thyssen collection, remains open.

Unlike the Johnson panel, this is a full Calvary of three crosses. Christ hangs at the centre on a high yellow cross, grey-green against the gold, small angels darting about his wounds; the thieves hang lower at the sides, their arms wrenched back over the crossbeams — and above them the panel states their judgement with fairy-tale literalness: a small angel rises beside the penitent thief at the left, while a dark winged devil perches on the cross of the impenitent at the right, waiting for his soul. At the very foot, under the bleeding rock of Golgotha, lies not the customary skull but a complete skeleton stretched in its dark cavity: Adam entire beneath the cross that redeems him.

Between these poles the crowd surges. At the left the Virgin, in a mantle of brilliant blue, folds backward into the arms of the holy women while the Magdalene, her hair loose to the waist, clasps the shaft; soldiers in pale brimmed hats raise their lances behind. At the centre and right the cavalry arrives in force — a horse masked in black netted mail with gilt trappings, its eye glinting through the armour, beside a white and a brown mount — and around the armoured centurion, hand raised in recognition, bearded elders dispute, a bare-backed executioner twists away, and in the lower corner the soldiers crouch over the seamless garment, the game of lots already begun while the blood is still running.

Set beside the Philadelphia panel, the Thyssen Calvary shows the same youthful fusion of violence, grief and luxury expanded to a bigger stage: more horses, more armour, more judgement. The observed cavalry that will rear in the *Saint George*, the swooning Virgin of the later Crucifixions, the taste for mail and trapping as precious surface — all are already here, around a dead Christ and a waiting skeleton, in what is among the most complete statements of Vitale's early manner to survive.

### The Crucifixion (Philadelphia)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-20.jpg" alt="Shaped panel by Vitale da Bologna in an engaged gilded frame with a trefoil arch, the black spandrels holding two winged creatures, a haloed angel with a red book at the left and an eagle-headed figure with an inscribed scroll at the right: at the centre Christ hangs on a pale blue-grey cross against gold tooled with scrolling foliage, his corpse-toned body sagging, blood springing from his hands and running down the shaft from his feet, a curling titulus above his head; at the left the Virgin in a black mantle collapses into the arms of the holy women while the golden-haired Magdalene in red kneels and grasps the foot of the cross, and soldiers behind them drive a long lance upwards into Christ's side beneath a teal banner; at the right a bearded man in a red hood prays upwards amid armoured soldiers under an orange banner, a horse in chain mail turns its caparisoned rump to the viewer, and at the lower corner a group of men stretches out a white garment between them, swords at their belts.">
  <figcaption>The Crucifixion, with symbols of Saints Matthew and John the Evangelist, c. 1335. Tempera and tooled gold on panel, 62.1 × 40 cm. Philadelphia, Philadelphia Museum of Art, John G. Johnson Collection, 1917, cat. 1164. Made in Bologna.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Philadelphia Crucifixion is a shaped panel of 62.1 × 40 cm, tempera and tooled gold, dated by the museum about 1335 and thus among the earliest works in the catalogue. It came to Philadelphia with the collection of the lawyer John G. Johnson, bequeathed to the city in 1917, where it carries the catalogue number 1164.

The frame is part of the argument. The painted field rises into a trefoil arch, and in the black spandrels outside it perch two winged creatures: the haloed angel of Matthew with his red book at the left, the eagle of John with his inscribed scroll at the right. Two evangelist symbols imply the other two, and with them a larger structure — the panel as the surviving element, or one wing, of a complex whose remainder is lost.

Within the arch the subject is told at full emotional pressure. Christ's body is a corpse among the living, blue-grey against the warm gold, sagging from arms stretched wide along the trefoil's curve; blood springs from the nailed hands and runs in a long ribbon down the shaft from his feet, and the gold behind him is tooled with scrolling foliage, a brocade of light around a dead man. At the left the Virgin, in black, folds up into the arms of the holy women; the Magdalene, golden-haired and dressed in flame, kneels to grasp the foot of the cross; behind them the lance of Longinus is driven upward into the side. At the right the crowd answers: the red-hooded centurion prays up at the cross amid armoured companions, a war-horse in chain mail swings its caparisoned rump into the viewer's space — a piece of observed cavalry worthy of the *Saint George* — and in the corner the soldiers stretch the seamless garment between them, the lots about to be cast over a piece of white cloth painted as carefully as a relic.

Here, five or so years before the storiette and the *Saint George*, the whole repertory is already in place: the crowd built from particulars, the violence and the grief given equal time, the precious surface — tooled gold, mail, brocade — wrapped around an image of death.

The diptych formed by an *Adoration of the Magi* in the National Galleries of Scotland, Edinburgh, and a *Body of Christ Mourned by Saints* in the Fondazione Longhi, Florence, is catalogued wing by wing below.

### The Adoration of the Magi (Edinburgh)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-21.jpg" alt="Arched gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna with its original mouldings: the Virgin sits at the left on a bench throne in a black, gold-hemmed mantle, holding the lively Child, who leans far out of her arms to lay his hand on the white head of the eldest magus, kneeling in a yellow mantle with his crown set aside by his halo; behind him a second magus in grey-green flings an arm high towards the cusp while the youngest, in a red damask tunic and yellow hose, carries a casket; an angel with red wings hovers at the upper left and a pale veiled figure at the upper right; at the lower left stand two crowned virgin saints, Ursula with her staff and companions crowding behind her and Catherine pointing, with her spiked golden wheel; at the lower right two grooms in tall hats wait among dark trees with the magi's horses, two pale and one dark.">
  <figcaption>The Adoration of the Magi, c. 1353–1355. Tempera and gold on panel, 60.4 × 38.6 cm including the original mouldings. Edinburgh, National Galleries of Scotland, NG 952 (purchased 1908). Left wing of a diptych; the right wing, The Body of Christ Mourned by Saints, is in the Fondazione Longhi, Florence.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Edinburgh Adoration is the left half of the catalogue's one surviving diptych: hinged in the middle to its companion in the Fondazione Longhi, it opened and closed like a book, an aid to prayer and meditation that paired the beginning of Christ's story with its end. The gallery dates it about 1353–1355, the years of the San Salvatore contract; it was purchased in 1908 and carries the inventory NG 952, the panel measuring 60.4 × 38.6 cm with its original mouldings.

The scene itself is pure narrative delight. The Virgin, in a black, gold-hemmed mantle, holds a Child who will not be held: he leans far out of her arms to lay his hand on the white head of the eldest magus, kneeling in yellow with his crown set aside beside his halo — blessing turned into a child's irresistible reach. Behind him the second king flings an arm high towards the star in the cusp, as vehement a gesture as any at Mezzaratta, while the youngest, in red damask and yellow hose, waits with his casket; an angel with red wings attends at the upper left. At the lower right, in a pocket of dark wood, the journey waits too: two grooms in tall hats holding the kings' horses, pale and dark, the travelling world of the storiette tucked beneath the epiphany.

What makes the panel singular is the company it keeps. At the lower left stand two virgin martyrs of later centuries, identified by their attributes: Ursula with her staff, the heads of her eleven thousand companions crowding behind her, and Catherine of Alexandria pointing, her spiked golden wheel in hand. Saints of the Roman persecutions admitted into the stable at Bethlehem is an anachronism with a purpose — the devotional present folded into the sacred past, exactly what a prayer-diptych was for — and it extends once more the devotion to Catherine that runs through Vitale's whole catalogue.

Criticism has long seen two hands in the diptych: the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* gives the design to Vitale and recognises the participation of an assistant, with Simone di Filippo the name most often proposed — which would make the little book of prayers also a document of the workshop in the master's last years, the next generation already at his elbow. The leaning Child, the flung arm, the horses in the wood are the old painter's own; the diptych as a whole, like the Mezzaratta walls, shows how his manner was already being shared as it was being perfected.

### The Body of Christ Mourned by Saints (Florence)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-22.jpg" alt="Arched gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna in its engaged gilded frame, the four winged symbols of the Evangelists in the spandrels — the angel and the eagle at the upper left, the lion and the ox at the upper right: at the centre the dead Christ stands upright in a red sarcophagus, grey-green, his head fallen on his shoulder and his arms hanging over the rim, the side wound visible; the Virgin in a black mantle holds him from behind with both hands spread on his chest; at the left the Baptist in camel hair crosses his hands on his breast beside a young fair-haired mourner in red, and at the right the Magdalene, golden-haired and dressed in red, joins her hands in prayer; below, a white shroud hangs over the front of the tomb, flanked by two kneeling bishops in mitres and brocade copes with raised hands — at the left with an aged, bearded hermit in grey-blue behind him, at the right with a young female saint in green.">
  <figcaption>The Body of Christ Mourned by Saints (Christ in Piety), c. 1353–1355. Tempera and gold on panel. Florence, Fondazione di Studi di Storia dell'Arte Roberto Longhi. Right wing of the diptych whose left wing, The Adoration of the Magi, is in the National Galleries of Scotland, Edinburgh.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Longhi panel is the closing leaf of the Edinburgh diptych: when the little book of prayers was shut, the Adoration and the dead Christ lay face to face, birth folded onto death. That it belongs to the Fondazione Longhi is itself part of the painter's history — the panel stayed with the scholar who did most to invent the modern Vitale, and it remains in the villa-foundation of Il Tasso in Florence among the works he chose to live with. The diptych is dated with its Edinburgh half to about 1353–1355.

The image is the *imago pietatis* — the dead Christ standing upright in his tomb — given the full apparatus of a lamentation. The body is the colour of the Philadelphia and Thyssen Christs, grey-green against the gold; the head has fallen onto the shoulder, the hair hangs forward, and the arms droop over the rim of the red sarcophagus with the long, boneless weight of a corpse. Behind him the Virgin, hooded in black, holds him up with both hands spread across his chest, her cheek almost against his — an embrace that is also a presentation, offering the body to the viewer as the angels do in the Venetian versions of the theme. At the left the Baptist crosses his hands on his breast beside a fair-haired young mourner in red; at the right the Magdalene, golden-haired, joins her hands in prayer and looks up at the face she cannot reach.

Below, the composition becomes an altar. A white shroud hangs over the front of the tomb like an altar-cloth, and at its two corners kneel two bishops in mitres and brocaded copes, gloved hands raised — the posture of celebrants at the elevation. Behind each stands a companion: at the left an aged, bearded hermit in grey-blue, at the right a young virgin saint in green. Their names are not inscribed, and the pairing is best left open; what matters is the design, in which the saints of the devotee's own calendar keep vigil at the Eucharistic tomb, just as Ursula and Catherine slip into the stable at Bethlehem on the facing wing. In the spandrels the four evangelist symbols — angel and eagle, lion and ox — frame the scene as the witnesses of the Gospel, completing the set of which the Philadelphia Crucifixion keeps only two.

Read with its companion, the panel makes the diptych's argument plain. The Adoration is all motion and outward journey — horses, gestures, the Child reaching out of his mother's arms; the Pietà is stillness and return, the Virgin's arms closing once more around the same body, now dead. Whatever the share of the workshop, the conception — infancy and Passion folded together for private prayer, the saints of the present admitted to both — is Vitale's, and it is among the most affecting in his catalogue.

  <figcaption>The Coronation of the Virgin ("Le Couronnement de la Vierge"), 14th century. Gold ground on poplar panel, 52 × 57 cm. Paris, Musée du Louvre, Département des Peintures, inv. RF 1996 19 (acquired 1996). Formerly Adolphe Stoclet collection, Brussels.</figcaption>
</figure>

Among the panels outside Italy the most recent arrival in a public collection is the Coronation of the Virgin in the Louvre, a nearly square poplar panel of 52 × 57 cm whose painted surface, the museum's record suggests, may originally have followed a polylobed cut at the top — the trace of a richer carpentry, and perhaps of a larger complex, now lost. Its modern history runs through one of the great twentieth-century collections: Adolphe Stoclet (1871–1949) in Brussels, then Mme Féron-Stoclet, a deposit at the Cleveland Museum of Art from 1980 to 1993, and purchase by the Louvre from the heirs, through the Paris gallery Brimo de Laroussilhe, in 1996 (ministerial decree of 27 November). It hangs in the Salle des Sept Mètres of the Denon wing, dated by the museum simply to the fourteenth century.

The subject is the San Salvatore centre re-imagined as a devotional close-up. Christ, in rose, sits on a red-cushioned bench and sets the crown with both hands; the Virgin kneels with her hands crossed on her breast, her near-black mantle striated all over with threads of gold. Behind them the cloth of honour is not merely hung but carried: three angels hold its upper edge aloft, a white swag draped across their hands, so that heaven's textile arrives like a canopy borne in procession — the woven heaven of the San Salvatore panel made explicit as an act. Haloed heads crowd the sides in punched clusters; at the left a tall angel in yellow raises a dark disc rimmed in red, most plausibly the mirror of the Marian litanies held towards the crowned Virgin; at the right an aged, bearded saint in a robe of silver damask attends with joined hands, a witnessing patriarch whom the record leaves unnamed.

Everything in the panel belongs to the vocabulary of Vitale's devotional painting at its most luxurious: the chrysography of the Virgin's mantle, the diapered textile, the crowding of small haloes until the gold itself seems inhabited, the courtly gentleness of the two protagonists. Whether it stands before or after the polyptych of 1353, the comparison between the two Coronations — the one public and architectural, the other intimate and processional — is among the most instructive in the catalogue.

The panel has also earned its place in the literature of the painter's name. It was discussed by Longhi in the Trecento course of 1934–1935, shown in *Giotto e le arti a Bologna al tempo di Bertrando del Poggetto* (Bologna, 2005–2006) and in the Autun exhibition of 2012; and it appears in the article of 1929, "Vitale delle Madonne e Simone dei Crocifissi" — the study whose very title fixed the formula by which the painter of these gentle Virgins has been known ever since.

### The Coronation of the Virgin (Budrio)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-24.jpg" alt="Small panel by Vitale da Bologna, cut at the top and sides: on a throne with grey-green marble posts and an elaborately stepped base inlaid with coloured panels, before a cloth of honour patterned all over with rosettes of red, blue and green on gold, Christ, young and fair-haired, crowned and haloed, in a rose robe and mantle lined with green, sits at the right and raises his right hand high to set a small crown on the head of the Virgin, his left hand lifted beside her face; the Virgin sits at the left in a near-black mantle edged with gold and marked with gold stars at the shoulder and knee, her hands crossed on her breast and her head bowed towards him; both sit on a long red cushion embroidered with small gold flourishes; at the cut edges, left and right, survive fragments of angels.">
  <figcaption>The Coronation of the Virgin, c. 1335–1340. Tempera and gold on panel, 42 × 30.5 cm. Budrio, Pinacoteca Civica Domenico Inzaghi, inv. C. 97. From the Inzaghi bequest (1824).</figcaption>
</figure>

The third of the catalogue's Coronations is the smallest and, on the prevailing view, the earliest. The panel in the Pinacoteca Civica Domenico Inzaghi at Budrio, 42 × 30.5 cm in tempera and gold (inv. C. 97), came to the town with the bequest of Domenico Inzaghi in 1824, whose inventory lists it simply as "an old little picture on wood"; the regional catalogue, compiled by Daniele Benati, excludes the old hypothesis of a provenance from San Domenico in Bologna. Once described as the work of a "Venetian primitive", it was given to Vitale by Roberto Longhi in his lectures of 1933–1934, and the attribution has never been questioned since; it was shown in the exhibition of Bolognese fourteenth-century painting of 1950. Its date has moved more: proposals range from after 1353 to about 1350, but the regional record favours about 1335–1340, on the strength of comparisons with the Mezzaratta frescoes and with the *Last Supper* from San Francesco.

The panel has been cut at the top and sides, with losses among the angels whose fragments survive at the edges; punched decoration in the upper corners suggests that it once ended in a polylobed arch, and broad losses of colour along the top and bottom were probably caused by a later frame. It may have belonged to a larger altarpiece. The base of the throne, with its stepped and inlaid profile, survives intact at the foot. It was suggested in 1981 that the composition recalls the great miniature that opens a manuscript of the *Decretals* in the Vatican Library (Pal. lat. 636) — a reminder of how closely Bolognese panel painting and the illumination of legal books were bound together in the city of the university.

The scene is reduced to its two protagonists and drawn with remarkable clarity. Before a cloth of honour patterned all over with rosettes of red, blue and green on gold, Christ, young and fair-haired, already crowned and haloed, sits at the right in rose with a green-lined mantle and raises his right hand high to lower a small crown onto his mother's head, his left hand lifted beside her face in a gesture between blessing and caress. The Virgin, in a mantle so dark it reads as black, its edges worked in gold and gold stars at shoulder and knee, crosses her hands on her breast and bows her head towards him. Both sit on a long red cushion embroidered with small gold flourishes, on a throne whose grey-green marble posts and inlaid steps give the heavenly event a solid architectural seat.

Set beside the San Salvatore centre and the Louvre panel, the Budrio Coronation shows the subject at its most intimate: no court of angels in view, no saints, only the bowed Virgin and the young Christ reaching up. Its relations with Vitale's workshop are underlined by a curious modern episode. For a time the panel and a *Coronation of the Virgin* signed by Simone di Filippo — "Symon fecit", one of the earliest works of the assistant who worked beside Vitale — changed places on loan: Simone's panel came from the Pinacoteca Nazionale to Budrio, while Vitale's was shown in Bologna. The exchange put the master's composition and the assistant's version of the same subject side by side in the history of the two collections, as the comparison deserves.

The catalogue closes with the late Madonnas, a group — among them the Poldi Pezzoli panel in Milan, the *Madonna della Pace* at Forlì and the Viterbo panel with a kneeling cardinal, all catalogued below — placed between the early 1340s and the mid-1350s: the works in which, as noted above, Benati sees Vitale's return to a Gothic ideal of beauty. The group continues into the workshop production that outlived him.

### Madonna of Humility ("Madonna dell'Umiltà")

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-25.jpg" alt="Small gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna in a gilded tabernacle frame with pierced vine tracery and a crocketed ogival gable: the Virgin, in a dark blue robe and hooded mantle covered edge to edge with gold filigree ornament and a jewelled band across her brow, sits on the ground on a red carpet strewn with dark trefoils, inclining her head towards the half-naked Christ Child at her side, who looks up and touches her chin with one finger while she steadies him in a gold-shot green cloth; at the left stands Saint Catherine with her wheel and a green book, at the right a second virgin saint with a slender attribute, both at reduced scale; beside the Child a small round table carries little flasks and a bowl, and the tooled gold ground radiates around the Virgin's halo.">
  <figcaption>Madonna of Humility with Two Female Saints ("Madonna dell'Umiltà"), c. 1353. Tempera and gold on panel, 41 × 24 cm. Milan, Museo Poldi Pezzoli, inv. 1574 (bequest of Gian Giacomo Poldi Pezzoli, 1879).</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the Milan panel of the late group: a small devotional tavola of 41 × 24 cm, tempera and gold, in the Museo Poldi Pezzoli, where it hangs in the Sala dei Fondi Oro. It came to the museum with Gian Giacomo Poldi Pezzoli's bequest of 1879 (inv. 1574), and the museum dates it about 1353 — the very years of the San Salvatore contract and of the second campaign at Mezzaratta. Its exhibition history tracks the painter's modern fortune: the Kunsthaus of Zurich in 1948–1949, Longhi's exhibition of Bolognese fourteenth-century painting in 1950, and "Le Madonne di Vitale" at the Musei Civici d'Arte Antica of Bologna in 2010–2011, the show whose title revived the old name "Vitale delle Madonne".

The subject is the Madonna of Humility: no throne, but the Virgin seated on the ground on a red carpet strewn with dark trefoils. Humility, however, wears extraordinary clothes. Her dark blue robe and mantle are covered edge to edge with gold filigree ornament, a jewelled band crosses her brow beneath the veil, and the gold ground is tooled into rays around her halo, so that the whole small surface glitters like the goldsmith's work its frame imitates — a gilded tabernacle with pierced vine tracery rising to a crocketed ogival gable. The Child, half-naked and hatched with gold, looks up from her side and touches her chin with one finger — the gesture of the *Madonna dei Denti* of 1345, returned after a decade with the monumentality of the cult image exchanged for intimacy. The Virgin steadies him in a gold-shot green cloth; his other hand reaches towards a little round table set with flasks and a bowl, a touch of nursery furniture painted with complete seriousness. Two virgin saints attend at reduced scale, half emerging behind the carpet: Catherine at the left with her wheel and a green book, and at the right a companion whose slender attribute no longer reads with certainty.

The panel is the clearest surviving instance of that late manner, with surfaces worked like ornament. Set beside the *Madonna dei Denti*, it shows the distance travelled between 1345 and the 1350s — from the cult statue to the jewel.

### Madonna della Pace

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-26.jpg" alt="Small panel by Vitale da Bologna in its engaged gilded frame with a red fillet: against a gold ground overlaid with a red lattice enclosing green quatrefoils, the Virgin is seen bust-length in a dark blue-green mantle with a gold-embroidered border drawn over her head, her heavy-lidded eyes turned to the left; across her arms lies the Christ Child in a transparent gold-hemmed shift, a gold bracelet at his wrist, his head ringed by a halo with a red cross, looking out at the viewer; the Virgin's long right hand supports his legs, her punched halo presses against the patterned ground, and the gilding of the frame is worn and cracked.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child ("Madonna della Pace"), 1345–1355. Mixed technique on panel. Forlì, Museo Civico San Domenico, on deposit from the Dominican Order since 2013. From the church of San Giacomo Apostolo of the Dominicans, Forlì.</figcaption>
</figure>

The small panel known as the *Madonna della Pace* extends the group to Forlì. It belongs to the Dominicans<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-dominic-guzman" class="footnote"><sup>11</sup></a> of the church of San Giacomo Apostolo, within the San Domenico complex, and has hung since 2013 in the civic museum installed there, on deposit from the Order; the museum dates it 1345–1355, the years between the *Madonna dei Denti* and the documented late works. Its name is the deposit of a long devotion: by tradition it was the image that the Dominican Beato Marcolino da Forlì (1317–1397) kept by him in his works of charity and his peacemaking between the city's rival families, and as late as the Second World War the Forlivesi gathered before it to pray for their relatives at war and for the end of the conflict.

The image is of the same dark, grave family as the Vatican *Madonna dei Battuti*. The Virgin appears bust-length, her blue-green mantle edged with a gold-embroidered border drawn over her head, her heavy-lidded eyes turned aside; behind her the gold ground is overlaid with a red lattice enclosing green quatrefoils, a painted cloth of honour that turns the whole field into textile. Across her arms — and this is the panel's distinction — the Child does not sit but lies, dressed in a transparent shift hemmed with gold, a gold bracelet at his wrist, his halo marked with a red cross; he turns his face out of the picture, towards whoever prays, while his mother looks elsewhere. The long right hand supporting his legs is the same impossibly elegant hand as in the Battuti panel. The engaged frame, gilded over a red fillet, is worn and cracked but preserved.

Small, portable and endlessly prayed before, the panel is a devotional image whose working life continued for six centuries after its painter. For the catalogue it adds a Romagnol address to the late group of Milan and Viterbo and, if its provenance reaches back to the Trecento, a Dominican one in a clientele otherwise dominated by Franciscans, confraternities and monks.

### Madonna and Child with a Cardinal and a Holy Bishop (Viterbo)

  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-27.jpg" alt="Shaped gold-ground panel by Vitale da Bologna, its trilobed arch framed by a twisted-rope moulding with small notches at the spring of the arch: the Virgin, half-length in a deep blue mantle over a gold-embroidered collar band, her blond hair showing, inclines her head above an unusually large halo tooled with floral ornament that fills the arch; the Child, in a rose wrap shot with gold, twists in her arms away from her breast to look back over his shoulder and raise his right hand in blessing towards the lower right corner, where a small kneeling cardinal in a dark habit, a red wide-brimmed hat on his head, prays with joined hands, presented by a taller haloed figure in a pointed, crown-ringed headdress holding a thin staff; the gilding and punchwork are rubbed throughout.">
  <figcaption>Madonna and Child with a Cardinal and a Holy Bishop, c. 1341–1350. Tempera and gold on panel. Viterbo, Museo Civico. Connected in the Fondazione Federico Zeri catalogue with the church of Santa Maria della Pace, Viterbo.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the Viterbo panel of the late group. It hangs in the Museo Civico of Viterbo, and the Fondazione Federico Zeri photo archive places it at the middle of the century (1341–1350), naming the church of Santa Maria della Pace at Viterbo in its history; the museum catalogue of 1955 (pp. 42–43) is the reference, and the panel's measurements and inventory have not been verified against it. The identity of the haloed figure who presents the kneeling donor is itself unsettled: the Fondazione Federico Zeri entry calls him a holy pope, and his pointed, crown-ringed headdress can be read as a tiara, but a holy bishop in a jewelled mitre has also been proposed; he carries a thin staff and remains, like the cardinal he sponsors, without a name.

The panel is shaped: a trilobed arch within a twisted-rope frame, with small notches at the spring of the arch and traces of lateral colonnettes, the field entirely gold and the Virgin's halo — enormous, tooled with floral ornament — filling the lobes like a second architecture. Against this the drama is one of attention. The Virgin, in deep blue over a gold-embroidered collar, inclines her head with lowered eyes; the Child, wrapped in rose shot with gold, twists away from her breast to look back over his shoulder at the kneeling cardinal and bless him — the same closed circuit of gaze, blessing and prayer as in the Vatican *stendardo*, here sprung by the Child's whole body. The cardinal kneels at reduced scale in a dark habit, the red galero on his head, hands joined; his sponsor's hand hovers at his shoulder.

For the catalogue the panel matters twice over. It is the most refined of the late Madonnas after the Poldi Pezzoli panel, with the same elongated eyes and ornamental gold; and it is the single trace of a curial clientele in Vitale's œuvre — a cardinal, in the papal city of Viterbo — though the donor is nameless and no document supports the connection, which rests, like the attribution itself, on the panel alone.

## The Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot

Four small panels in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna (invv. 10039–10042) — hung together in Room 1, on deposit since 1937 from ecclesiastical property — are all that survives of an ensemble dedicated to Saint Anthony Abbot. Tall and narrow, nearly uniform in size (between 77 × 39 and 79 × 37 cm), painted in tempera about 1340–1345, they were the wings of a dismembered complex whose central image of the titular saint, known only from the sources, carried the signature *Vitalis f(ecit)*: had it survived, it would stand today among the fixed points of the catalogue. The Fondazione Federico Zeri photo archive records an earlier station in the Museo di Santo Stefano.

Their subjects long resisted naming, for they do not come from the life of the hermit by Athanasius<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-athanasius-the-great" class="footnote"><sup>12</sup></a>. They were deciphered from the apocryphal *Legend of Patras*, the romance in which Anthony is a monk of Patras who leaves his monastery by stealth for the desert, is provisioned by the King of Palestine through camels guided by a wheel of fire, and goes on working miracles after his death — upholding the unjustly hanged, reviving the victims of wild beasts, healing the possessed daughter of an emperor at his own funeral. Each panel carries two episodes, stacked in a single field without dividing frames; the sections below follow the order of the legend.

Criticism has loved the storiette. Longhi found in them "movements of a descriptive liveliness that cannot be found in Tuscan art"; a later critic observed how Vitale's paint runs out over the gilded framing bands, punched with arcs, so that each scene seems a slice of life caught as the page turns. They are the narrative counterpart of the *Saint George*: the same years, the same fluent line and burning colour, applied not to a single combat but to a whole romance — court and desert, gallows and funeral.

  <figcaption>Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot: Anthony leaves the monastery of Patras; Anthony defeats the dragon that appeared to him at the spring, c. 1340–1345. Tempera on panel, 79 × 37 cm. </figure>

The series opens, in the order of its legend, with the departure from Patras. Two episodes share the field: above, Anthony takes his leave of the monastery; below, he overcomes the creature that rises against him at a spring.

In the *Legend of Patras* Anthony, resolved on the desert, leaves his community by stealth, lowered from the walls in a basket — the escape of Paul from Damascus transferred to the hermit — and on his way defeats the demon that confronts him in monstrous form.

The two episodes are stacked without a dividing frame. A shelf of red-brown rock is the only threshold: above it the monastery precinct stands against burnished gold, below it a night-dark thicket closes in. The eye descends as Anthony does, from architecture to wilderness, and the fall of the ground itself carries the story.

Above, the monastery is a building of rose-coloured blind arcades and tiled roofs, with a tall grey tower behind; a thin rope still hangs from its summit into Anthony's hands, the trace of the basket of the legend. The saint, bearded and haloed, in the grey-black habit he wears throughout the series, has reached the ground and clasps the hand of a monk who bows to him, a gold lantern hanging at his wrist; from the doorway at the left an older face watches. The lantern makes the hour explicit: this is a flight by night.

Below, the thicket thickens, bush upon stippled bush of yellow-green against darkness. Anthony kneels at its centre and stretches a hand towards a Gothic fountain — a hexagonal basin on a slender stem, crowned by a small white statuette — in whose water the creature appeared; behind him the companion of the upper scene draws back, lantern in hand. The defeated creature lies in the water of the basin, golden-brown against the dark; at the lower right a man in a red tunic lies stretched on his back, a hand raised towards the saint, where the paint breaks off.

Small inventions bind the episodes. The lantern-bearer recurs in both, a travelling companion who carries the narrative as he carries the light; the fountain, an elegant piece of civic furniture set down in a wilderness, is exactly the displacement the Patras romance invites and Vitale relishes. Nothing is generic: the arcading of the monastery, the rope of the lost basket, the statuette on the fountain are described with a miniaturist's appetite.

Colour carries the story as much as line: the rose of the monastery and the warm gold above give way, below the rock, to the near-black green of the thicket, so that the passage from community to solitude is also a passage from light to night — a scheme the following panels keep.

The edges are ragged, the losses biting into both scenes and into the lower right corner, and the gold is rubbed. Its small scale suits its function: a wing for a cult image, made to be read close to, like the pages of the romance it translates.

### The Beggar before the King of Palestine; the Camels Arrive with Provisions

  <figcaption>Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot: a beggar urges the King of Palestine to send provisions to Anthony in the desert; the camels arrive, led by the wheel of fire, c. 1340–1345. Tempera on panel, 77 × 39 cm. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 10039, Room 1 (on deposit since 1937; ICCD cat. 00065212). From the same dismembered ensemble.</figcaption>

The second panel carries the provisioning of the desert. Its two episodes answer one another across the height of the field: at the top, in the king's house, the demand; below, in the wilderness, the delivery.

In the romance, Anthony and his companions hunger in the desert, and God sends a beggar to the court of the King of Palestine to require bread of him; the king loads his camels, and the beasts are led across the waste, without a driver, by a miraculous wheel of fire. It is a fairy-tale economy of cause and effect — a pauper's word moving a kingdom, a prodigy doing the work of a caravan master — and Vitale gives each link of the chain its own register of the panel.

The court occupies an open loggia of red walls and dark tiled roof, set against the gold like a piece of goldsmith's architecture, with a flowering tree standing on the burnished ground beside it. The king lies in bed beneath a coverlet striped in red and yellow — majesty surprised in its chamber — while the broad-hatted beggar stands at the bed's foot and urges his errand; a dark-skinned attendant in blue and white waits at the bedside. The scene is all interior intimacy, told through furnishings: the bed, the coverlet, the hat in the hand of the man who has walked from the desert.

Below, the wood takes over, the same stippled thicket as in the first panel, parted by a track of rose-coloured earth. Down it come the camels, two of them, laden with white packs and a pannier in which the round loaves can still be counted; their long necks and boxy saddles are observed with the relish of a painter to whom such beasts were travellers' tales made flesh.

At the centre of the wood hovers the prodigy: a ringed wheel sheathed in blue flame, hanging among the bushes like a second moon. It has been described as the "admirable blue gleam of the miraculous winged wheel", the chromatic climax of the whole series — a cold fire that lights the thicket from within. Two brown-robed companions lift their hands towards it, caught between alarm and recognition.

Anthony himself kneels at the lower right, grey-habited, gold rays scratched around him, his hands joined: the terminus of the chain, receiving as prayer what the panel above set in motion as command. The downward narrative of the field — court, caravan, kneeling hermit — runs with the gravity of the gift itself.

The panel is the most purely delightful of the four, and the one in which the romance's appetite for marvels and Vitale's appetite for description coincide most completely: the camels' long necks, the king's striped coverlet and the burning wheel are given the same attention.

Its condition matches its companions: ragged edges, losses along the margins, rubbed gold. It is the one wing in which the hermit of the desert is fed by a king, painted for viewers who would have known hunger and provisioning as civic facts.

### Saint Anthony Supports the Hanged Efron; the Men Killed by Wild Beasts Revived

  <img src="/Images/Vitale-da-Bologna/vitale-da-bologna-30.jpg" alt="Tall, narrow fragmentary panel by Vitale da Bologna: against a gold sky punched with arcs, a great dark tree spreads at the top, and from its branch hangs a red-haired youth in a white shirt, his eyes closed; beneath his feet stands the haloed Anthony in grey, upholding the body, while a man in a white cap climbs the trunk at the right to reach the rope and a small man in yellow with a blue cap hoists a ladder; at the left a crowd of kin — veiled women, bearded men in orange and red — raise their hands in supplication; behind them a walled pink city with its gate stands at the left and a pink chapel beside blue water at the right; in the lower zone Anthony appears again, bending deeply with scratched gold rays over a fallen man whose red-clad body lies at the corner; the panel's edges are ragged.">
  <figcaption>Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot: after his death Anthony supports the body of Efron, unjustly hanged; Anthony revives the men killed by wild beasts, c. 1340–1345. Tempera on panel, 78.5 × 38.5 cm. Bologna, Pinacoteca Nazionale, inv. 10041, Room 1 (on deposit since 1937; ICCD cat. 00065215). From the same dismembered ensemble.</figcaption>
</figure>

The third panel gathers the posthumous miracles. Its principal scene is the strangest of the series: a gallows with the saint beneath it. The episodes, again two, come from the close of the Patras romance — the innocent Efron, hanged unjustly, kept alive by the dead saint who upholds his body, and the men slain by wild beasts whom Anthony restores.

In the legend the miracles are the proof of the romance's claim: that the hermit's power did not die with him. Vitale takes the claim literally. Anthony, dead, is simply present — haloed, grey-habited, standing where he is needed — and the panel asks no questions about how; the faith of the text becomes the matter-of-factness of the image.

The gallows scene fills the upper two-thirds. From the branch of a great dark tree, spread against the gold sky, hangs Efron: a red-haired youth in a white shirt, eyes closed, the rope at his neck. Directly beneath him stands the saint, frontal and calm, taking the weight of the body so that the noose cannot do its work — the quietest figure in the most violent scene, the miracle depicted as nothing more than a man standing in the right place.

Around this still centre the living bustle. A man in a white cap swarms up the trunk at the right to reach the rope; a small man in yellow, blue-capped, hoists a ladder against the tree; at the left the youth's kin press forward — veiled women, bearded men in orange and red, a woman in rose-white — their raised hands making a hedge of supplication. Every figure is doing something particular, and the retrieval of a hanged man is observed with the exactness of a city that knew its gallows.

The setting is a landscape of unusual fullness for a Bolognese panel of the 1340s: at the left a walled pink city with its gate, an animal at a trough outside; at the right a pink chapel beside blue water; between them green bushes and the rising ground. The story is placed in a world with an inside and an outside, a town to be expelled from and a sanctuary to be reached.

In the lower corner the second miracle is compressed to two figures: Anthony again, bending so deeply that his grey habit becomes a single falling curve, over a man stretched at the panel's edge, his red-clad body mangled by the beasts; scratched rays of gold spring from the saint. The economy is deliberate — after the crowded gallows, a miracle in private.

The curve of the stooping saint, answered by the hanging vertical of Efron above, gives the panel its skeleton: two bodies given over, one upheld, one raised. It is the same instinct for the expressive posture that bends the Virgin over her knee in the Mezzaratta *Dream*, here turned to the mechanics of rescue.

The edges are ragged and the lower corner fragmentary. Within the ensemble this was the wing of proofs: the panel that showed the saint's clients — perhaps a confraternity with its own condemned to comfort — what his patronage was worth at the worst of moments.

The series closes with the funeral, and with the miracle worked at it: the healing of Sofia, daughter of the Emperor Constantine, possessed by the demon and freed at the saint's bier in the presence of Bishop Theophilus. The panel is the best documented of the four in its modern care, with restorations recorded in 1937, 1940, 1976 and 1986.

Nothing in the romance's text prepares for the design. The bier does not stand: it plunges. Laid on a cart with a great spoked wheel, draped in a pall of red and gold brocade, it tilts steeply down towards the lower left, and the pavement of black and white triangles rushes beneath it at the same raking angle — the "tall, hyper-Gothic space" of the critical description, a ceremony seen as if the ground itself were giving way under its solemnity.

On the bier lies the saint, in the black habit of the series, his flesh darkened — the desert written on the corpse — his feet towards the kneeling women, his head towards the gold. The brocade of the pall, patterned in interlace, is painted with the same appetite for precious textile as the cloths of honour of the Madonnas: death dressed in the best the painter owns.

At the right stands Theophilus, the officiating bishop of the legend, in a white cope over red, mitred, his hands joined; behind him the tonsured heads of his clergy make a quiet choir. Before him the drama: two richly dressed young women press towards the body, one in a patterned rose gown lunging to touch the pall, the other kneeling in a gown of dark netting. They are best read as Sofia twice over — possessed, and freed — unless the second is a companion; the burst of red fire beneath the cart, on this reading, is the demon itself quitting her, made visible as flame at the moment of the cure.

At the left the empire attends. The crowned Constantine, in red and blue worked with gold, is supported and steadied by his court — a man in a tall blue hat, a youth in a red-and-white cap — on a raised stand, with a pink balcony of onlookers above: rank arranged in tiers, the highest furthest from the miracle, which belongs to the sick girl and the dead man.

The lower zone is that slice of life. Through a black doorway at the panel's edge a crowd climbs up onto the chequered pavement — hooded men, a youth in yellow with red hose, a woman with loose red hair — latecomers to the ceremony, cut by the frame as if the page were turning while we look. Here more than anywhere the paint overruns the gilded bands: the funeral is not composed for us, it is happening, and we have arrived with the crowd.

The panel's edges are ragged and the margins eaten, but the principal group is well preserved, its reds and brocade gold still carrying across a room. As the last wing of the ensemble it did what funerals in painting are for: it showed the saint's death as the beginning of his working life, with an emperor's daughter healed at the bier.

## Disputed Attributions and Errors in Circulation

Vitale's reputation has attracted errors of two kinds: misplaced works and invented biography. They are reviewed here with the evidence that corrects them.

The date of birth comes first. No document gives it: the year 1309, repeated in many handbooks, is nothing more than a hardened form of the inference that a man first enrolled in the *venticinquine* in 1331 was born around 1310. The surname fares no better. The documents give *de Equis*, "degli Equi", and "de' Cavalli", found in older literature, is an Italian translation of the Latin name, not a separate form found in the records. Nor does any source give the cause of his death: he is last recorded alive on 8 December 1359 and was dead by 31 July 1361, and the statement that he died of plague in 1361 is a conjecture made plausible, but not proven, by the return of the epidemic in that year.

Other errors concern the works and their whereabouts. *Saint George and the Dragon* is sometimes described as a fresco cycle in the church of San Vitale; it is a panel, in the Pinacoteca Nazionale of Bologna (inv. 6394). The *Madonna dei Battuti* is sometimes placed in the Pinacoteca of Bologna; it comes from the Battuti Bianchi of Ferrara and is in the Pinacoteca Vaticana (inv. MV 40017). The *Madonna dei Denti* is said to have come from a parish church near Bologna and to have been commissioned by a family after a pilgrimage; it came from the oratory beside Sant'Apollonia at Mezzaratta, and no source records the patron. A crucifix painted for San Francesco in Bologna appears in some accounts, but no such document or work is known: his documented work for the church is the Odofredi chapel of 1330 and the guest quarters of 1340.

Beside these outright errors stand attributions that are still legitimately debated. The *Stories of Saint Maurelius* in Ferrara were attributed to Vitale on their discovery in 1949, and the attribution has been supported by later studies, but others judge the frescoes too damaged, or too weak, to sustain the name. At Udine the fragments of the high chapel are given to Vitale on style, and the lost scenes are reconstructed through the Spilimbergo copy, but the extent of his own hand there, against the Fabbri chapel where the workshop is more visible, is still argued.

Two further attributions have been rejected outright. In the older literature the *Triumph of Death* and related frescoes in the Camposanto of Pisa were at times given to Vitale; the attribution has been abandoned, and the frescoes are now given to [Buonamico Buffalmacco](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Buonamico-Buffalmacco.html). A panel that once bore a false signature of Vitale with the date 1320 was reattributed to Simone dei Crocifissi in 1935; the panel has not been verified against a published catalogue, but in any case a date of 1320 precedes Vitale's first documented activity by ten years, so the signature cannot be his.

## Patrons, Workshop and Legacy

The documents allow a fairly precise picture of who employed Vitale. It is a picture of religious houses, lay confraternities and bishops, not of the Commune or the great families.

Vitale's earliest documented work, from the Odofredi chapel of 1330 to the guest quarters of 1340, was all for San Francesco: for the first decade of his career the Franciscans were his steadiest employers. In Udine too his first recorded appearance, in June 1348, is as a witness in the Franciscan church.

Three of his principal works were made for confraternities. Mezzaratta was the seat of a lay devotional company. The *Madonna dei Battuti* was made for the flagellant confraternity of the Battuti Bianchi of Ferrara. The Udine payments of 1348–1349 came from the confraternity of the smiths, San Nicolò dei Fabbri. Confraternities paid for images that their members would see at every meeting, and the small kneeling members painted at the Virgin's feet in the Ferrara panel are a portrait of the patron as a group.

Bishops and monasteries complete the picture. The contract of 1343 was signed with Guido, bishop of Ferrara, for statues for his cathedral. At Pomposa the patron named in the apse is Abbot Andrea of Fano. The 1353 polyptych was commissioned by Raniero Ghisilieri for the canons of San Salvatore, an Augustinian community. In the last years the Servites appear: the 1359 deed was drawn up at their chapter, and the Magdalene frescoes in Santa Maria dei Servi are placed in this period.

The journey to Udine is the one episode that takes Vitale outside Emilia. Udine was the seat of the patriarchs of Aquileia. Whether Vitale was called there by the patriarch's circle, by the confraternity of the smiths, or by both, the documents do not say. The departure from Bologna falls between May 1347 and June 1348, the months of the arrival of the Black Death in northern Italy; the coincidence has been noted, but no document links the two.

What is not documented is just as telling. No document connects Vitale with the Commune of Bologna, with the Pepoli, who ruled the city from 1337 to 1350, or with the papal legates. Claims of civic or princely patronage in older accounts rest on nothing more than the assumption that a leading painter must have had such patrons.

Vitale is often called the founder of a Bolognese school. The phrase is fair if it means that the next generation of Bolognese painters started from his work. It is misleading if it suggests a documented line of pupils. Only one assistant is named in the documents.

That assistant is Balduccio di Francesco, whose contracts of 1353 and 1357 appear among the documents above. No work has been securely identified as his; the contract of 1353 is nonetheless the first proof that Vitale ran a workshop with assistants under contract, at the time of the San Salvatore polyptych.

The uneven quality of Pomposa, Udine and the later parts of Mezzaratta shows that Vitale worked with collaborators from the 1340s, and two names recur. The first is Simone di Filippo, called Simone dei Crocifissi, who worked at Mezzaratta and is the assistant proposed for the Edinburgh–Florence diptych. He became the leading Bolognese painter of the following generation; that he trained with Vitale is a reasonable inference from this collaboration, not a documented fact. The second is Andrea di Deolao de' Bruni, whom the *Dizionario Biografico* names as Vitale's principal follower. He is a different person from the painter and illuminator [Andrea de' Bartoli](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Andrea-de-Bartoli.html); the two are sometimes confused, and both are called "Andrea da Bologna" in the older literature.

Other painters of the next generation are linked to Vitale only by the general debt of Bolognese painting to his example: [Dalmasio degli Scannabecchi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Dalmasio-degli-Scannabecchi.html) and his son [Lippo di Dalmasio](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Lippo-di-Dalmasio-degli-Scannabecchi.html), though no document connects Dalmasio with Vitale's workshop; [Nicolò di Giacomo](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Nicolò-di-Giacomo.html), the city's leading illuminator in the second half of the century, whose figures are often described as Vitalesque; and Jacopo Avanzi and Jacopo di Paolo, who worked at Mezzaratta in a later phase and carried a "neo-Vitalesque" manner into the 1370s and beyond. The relationships among these painters are treated in their own entries.

The Udine stay left a stronger trace than any single pupil. Workshop and local followers continued Vitale's manner in Friuli: an *Annunciation* in Udine, a *Martyrdom of Thomas Becket* at Rualis near Cividale, a *Madonna of Humility* at Pordenone. The *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* judges this Vitalesque tradition strong enough to shape Friulian painting into the early fifteenth century.

Vitale's fame has had two lives: a local one, sustained by devotion to his images, and a critical one, created in the twentieth century.

Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>13</sup></a> mentions the paintings at Mezzaratta, and Carlo Cesare Malvasia, the historian of Bolognese painting, described the *Madonna dei Denti* in its oratory in the *Felsina pittrice* of 1678. The name "Vitale delle Madonne", used in older writing, reflects a reputation built on devotional images of the Virgin, several of which were venerated as miraculous. For Malvasia and his successors, writing in the age of the Carracci and Guido Reni, the fourteenth-century painters were venerable precursors rather than masters to be studied for their own sake.

The modern Vitale begins with Longhi. In his lectures of 1934–1935 he linked the Mezzaratta frescoes to the *Madonna dei Denti* and presented Vitale as a forerunner of the courtly painting of the late Gothic. In 1950 the exhibition of Bolognese fourteenth-century painting that he curated at the Pinacoteca made Vitale its centre and brought the Mezzaratta frescoes into the gallery. The rediscovery in 1935 of the *Last Supper* in San Francesco belongs to the same years.

Gnudi's monograph of 1962 established the catalogue and the chronology that most later work has refined rather than replaced. The detachment and reinstallation of Mezzaratta, the restoration of the San Salvatore polyptych in 1976–1979, and the catalogue of the Pinacoteca's fourteenth-century holdings in 1986 gave the works their present form.

Benati's contributions, from the *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* entry of 2000 to his studies of Vitale's career (2010) and of the programmes at Pomposa (2017), have defined the current view: a painter rooted in Rimini and in local expressionism, open to French Gothic models, whose late work moves towards a softer and more ornamental beauty. The publication of the archival sources, summarised in the *Dizionario Biografico* entry of 2020, has given his biography its documentary base.

Vitale is now one of the few Trecento painters outside Tuscany whom general histories of Italian art treat as a protagonist rather than a regional figure. The Mezzaratta room in the Pinacoteca, the *Saint George* and the *Madonna dei Denti* make Bologna the place to see him. The San Salvatore polyptych, still in a Bolognese church in its original frame, is the best surviving example of how one of his altarpieces looked as a whole.

## Conclusion

The Vitale who emerges from this survey is at once well documented and elusive. About twenty records follow him across three decades, from the payment for the Odofredi chapel in 1330 to the notice of his death in 1361, and a small group of works anchors his name to particular objects: the dated *Madonna dei Denti*, the monogrammed *Saint George*, the signed Vatican panel, the contracted polyptych of San Salvatore. Yet the documents name almost nothing that survives, and the surviving works name almost nothing that is documented. Between these two bodies of evidence the catalogue has to be built by inference, and the distinction maintained throughout — between what the sources establish, what a broad consensus infers and what remains open — is not a pedantic precaution but the condition of saying anything reliable about him.

Within those limits his artistic personality is unusually clear. Vitale is a painter of movement: the rearing horse and the twisting saint of the *Saint George*, the Child who stands and reaches on his mother's lap, the crowded and agitated narratives of Mezzaratta. His line is quick and elastic, his colour bright and decorative, his figures more often caught in action than composed in repose. Set beside the measured, volumetric painting of Giotto's Florentine followers, his work shows that the Trecento in Italy did not move along a single path, and that a Bolognese painter could draw on Rimini, on local expressive traditions and on French Gothic elegance to arrive at a language entirely his own.

He was also a painter of remarkable range. The documents and the works together show him painting chapels and guest quarters for the Franciscans, gilding and colouring wooden statues for a cathedral, decorating a confraternity's oratory and a monastic apse, delivering a polyptych to a contract and producing small devotional panels for private and collective prayer. The same hand that could fill the entrance wall of Mezzaratta with incident could concentrate the whole meaning of a Crucifixion in a single inclined head. Few of his contemporaries outside Florence and Siena have left evidence of such versatility across media and scales.

His patrons explain much of the character of his work. Religious houses, confraternities and bishops, not the Commune or the ruling families, gave him his commissions, and they wanted images that would be used: venerated at every meeting of a company, carried in devotion, prayed before in illness and grief. Several of his Madonnas acquired reputations as miraculous images, and some of his panels remained in use for centuries in the places for which they were made. Vitale's art was shaped by the needs of communities that lived with their images, and its emotional directness is inseparable from that function.

What remains of that art is a field of fragments. The Franciscan chapels are lost; Udine survives in pieces and in a later copy; Mezzaratta has been detached and reassembled in a museum room; the *Stories of Saint Anthony Abbot* are panels separated from a lost whole; the San Martino Crucifixion is reduced to the head of Christ framed by a later tomb. Only the San Salvatore polyptych, still in its church and its frame, shows how one of his ensembles was meant to be seen. Any account of Vitale's achievement must allow for the scale of these losses, and resist the temptation to fill them with confident reconstructions.

Several questions therefore remain open. The authenticity of the 1351 inscription at Pomposa, the share of Vitale's own hand at Udine and in the later parts of Mezzaratta, the attribution of the Ferrara *Stories of Saint Maurelius*, the chronology of the undated panels and the identity of patrons such as the kneeling cardinal of Viterbo are all still argued. So is the shape of his workshop: Balduccio di Francesco is the only assistant named in the documents, and the role of Simone dei Crocifissi and of other younger painters rests on stylistic inference.

The painter who branded his name on the flank of a horse knew his own worth, and history has largely confirmed his judgement. Vitale gave Bolognese painting a voice distinct from that of Tuscany, one that his followers carried through the second half of the century and that his Friulian successors kept alive into the fifteenth. Rediscovered in the twentieth century after long local veneration, he now stands among the few Trecento painters outside Tuscany who are treated as protagonists of Italian art. To look at his works in Bologna, Pomposa, Udine and the museums that hold his dispersed panels is to see a painter for whom devotion, narrative and invention were never separate, and whose energy still reaches the viewer across nearly seven centuries.

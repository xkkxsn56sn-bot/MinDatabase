## Introduction: A Book That Became an Image of Hildegard

Few medieval manuscripts have become as visually familiar to modern readers as Lucca, Biblioteca Statale, MS 1942, and few are so often misunderstood. Its ten full-page miniatures illustrate <a href="/Content/Artists/XII-c/Hildegard-von-Bingen.html">Hildegard of Bingen</a>'s *Liber divinorum operum*, the "Book of Divine Works", also transmitted under the title *De operatione Dei*. They have become the default iconography of Hildegard herself. The red-faced figure of divine Love with an old man's head rising from her crown, the human figure with arms outstretched inside the concentric rings of the cosmos, and the small figure of the visionary at her desk in the lower margin of each page all appear on book covers, in exhibitions and on websites with a frequency that has erased their historical distance from her. The images are commonly captioned as if Hildegard had made them. They were in fact produced decades after her death in 1179, in a workshop whose identity remains a matter of hypothesis.

This essay takes the codex as its protagonist. It places the manuscript within the genesis of the text it carries and the manuscript tradition to which that text belongs. It reviews what is documented, what is consensus and what is conjecture about its date, origin, physical make-up and path to Lucca. It then reads the ten miniatures one by one against the visions they introduce, before turning to the vexed question of authorship and to the codex's long afterlife. Throughout, the essay distinguishes between what the sources establish, what scholarship broadly agrees upon, what remains hypothesis and what belongs to popular legend. The account draws above all on the work of <a href="/scholars.html#derolez-albert">Albert Derolez</a> and <a href="/scholars.html#dronke-peter">Peter Dronke</a>, the editors of the text, on <a href="/scholars.html#caviness-madeline-h">Madeline Caviness</a>'s studies of Hildegard and the images, and on the 1973 Lucca monograph on the miniatures by <a href="/scholars.html#masetti-anna-rosa-calderoni">Anna Rosa Calderoni Masetti</a> and Gigetta Dalli Regoli.

## Hildegard and the Genesis of the *Liber divinorum operum*

The *Liber divinorum operum* is the third and last of Hildegard's great visionary works. It follows *Scivias*, composed between 1141 and about 1151, and the *Liber vitae meritorum*, composed between 1158 and 1163. The Library of Congress's catalogue record for the digitized Lucca codex gives the third work's chronology as begun in 1163–64 and finished in 1172–74, which agrees with the standard modern dating. Hildegard supplies the starting point herself. In the prologue she dates the onset of the visions underlying the book to the reign of Frederick I, explicitly to the year 1163 and her own sixty-fifth year, and she describes the labour of writing as lasting seven years. An autobiographical passage preserved in the *Vita sanctae Hildegardis* (II.16) relates the conception of the work to a visionary meditation on the Prologue of the Gospel of John: the Word without beginning who called all creatures into being. This Johannine core explains the book's title. The "work of God" (*opus Dei*) is creation as a whole and, above all, the human being, who is both the work of God and, in Hildegard's phrase, the worker of the Divinity and a shadow of its mysteries.

The work is organized in three parts comprising ten visions. Part I contains four visions; Part II a single, very long vision; Part III five visions. The first part moves from a theophany of divine Love (*Caritas*) to the structure of the cosmos, the winds and the humours, and the correspondences between the firmament and the human body and soul. It closes with an extended exegesis of John 1:1–14. The single vision of Part II treats the earth divided into five parts, the places of purgation and punishment, and a commentary on the six days of creation in Genesis 1, read literally, allegorically and tropologically. Hildegard is often described as the only premodern woman known to have written full commentaries on both the Johannine Prologue and the hexaemeron, and the 2018 English translation presents her that way. Part III unfolds salvation history around the image of a city: the city of God and the mirror of the angels, the city across the ages from creation to the Incarnation, the fountain of God's work with *Caritas*, Humility and Peace, Wisdom and the ancient counsel, and finally divine Love enthroned upon a wheel of eternity and history, leading to the Antichrist and the end of time.

The theological programme is announced in the first vision. A figure of surpassing beauty, identified as divine Love, speaks as the "fiery life of God's substance", kindling every spark of life in the fields, the waters and the stars. The second vision then makes a revision that Hildegard draws attention to herself. The chapter summaries of I.2 explain that in *Scivias* (I.3) the world-sphere was shown in the figure of an egg, whereas here it appears in the likeness of a wheel. The wheel, they add, signifies divinity, "complete and whole, without beginning or end", circumscribed by neither space nor time. Within this wheel, which rests upon the breast of the figure from the first vision, stands a human figure whose crown, feet and outstretched hands touch the circle of strong, bright white air. This is the textual basis of the famous "universal man": a human microcosm set at the centre of the macrocosm, upon whom the winds, represented by animal heads, and the rays of the seven planets act. The doctrine of correspondence is worked out in exhaustive anatomical detail in I.4. The measurements of the head, limbs and trunk are mapped onto the thicknesses of the cosmic circles, the winds and the seasons. The chapter summaries assign these meanings to body, cosmos and soul together, so that the macrocosm–microcosm scheme is always also a moral and soteriological scheme.

The circumstances of composition at the Rupertsberg are reasonably well documented. Hildegard's long-time collaborator, the monk Volmar of Disibodenberg, who served as provost and secretary of her community, died in 1173. With the text not yet complete, Hildegard turned to Abbot Ludwig of St Eucharius in Trier, who with other learned helpers took part in the final redaction. An epilogue preserved in the great Rupertsberg collected-works manuscript, the Riesencodex, records this collaboration. Wibert (Guibert) of Gembloux, her last secretary, belongs to the final years of her life and to the posthumous effort to assemble and polish her corpus rather than to the drafting of the *Liber divinorum operum* itself. Each of the work's 316 chapters is preceded by a summary (*capitula*). These summaries were composed separately, in the decade after completion, by one of Hildegard's close aides. In the oldest manuscript they survive as a block inserted at the front; in later witnesses they were distributed through the text. They are thus an early reader's guide produced in her circle, and, as will be seen, the Lucca codex inherits the rubrication they made possible.

## The Textual Transmission and the Place of the Lucca Codex

The textual history of the *Liber divinorum operum* is unusually transparent compared with that of *Scivias*, largely because of one manuscript: Ghent, Universiteitsbibliotheek, MS 241. Codicological analysis by Albert Derolez, first published in an essay on the genesis of the work and then deepened in the introduction to the critical edition, has shown that this codex is the earliest surviving copy. It was written at the Rupertsberg around 1170–1174 and later belonged to St Eucharius in Trier, where it bore the title *De operacione Dei*. The Ghent manuscript preserves the text in two layers: a first copy, which Derolez argued was taken more or less directly from the wax tablets on which the text was drafted, and a dense layer of corrections by several hands. In the edition's account the Ghent book was sent to Abbot Ludwig, corrected, and returned. Peter Dronke identified one of Hildegard's letters (Letter 217 in the standard numbering) as the cover letter that accompanied the codex. The Ghent manuscript is therefore as close to an "autograph" as the tradition allows. It is not written in Hildegard's own hand. It is a working copy produced under her supervision, in which the passage from dictated draft to polished Latin can be followed correction by correction.

The critical edition by Albert Derolez and Peter Dronke, *Hildegardis Bingensis Liber divinorum operum* (Corpus Christianorum Continuatio Mediaevalis 92, Turnhout: Brepols, 1996), prints the Ghent text in its final corrected form. It supplies an apparatus criticus of variants from the other early manuscripts, a pioneering apparatus correctionum recording every intervention in the Ghent codex with its extent, technique and corrector, and an apparatus fontium compiled by Dronke, who also wrote the introductory studies on the work's place in Hildegard's oeuvre, its originality and its structure. Derolez was responsible for the codicological analysis of the witnesses and the editorial method. For anyone working on the Lucca codex, his description of the manuscripts in the introduction to CCCM 92 is the indispensable starting point.

Beside Ghent, the edition treats three other early witnesses. The first is the Riesencodex (Wiesbaden, Hochschul- und Landesbibliothek RheinMain, Hs. 2), the monumental Rupertsberg collection of Hildegard's works, generally dated to around 1180–1190. It contains the *Liber divinorum operum* on fols. 202r–308r, together with *Scivias*, the *Liber vitae meritorum*, letters, the *Vita* and the songs. The second is Troyes, Bibliothèque municipale (now Médiathèque Jacques-Chirac), MS 683. In Derolez's description this is a parchment codex of 117 folios measuring 310 × 210 mm, whose section containing the *Liber divinorum operum* was copied at the Rupertsberg late in the twelfth century and later reached Clairvaux by a route that remains unknown. Derolez judged the Riesencodex text older in script and textual features than that of Troyes. The third early witness is Lucca. The tradition also includes later copies: a fifteenth-century manuscript in the British Library (Add. MS 15418), a Trier manuscript of 1487 from the Beatusberg near Koblenz (Stadtbibliothek 722/277) that is a copy of the Ghent codex, and a fragment in Frankfurt. Published counts therefore vary with what is being counted. The reference repertory of German historical sources speaks of four complete early manuscripts, while other surveys count six complete manuscripts and a fragment. The statement repeated in Italian popular writing and in the World Digital Library description, that "only three" codices of the work survive, should not be repeated.

Where does Lucca stand? The edition's sigla designate it L. Derolez characterizes it as a large parchment codex of 164 written folios that "stands apart as the only LDO codex containing noticeable variants, along with the numerous inversions" of word order he documents. The Lucca text is therefore not simply a faithful copy of the corrected Ghent text or of the Riesencodex. It is a witness with a distinctive textual profile, of no value for establishing Hildegard's text and of great value for showing how her text was handled two generations later. Its precise stemmatic relationship to its exemplar is not discussed here. It is clear, however, that L is the latest of the four early witnesses and that it is the only one with an illustrative programme.

Lucca also played an outsized role in the printed history of the text. Giovanni Domenico Mansi (1692–1769), the Lucchese historian and later archbishop, published the editio princeps in 1761 in his re-edition of Étienne Baluze's *Miscellanea* (vol. II, pp. 336–452), using the Lucca manuscript. Jacques-Paul Migne reprinted Mansi's text in *Patrologia Latina* 197 (1855), cols. 741–1038. The continuous numbering of the visions from I to X, which became standard through the Patrologia, derives from the Lucca codex. For almost a century and a half the work was read in a text based on its most idiosyncratic early witness. That is a further reason why the 1996 edition, grounded in Ghent, marked a turning point.

## The Codex Itself: Date, Origin, Make-up, Provenance

**Dating.** MS 1942 carries no colophon or date, so every dating rests on script and style. The published range is wide. Mansi, who held the book in his hands, called it a codex "saeculi XII vel saltem ineuntis XIII", of the twelfth or at least the beginning of the thirteenth century. The 1973 Lucca monograph on the manuscript by Calderoni Masetti and Dalli Regoli placed it in the first half of the thirteenth century, more precisely in the second or third decade, and this is the dating adopted by the Library of Congress record ("between the second and third decades of the 13th century", catalogued as 1210–1230). The Italian *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* (1996) narrows it to the third decade. German scholarship and the Abbey of St. Hildegard at Eibingen usually give "um 1220/30". The Hamburg Centre for the Study of Manuscript Cultures says "first half of the 13th century", and a 2008 seminar survey reports that earlier literature spoke of the beginning or first half of the century while newer literature names 1220–1230. A 2021 illustrated volume describes the miniatures as produced "about 20 years after Hildegard's death", which would put them close to 1200. The defensible statement is that the codex belongs to the early thirteenth century and most probably to the 1220s, with c. 1210–1230 as the cautious bracket. Neither a twelfth-century date nor a late-thirteenth-century date has scholarly support.

**Origin.** What is known is negative as well as positive. No document records where the codex was made. Art-historical consensus places the miniatures in the Middle Rhine region, and the enduring hypothesis connects the book with the Rupertsberg. The Hamburg description gives "Middle Rhine, Rupertsberg (?)". The Eibingen abbey speaks of the scriptorium of a monastery "connected with the Rupertsberg". The Italian encyclopedia says "probably" a Rhenish scriptorium. A popular image database attributes the work to a "German miniaturist active around 1230 in Mainz", a precision that the sources consulted here do not support. A 1976/77 article in the *Mainzer Zeitschrift* devoted to the "Gothic miniatures" of the Lucca manuscript is the principal art-historical study in German and probably the source of the "um 1220/30" dating. A 1998 Bern dissertation comparing the lost Rupertsberg *Scivias* with the Lucca codex concluded, on stylistic grounds, for the first half of the thirteenth century on the Middle Rhine, and argued that several masters with differing styles were at work. An older palaeographical study of the authenticity of Hildegard's writings (1956) is reported to have identified a copyist of the Lucca codex with a scribe who copied a collection of Hildegard's letters in the early thirteenth century, possibly at the Rupertsberg. The identification is known only at second hand. A recent handbook likewise mentions a fragment written by the same scribe as the Lucca codex, which, if confirmed, would anchor the manuscript in a Rhenish, Hildegard-centred milieu. The Rupertsberg origin is therefore a respectable hypothesis, strengthened by these scribal links and by the codex's Hildegardian self-presentation. It has not been demonstrated.

**Make-up.** The physical data published by the holding library, the Library of Congress and the edition largely agree. The codex is of parchment and comprises 167 leaves measuring c. 39 × 25.5 cm; the edition speaks of 164 written folios. A summary of the 1998 dissertation reports a structure of twenty quaternions plus seven leaves and a front leaf, with the explicit on fol. 164v and the last three leaves blank. The text is in a Gothic book hand, in two columns of 38 lines, ruled in hard point. Titles, the summaries (*capitula*), and the incipit and explicit of each vision are in red. Sections begin with red and blue initials with frames, and there are illuminated initials. The opening of the prologue and of each vision is marked by an ornate initial with gold leaf. Leaves are numbered within each vision in red and blue Roman numerals in the side margins. There is also a double foliation, ancient and modern, which coincides up to fol. 108. Marginal notes in various hands, dated by the Hamburg description to the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, were partly cut away when the leaves were trimmed, evidence that the book was read for a long time after it was made. Sources disagree about the binding: the Hamburg description calls it "contemporary", while an Italian account states that the binding is modern. The codex underwent conservation treatment in 1936. No technical analysis of its pigments, gold or binding structure appears to have been published, and any statement about the chemistry of its colours would be speculation.

**Provenance.** The documented facts are few and solid. The first leaf carries a manuscript ownership note and stamp of the library of the convent of S. Maria Corteorlandini in Lucca, which belonged to the Clerics Regular of the Mother of God. When that convent was suppressed, the state library moved into its buildings in 1877 and absorbed the order's original book collection, which the Italian Ministry of Culture's library directory puts at "circa 13.000 volumi", and MS 1942 came with it. The Mansi connection is equally well documented, but its meaning is debated. In the preface to his 1761 edition Mansi wrote that he had found the work "in vetusto codice bibliothecae meae eleganti et auro ac coloribus exornato", that is, in an old codex of "my library", elegant and adorned with gold and colours. In the introduction to CCCM 92 Derolez takes this as evidence that the codex belonged to Mansi before it entered the Corteorlandini library. He then lists three possibilities without resolving them: the book could have been in the Mansi family for generations, a family he describes as originating from Mainz and hence from the neighbourhood of the Rupertsberg; Mansi could have acquired it on his scholarly travels; or it could already have belonged to the Corteorlandini convent, which Mansi had entered at sixteen and could therefore call "my library". The 1973 Lucca monograph emphasizes that Mansi contributed decisively to forming the Corteorlandini collection. The careful formulation is therefore this: Mansi certainly had the codex at his disposal and edited it in 1761; whether it was his private property or the convent's, and when and how it crossed the Alps, are not known. A claim in a recent handbook that it reached Lucca "possibly as early as the twelfth century" is unsupported and chronologically impossible for a thirteenth-century book.

**Digitization.** The codex was digitized for the Italian national portal Internet Culturale and contributed to the World Digital Library, where it was item 21658. Since the closure of the WDL it has been hosted by the Library of Congress (LCCN 2021668244), with a IIIF manifest, as a 353-image sequence that includes bindings and flyleaves. Because image numbers in that viewer do not equal folio numbers, citations should always use folios, not image numbers.

## The Ten Miniatures: Vision by Vision

The ten full-page miniatures stand at fols. 1v (I.1), 9r (I.2), 28v (I.3), 38r (I.4), 88v (II.1), 118r (III.1), 121v (III.2), 132r (III.3), 135r (III.4) and 143r (III.5). Each miniature stands at the threshold of the vision it illustrates, so that the reader meets the image before the description and exegesis delivered by the "voice from heaven". Scholarship agrees that the images are not exhaustive translations of the text. The 1973 Lucca monograph called them *condensations*, selecting the most intense moments of each vision, and stressed that they are built on combinations of elementary geometric figures. The descriptions below follow the vision texts as summarized in the *capitula* and the published descriptions, checked against the digitized pages. All images are reproduced from the digitization published through the World Digital Library and the Library of Congress.

In the text, Hildegard sees in the midst of the southern air a figure of wondrous beauty in human form. Its face is of such brightness that one could more easily gaze at the sun. Above its head is a second, aged face; it is winged and robed. In its hands it holds a lamb, and it treads underfoot a monster and a serpent. The miniature renders this with a red face, red being associated in the period both with divine love and with fire, and with the old man's countenance rising from the crown of the head. The figure tramples the monster, which the commentary interprets as discord, and the serpent, the ancient enemy. The lower register contains the most quoted vignette in the manuscript. At the right of a slender column Hildegard sits holding a pair of wax tablets and a stylus, and a red stream pours down upon her head from the frame of the miniature above, the visual sign of inspiration. At the left of the column the monk Volmar sits writing at a desk; behind Hildegard stands a nun, the second witness named in the prologue. The prologue does not give her name. Captions identifying her as Richardis of Stade, which are repeated in widely used image databases, are anachronistic, since Richardis died in 1151, long before the work was begun.

The composition of the page is unique in the cycle: the theophany stands in a tall, narrow field, and the panel with the visionary and her helpers is attached below it like a predella, slightly off-centre, so that the stream of red light can run from the upper frame straight onto Hildegard's head. The figure of Love, with her two pairs of wings sweeping upward and then falling to the knees, fills the gold field from top to bottom, and the eagle and human heads at the ends of the upper wings frame her own doubled head.

This is the image usually called "universal man", and the one most often compared with Leonardo's Vitruvian Man, a comparison that belongs to modern reception rather than to its medieval meaning. The text describes a wheel upon the breast of the figure of Love. The wheel has six circles: bright fire, black fire, pure ether, watery air, strong white air and thin air, with the globe of the earth at the centre. A human figure stands in the wheel, touching its circles with crown, feet and hands. Four principal winds appear as heads of animals: a leopard above the figure's head (east), a wolf below its feet (west), a lion at the right (south) and a bear at the left (north). Each emits collateral winds in the form of crab, stag, serpent and lamb heads. Seven celestial bodies are set in the upper circles, with rays reaching parts of the image, and luminous threads issue from the mouth of the figure who holds the wheel. The miniature follows medieval convention in placing east at the top and is remarkably accurate in scale. As the English translator has shown, the diameter of the central globe is one-fifth of the whole instrument, exactly as the ratios in the text require. Close comparison with the Latin nevertheless reveals departures. Peter Dronke observed in the edition's introduction that the only line in the miniature corresponding to the line Hildegard describes "from the beginning of the eastern part of that wheel to the end of its western part" is a red horizontal line running from the bear's head to the lion's head, that is, north to south. The translator has further argued that the miniaturist reversed the positions of the crab and stag heads flanking the leopard and the wolf, and that the painter drew a ray from the sun to the bear's head, although the text says expressly that the sun's rays touch the leopard, lion and wolf "but not the bear". These are small errors, but they matter for the authorship question.

On the page the figure of Love is reduced to a head, a pair of arms and a pair of feet, so that the wheel seems to be her body; the head of the old man rests on her own like a crown. The bands of the wheel are painted with great care, the blue zone of watery air with regular wavy lines and the white zone of luminous air with fine radiating strokes, and the golden lines of the planetary rays cross the whole construction. Hildegard, at the lower left, is enclosed in a small tower-like niche and writes, unaware, as it were, of the immense structure above her.

The third vision returns to the winds and to the upper circle whose emissions temper the courses of the planets. It shows how the humours within the human being change with the qualities of the winds and air that surround them, and how the veins and organs of the body work together or fall out of balance. The miniature again sets a human figure within a network of animal-headed winds and their breaths. The English translator's notes record that here, too, the Lucca painter erred: he reversed south and north, so that wolf and bear stand at the figure's right and left hands, and he read right and left from the perspective of the animal head rather than of the viewer. The same notes observe that the four "tongues" of breath in the miniature correspond to the ordinal directions formed by the confluence of the collateral winds, which is a correct and intelligent reading of a difficult passage.

Without the figure of Love the wheel stands free on the gold ground and becomes a pure diagram. The painter repeats the structure of the second vision but changes its emphasis: the human figure is smaller, the cloud-creatures in the white zone more numerous and agitated, and the breaths of the animal heads, painted as fine white streams, converge on the figure from every side, so that the image shows not the order of the cosmos but its constant action on the body.

The fourth and longest vision of Part I lays out the thickness of the firmament and all that is attached to it, the harmful emissions of the upper circles and their tempering by others, the "milky" cloud curved like a bow, and the exhaustive correspondences of head, limbs and organs with the cosmos, the months and the seasons. It closes with the exegesis of the Johannine Prologue. Published descriptions of the miniature emphasize that it gives prominence to the earth and to the influence of the upper elements on it. At the centre of the wheel the earth is a small globe divided into four quarters of different colours, and around it, set radially so that the book must be turned to read them, the seasons and human labour on the land are represented by trees in leaf, in flower, in fruit and bare, and by small figures digging with a spade, reaping with a sickle, beating with a pole, sitting and lying on the ground. Animal-headed winds and their breaths underline the interdependence of cosmic and terrestrial events, and red drops of fire and a yellowish, smoky vapour descend into the zone of air near some of the figures, illustrating the text's teaching that disorder in the elements strikes body and soul when the soul is out of harmony. This is therefore the miniature that carries the agricultural and seasonal imagery sometimes attributed in popular accounts to Part II.

The radial arrangement is the painter's most original solution in the cycle. Each figure stands with its feet on the globe and its head towards the circles of air, so that the scenes must be read by turning the book, as one would turn a wheel; the quartering of the globe and the alternation of bare and fruit-bearing trees make the passage of the seasons visible at a glance. Below the wheel a hand reaches into the gold field holding a long white scroll, a device that recurs in other miniatures of the cycle as the sign of the voice that explains the vision.

### The Portrait of Hildegard

Every one of the ten miniatures includes a small figure of Hildegard, as the digitized pages show; the figure of "eight" portraits given in one source is mistaken. In I.1 she appears in the lower panel with her secretary and a nun; in the other nine she sits alone at a lectern, in a small arched niche inside the frame or in a separate panel below it, with an open book or writing tablets. In the words of the major Italian account, she is "always caught seated, intent on her work, looking upward and always with the instruments of writing in her hands, or leaning but nevertheless just being used or about to be used". The function of the motif, however, is clear. In the Hamburg description, Hildegard presented herself not as an author of doctrine, which a woman of her time could not claim, but as a prophetic intermediary, and the repeated portrait makes the reader a witness to her witnessing. Inspiration is visualized at the beginning of the cycle and then re-asserted at every threshold. The visionary authority of the text is built into the layout of the book.

## Style, Colour and Gold

The cycle's style is described consistently in the literature, though not in great technical depth. The Italian encyclopedia characterizes the Lucca illustrations as markedly didactic. They are structured on geometric grids, they tend to "rationalize and arrest" Hildegard's mobile and tumultuous visions, and they draw on established figural modules such as the *homo ad circulum* and the *urbs quadrata*. The same entry contrasts this with the lost Rupertsberg *Scivias*, whose images, it says, more faithfully mirrored the author-redactor's visionary psychology and lingered on the supernatural and fantastic. Common to both, according to this account, are the mirror-like surfaces of gold grounds, dark contours defining solemn and hieratic figures, and a classicizing decorative repertory. These features recall the great tradition of Ottonian illumination, which remained a living reference in the Rhineland around 1200. The Lucca codex thus sits at the threshold between late Romanesque and early Gothic, and the German literature's habit of calling its miniatures "gothic" reflects that position. Whether specifically "byzantinizing" elements, of the kind common in German painting of the early thirteenth century, can be identified in the Lucca cycle remains an open question.

On colour and gold, the Hamburg description emphasizes the "luminous gold background typical of all miniatures". Depending on the light, it can shimmer so intensely that the figures are barely legible, an effect it compares with Hildegard's own language of the "living light". Red carries a theological charge: the face of *Caritas* in I.1 and the stream of inspiration pouring onto Hildegard are both red, and the circle of fire bounds the cosmos in I.2. No analytical study of the pigments, of the gold technique (leaf over bole, shell gold, or both) or of the underdrawing appears to have been published. Statements about the materials of the miniatures should therefore be limited to what can be seen.

## Text and Image: Visual Exegesis and the Question of Authorship

How the miniatures relate to the text has been the central question in the study of the Lucca codex. The question has been framed by a debate about the lost Rupertsberg *Scivias* (see the page on <a href="/Content/Codex/Scivias-of-Hildegard-of-Bingen.html">the Scivias of Hildegard of Bingen</a>). That manuscript, formerly Wiesbaden, Hessische Landesbibliothek, Hs. 1, with thirty-five miniatures, was made at the Rupertsberg during Hildegard's lifetime (datings range from the 1160s to around 1175–1180). It was evacuated to Dresden for safety during the Second World War and disappeared in 1945. Between 1927 and 1933 the nuns of Eibingen produced a hand-painted facsimile on parchment, and black-and-white photographs were also taken. Every reproduction of the Rupertsberg *Scivias* images in circulation today derives from these surrogates.

For the Rupertsberg *Scivias*, a strong case has been made that Hildegard designed the images. Madeline Caviness's essay "Hildegard as Designer of the Illustrations to Her Works" (1998) argued that Hildegard was the probable designer of the Rupertsberg *Scivias* cycle. It extended the argument to propose that the Lucca miniatures could plausibly rest on designs going back to her, perhaps through a lost Rupertsberg model. Caviness's position has been useful as a corrective, insisting that text and image be read together and that Hildegard belongs in the history of art. Yet even a sympathetic study, the article "Vision Two of Hildegard of Bingen's Book of Divine Works: A Medieval Map for a Cosmic Journey", calls these "formidable though still speculative arguments", valuable chiefly "as a corrective to any views that would completely dismiss consideration of Hildegard's direct influence on the illustrations"; for the Lucca codex the evidence points the other way.

Three kinds of evidence weigh against an authorial model for Lucca. The first is textual. In the critical edition, Derolez treated certain features of L as "an argument against the idea that L has been copied from an illustrated copy supposedly made during Hildegard's lifetime". Dronke's observation on the misplaced east–west line in I.2 belongs to the same pattern. The second is iconographic. The 1998 Bern dissertation, the only monograph dedicated to comparing the two cycles, concluded that the Lucca miniatures aim at an optimal expression of the vision text, which explains their schematic and static compositions. It offered what one reviewer called convincing arguments against both a lifetime model and Hildegard's artistic authorship. The Eibingen abbey states plainly that the painters "had only Hildegard's texts as a model". The third is the pattern of errors already described: reversed collateral winds, misplaced cardinal animals, a solar ray to the bear's head that the text explicitly excludes. Painters copying a model supervised by Hildegard would not be expected to diverge from her text in this way, whereas painters building diagrams from a difficult Latin description would be. An error could in principle have been inherited from a flawed model, so the argument is cumulative, not conclusive. It is nevertheless telling that the chapter "Picturing Hildegard of Bingen's Sight: Illuminating Her Visions" in *The Cambridge Companion to Hildegard of Bingen* describes the Lucca cycle as designed "by a later generation of her monastery's nuns" and states that its "famous cosmological diagram diverges from the text because the designer did not understand its meaning".

The scholarly position can therefore be stated with confidence. It is documented that Hildegard died in 1179 and that the Lucca miniatures were painted in the early thirteenth century. It is impossible that she painted them. That she designed them cannot be proven; the hypothesis of a lost Rupertsberg model is defensible for the *Scivias* tradition but currently unsupported for the *Liber divinorum operum*. The weight of opinion holds that the cycle was conceived after her death by readers who worked from the text, probably in a milieu loyal to her memory. The common practice of captioning the images "by Hildegard von Bingen, 1230", found on popular art websites and in image banks, is an error. So is the conflation of the Lucca codex with the "Rupertsberg codex", and so is the idea that the Eibingen copy reproduces Lucca rather than the lost Wiesbaden *Scivias*.

Read in their own terms, the miniatures are a remarkable act of visual exegesis. Part I's diagrams make spatial the numerical and proportional statements of the text: the thicknesses of the circles, the placement of planets, the twelve winds. They make the macrocosm–microcosm correspondence legible at a glance, which is surely what readers needed from a text whose own descriptions, as anyone attempting to follow I.2 discovers, almost demand to be drawn. Part III's architectural frame turns history into a building, so that the ages of the world become positions along the walls of a city. The recurring *Caritas* frames the whole book in divine Love, at its opening and in its final wheel. The marginal Hildegard, finally, repeats the prologue's claim that what the reader sees was given to her. The 1973 monograph described the result as a kind of "machine", with slow, continuous movements, apparitions and gradual transitions from darkness to light. The images do not illustrate Hildegard's visions in the modern sense of reproducing them. They interpret them, choosing, ordering and fixing a visionary text for study. An Italian account adds the reasonable inference that a book of this size and finish was meant not for private devotion but for study, and the trimmed marginalia of later readers bear that out.

## Purpose and Reception: From the Canonization Inquiry to the Twenty-First Century

Why should a large, gold-illuminated copy of the *Liber divinorum operum* have been made two generations after the author's death? The most frequently proposed context is the effort to have Hildegard canonized. The chronology is well documented. In 1226 or 1227 the abbess and convent of the Rupertsberg petitioned for canonization. Pope Gregory IX responded with the rescript *Mirabilis Deus*, appointing commissioners from the Mainz chapter. Their protocol of witness testimony (the *Acta inquisitionis de virtutibus et miraculis s. Hildegardis*) was completed in December 1233. The reference repertory dates it 16 December; the Eibingen abbey's chronology gives 6 December as the date of sealing at the Rupertsberg. The protocol was carried to Rome by Bruno, custos of St Peter's in Strasbourg, together with a copy of the *Vita*, Hildegard's writings and an opinion of Paris theologians. Finding the dossier deficient in form, Gregory IX appointed a new commission on 6 May 1237 (*Supplicantibus nobis*). Innocent IV pressed the matter again in 1243, and the revised protocol with additions of that year survives with the 1233 text in the Landeshauptarchiv at Koblenz. The dossier was put before John XXII around 1317, and the Abbey of St. Hildegard's chronology records that he granted the Rupertsberg indulgences for "the Feast Day of Saint Hildegard", "the first papal document, which officially refers to Hildegard as a saint" (dated variously to 1324 or 1326). The abbot and humanist Johannes Trithemius later reported: "Having carefully read over the testimonies submitted to him, [John XXII] made no difficulty about canonizing the virgin, as I am assured by the apostolic writing, although this event, longed for by many, was not brought to pass." Regional veneration continued regardless.

The Hamburg description suggests that the Lucca codex was "likely to have been produced in the efforts to have Hildegard proclaimed a saint", while candidly adding that it is "impossible to say" whether it was among the writings sent to Rome. A facsimile publisher's brochure similarly states that the book was "probably made on the Middle Rhine in connection with the efforts for Hildegard's canonization". The hypothesis fits the codex's date, its scale and its insistent portraiture of the visionary as divinely inspired. It has no documentary support. It should be presented as an attractive conjecture, and it should not be confused with the documented despatch in 1233 of unspecified "writings".

The codex re-enters history in eighteenth-century Lucca, where Mansi's 1761 edition made it, unknowingly, the basis of the work's printed text for over two centuries. Its modern fame as a visual object began in the twentieth century. The 1973 monograph published in Lucca reproduced the cycle in colour. The German translations issued by the Abbey of St. Hildegard with the Beuroner Kunstverlag reproduced the miniatures with every vision, and the abbey's own editions popularized the "um 1220/30" dating. A 2021 Skira volume, published in Milan in association with the Fondazione Cassa di Risparmio di Lucca and in English in 2022, accompanied an exhibition held at Susa and Turin in 2021–2022. It reproduces the ten images at large scale with a symbolic key and opens with an essay on Incarnation and humanity in the *Liber divinorum operum*. The full digitization at the World Digital Library and the Library of Congress, together with the online table of chapter summaries published by the International Society of Hildegard von Bingen Studies with the Lucca images under ministerial licence, has made the cycle available to everyone.

The canonical events of 2012 amplified this fame. On 10 May 2012 Benedict XVI extended Hildegard's liturgical cult to the universal Church by equivalent canonization. On 7 October 2012, at the opening of the Synod of Bishops, he issued an Apostolic Letter proclaiming her a Doctor of the Universal Church, the fourth woman so honoured after Catherine of Siena, Teresa of Ávila and Thérèse of Lisieux. The Hamburg description reads the codex itself as an instrument of that long process: a manuscript that "makes the reader a witness to the visions", conceived, perhaps, for a canonization that took eight centuries. Popular reception has been less disciplined. The "universal man" of fol. 9r is routinely presented as a forerunner of Leonardo's Vitruvian Man, a comparison worth making historically, since both draw on the antique *homo ad circulum*, but meaningless as genealogy. New Age literature has turned *Caritas* and the cosmic wheel into emblems of a holistic spirituality detached from their Christological and eschatological text. None of this need be repeated, but it is worth naming, because the codex's very fame is the main obstacle to seeing it.

## Conclusion

Lucca, MS 1942 is not Hildegard's book in the sense in which the Ghent manuscript is. It is a monument of the generation that remembered her, made in the early thirteenth century, probably in the Middle Rhine region and plausibly in the Rupertsberg's orbit, with a text that departs noticeably from the authorial tradition. Its ten miniatures are the only medieval visualization of the *Liber divinorum operum*. Their value lies precisely in being an interpretation: a didactic, geometrical translation of a dazzlingly difficult text, framed by the repeated image of Hildegard receiving it. To read them well is to read them with the Latin open beside them, attentive to where they condense, where they rationalize and where they err. To present them honestly is to stop calling them Hildegard's paintings and to start calling them what they are: the earliest surviving reading of her cosmos.

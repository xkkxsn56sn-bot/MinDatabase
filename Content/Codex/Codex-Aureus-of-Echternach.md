## Introduction and scope

The Codex Aureus of Echternach — in Latin the *Codex Aureus Epternacensis*, in German *Das Goldene Evangelienbuch von Echternach* — is one of the supreme achievements of early medieval book art and among the treasures of the Germanisches Nationalmuseum (GNM) in Nuremberg, where the manuscript block is catalogued as Hs. 156142 and the jewelled front cover, stored and conserved separately, as KG 1138. It is a Gospel book written entirely in gold on calfskin parchment, produced in the Benedictine abbey of Echternach (in present-day Luxembourg) in the second quarter of the eleventh century, and bound in a treasure cover of gold, ivory, cloisonné enamel, gems and pearls that was made roughly half a century earlier in Trier.

This essay surveys the manuscript comprehensively: its codicology and physical description; its treasure binding and the debates surrounding it; its date, place and patronage; the Echternach scriptorium and its Insular ancestry; the iconographic programme; the manuscript's art-historical significance; its provenance and modern history; the state of digitization and facsimile publication; and the historiographical debates that continue to attend it. Throughout, where sources disagree — over dating, over folio and image counts, over the ivory, over provenance details — the disagreement is reported rather than smoothed over, and points that cannot be verified against institutional catalogues are flagged as such.

One correction must be made at the outset, because it bears directly on how the manuscript is described. It is sometimes stated — and the framing of some popular accounts encourages the error — that the Codex Aureus of Echternach was presented by Emperor Henry III<a href="../../../endnotes.html#fn-henry-iii" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> to his collegiate foundation of Saints Simon and Jude at Goslar around 1050. This is not correct. The Gospel book that Henry III gave to Goslar is a different Echternach manuscript, the *Codex Caesareus Upsaliensis* (the "Emperor's Bible" or "Goslar Gospels"), today in Uppsala University Library (MS C 93). The Codex Aureus of Echternach itself appears to have remained at Echternach abbey from its creation until the French Revolutionary period. The two books are closely related members of the same Echternach group, and Henry's admiration of the older golden Gospel book at Echternach is generally held to have prompted his commissions for Speyer and Goslar; but the Goslar presentation belongs to the Uppsala manuscript, not to the Nuremberg one. This distinction structures several sections below.

## Physical description and codicology

The Codex Aureus is a large-format parchment Gospel book. The most widely cited dimensions are approximately 446 × 310 mm (the English-language reference literature typically gives 446 × 310 mm; German and Italian descriptions give 445 × 310 mm), and the manuscript comprises 136 folios (leaves) of calfskin parchment (vellum). The facsimile literature describes the collation as seventeen quaternions (gatherings of four bifolia, i.e. eight leaves each), which yields 136 leaves and is consistent with the stated folio count. The parchment is of high quality and unusually white, a deliberate choice that sets off both the gold script and the passages of imperial purple.

The manuscript's defining feature — and the source of its name — is its chrysography: the entire text is written in gold ink. A *chrysograph*, a book whose text is executed in gold, is a rare and enormously costly undertaking, and the Codex Aureus is among the most complete realisations of the type: not a few display pages but the whole of the four Gospels, together with the standard prefatory apparatus, is laid down in gold. The prefatory matter includes three prefaces by Jerome and the letter of Eusebius to Carpianus (the standard prologue to the canon tables), as is usual in medieval Gospel books, plus the short verse tituli carried by angels on the display pages.

The script of the running text is a clear and disciplined Caroline (Carolingian) minuscule, the letters small, lively and precisely aligned; the facsimile commentary attributes the writing to at least two, and probably more, copyists whose hands can be distinguished. Headings, incipits and explicits are executed in display scripts — Roman square capitals (capitalis) with some uncial elements, and rustic capitals — and the book is furnished with gold interlace initials and versals in an uncial manner. The decorative apparatus is exceptionally dense. The GNM's own account speaks of "over 60 pictorial and ornamental pages" and "over 500 initials." The fuller breakdown given in the English reference literature and by the facsimile publisher runs: sixteen full-page miniatures; nine full-page initial pages; sixteen half-page initials; ten decorated canon-table pages; the evangelist portraits; the double "textile"/carpet pages; and 503 smaller initials in colours, gold and silver. Silver tones and imperial purple grounds heighten the effect of the gold.

As to state of preservation and material history, the manuscript block is in good condition and is today kept separately from its cover so that both can be conserved under appropriate conditions. The most important modern discovery about its physical state comes from the scientific study of its painting technique carried out by conservation scientists [Doris Oltrogge](/scholars.html#oltrogge-doris) and [Robert Fuchs](/scholars.html#fuchs-robert) and published by the GNM in 2009 (*Die Maltechnik des Codex Aureus aus Echternach: ein Meisterwerk im Wandel*, 178 pp.). Their systematic examination of all the pictorial and ornamental pages established that reworkings appear "on about a third of the leaves" (*Überarbeitungen auf etwa einem Drittel der Blätter*) — and, crucially, that these overpaintings were most probably carried out already during the manuscript's original production, for aesthetic reasons ("*offenbar ästhetische Gründe*," above all colour), using "nearly all the pigments available in the eleventh century" (*nahezu alle im 11. Jahrhundert verfügbaren Farbmittel*). The book was thus a "work in transformation" (*ein Meisterwerk im Wandel*) even as it was being made, its first painted versions revised before completion; the study also recovered information about the original appearance of the overpainted first versions.

The front cover of the Codex Aureus is one of the most celebrated pieces of Ottonian goldsmith's work to survive, and it is significantly older than the manuscript it now protects. The GNM dates the binding to "between 983 and 991, perhaps 985–987" (*zwischen 983 und 991, vielleicht 985-987*), and localises its manufacture to Trier, in one of the leading goldsmith workshops of the age — the workshop associated with Archbishop Egbert of Trier (in office 977–993), the great episcopal patron whose atelier produced the finest Trier metalwork and whose Gregory Master shaped Trier painting. The English reference literature frames the dating slightly differently but to the same effect: the cover "must date between Otto III's royal coronation of 983 and Theophanu's death in 991." The GNM's own object record grounds this dating on the inscription beside the boy ruler on the cover, who is titled "Otto III" — a title he received at his coronation as German king in 983, at the age of three — so that the cover must postdate 983 and, given the presence of his mother, predate her death in 991.

The cover was a gift (*Stiftung*) of the Empress Theophanu, the Byzantine princess who married Otto II and who governed as regent for her young son, together with Otto III. It is materially sumptuous. Mounted on an oak board, it centres on a carved ivory plaque of the Crucifixion; around this run bands of cloisonné enamel plaques (executed in the *Zellenschmelz* technique of soldered gold cells filled with coloured glass), alternating with plaques bearing cell-mosaic and filigree ornament; between and around these are set gold repoussé reliefs, large semi-precious and precious stones in gold filigree mounts, and rows of pearls. The facsimile description counts forty-eight larger gemstones in filigree settings, each accompanied by four smaller heart- or drop-shaped gems, with pearls at the corners. The spine and back cover are covered in silk. Thin gold bands set with small pearls run along the diagonals, subdividing the relief figures into compartments and forming an "X" that has been read as a Christogram — recalling the "X" traced in ash on the floor of a church at its consecration.

The ivory plaque at the centre depicts Christ on the Cross between the two soldiers of the Passion narrative: Longinus (who pierces Christ's side with the lance) at the right and Stephaton (who offers the vinegar-soaked sponge) at the left, with the personification of Terra (Earth) crouching to support the *suppedaneum* (foot-support), and roundels of Sol and Luna in the upper corners; the titulus reads "(I)HC NAZARENV(S)" and the base of the suppedaneum is inscribed "TERRA." The GNM condition report notes only sparse remains of colour — traces of purple on the figures' legs and garments, on the body of Christ, on the relief ground and the frame, and gold on the edge of the cross, the suppedaneum, the ground and the foliate frame, with the cross blue and various subsidiary elements green, and the pupils blackened. Adolf Goldschmidt (1918) judged the surviving colour not to be original.

The surrounding gold repoussé reliefs form an iconographic programme keyed to Echternach. Along the top and bottom compartments are the symbols of the four evangelists paired with the four rivers of Paradise, signifying the spread of the Gospel to the four quarters of the earth. Along the long sides stand a series of figures, each labelled by inscription: the Virgin Mary and Saint Peter (the patrons of Echternach abbey) at the top of the sides; Echternach's founder Saint Willibrord, and Saints Boniface and Ludger (Liudger), also early missionaries in Germany; and Saint Benedict, founder of the order — together with, at the lowest position on each side, the standing donor figures of King Otto III and the Empress Theophanu, presented as equals among the saints, an image of the close binding of ruling house and Church. These elongated, elegant repoussé figures contrast strongly in style with the forceful, slightly squat figures of the central ivory — a stylistic disjunction that has been much discussed (see §8).

The relationship of cover to codex, and the question for whom the cover was originally made, remains genuinely open. Because every figure on the cover has an Echternach connection, one natural reading is that it was made for Echternach from the first. But some scholars have argued that the cover (or the manuscript it originally clothed) was not made for Echternach at all — for example that Archbishop Egbert presented it to Otto III and Theophanu, perhaps as a reconciliation gift after he had initially supported Henry the Quarrelsome against the child Otto III in 983–984, and that the imperial family subsequently passed it to Echternach.

It has also been suggested that the cover was made for the Trier manuscript now in Paris known as the Sainte-Chapelle Gospels (BnF, lat. 8851), illustrated by the Gregory Master, whose style influenced the later miniatures of the present text. A specific and much-cited reconstruction was proposed by the historian Gunther Wolf, who argued that the cover was commissioned for Echternach — ordered from Egbert while Theophanu met him at Christmas 988 in Cologne — out of the empress's gratitude for recovery from an illness that had struck her at Lake Constance in the late summer of 988, with her devotion to Willibrord (whose death in 739 was approaching its 250th anniversary) an additional motive. Wolf's proposal is regarded in the literature as highly plausible but remains a hypothesis. What is not in doubt is that the cover was joined to the Echternach text after the fact: the sumptuous binding had waited, and was bound with the parchment leaves of the Echternach Gospels most probably not long after the consecration of the abbey's new church buildings on 19 October 1031.

## Date, place of production, and patronage

The manuscript (as distinct from its cover) was written and illuminated at Echternach. On its date the literature converges on the second quarter of the eleventh century but does not agree on a narrow bracket. The GNM dates the codex "around 1030" and elsewhere "around 1030/45"; the Bibliothèque nationale de Luxembourg dates it 1030–1045; the standard English-language summary gives "the approximate period 1030–1050"; Anja Grebe's catalogue gives "around 1045"; some German booksellers, presumably echoing older scholarship, narrow it to "between 1039 and 1043." Against this consensus stands a minority position associated with [Peter Metz](/scholars.html#metz-peter) and followed by [Peter Lasko](/scholars.html#lasko-peter), who preferred a date "between 1053 and 1056," i.e. after Abbot Humbert's death — a dating that, in the words of the reference literature, "do[es] not seem to have convinced later scholars." The essay therefore treats c. 1030–1045/50 as the mainstream dating and flags the 1053–1056 proposal as a real but now largely rejected alternative.

The place of production is not in dispute: the scriptorium of Echternach abbey, then at the height of its powers. The person most often named as patron or director of the enterprise is Abbot Humbert of Echternach (abbot 1028–1051), under whose rule the Echternach school reached its zenith; the facsimile literature names him as the probable person who commissioned the book. It should be stressed that "patron" here most plausibly means the abbot who commanded and oversaw the work within the monastery, rather than an external imperial commissioner; unlike the Speyer and Goslar Gospels, the Codex Aureus is not documented as an imperial commission, and scholars do not agree on the book's original intended beneficiary.

The imperial dimension enters through Henry III (king from 1039, emperor 1046–1056) and his mother, the Empress Gisela of Swabia (d. 1043), and — for the related Goslar book — his wife, the Empress Agnes of Poitou. It is widely held that Henry saw and admired the golden Gospel book at Echternach (i.e. the present Codex Aureus, then in use in the monastery), and that this admiration prompted him to commission from Echternach two further golden Gospel books: the *Codex Aureus Escorialensis* (the Speyer Gospels / Golden Gospels of Henry III), which he presented to Speyer Cathedral — the burial church of his Salian dynasty — around 1046, and the *Codex Caesareus Upsaliensis*, which he gave to his new collegiate church of Saints Simon and Jude at Goslar, the great imperial palace foundation, around 1050.

The Uppsala manuscript's dedication miniature shows Henry III, robed in imperial purple, presenting the book to Saints Simon and Jude, while the facing page shows Henry and Agnes crowned by Christ; a frame carries the date MXLV (1045), which is generally read as symbolic rather than the actual year of production. The Goslar presentation, to repeat, belongs to the Uppsala book; the Codex Aureus of Echternach is the model and inspiration, not the gift. Henry's elevation of the Echternach scriptorium to the status of an imperial scriptorium — a court atelier of the Salian dynasty — is the political context that made this remarkable clustering of golden Gospel books possible.

## The Echternach scriptorium: history, Trier, the Insular inheritance, and the Golden Gospels group

Echternach abbey was founded in 698 by the Northumbrian monk Willibrord (c. 658–739), one of the Anglo-Saxon missionaries who evangelised the Continent; Willibrord had been consecrated in Rome by Pope Sergius and set out from an Irish base (Rath Melsigi, Co. Carlow), so that both Anglo-Saxon and Irish influences fed the abbey from the start. A scriptorium existed at Echternach no later than the eighth century, and its earliest products are written in a distinctive "mixed" style combining Caroline and Insular minuscule features (very pointed script; long narrow stems of "s" and "r"). The most famous witness to this Insular phase is the *Echternach Gospels* (Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS lat. 9389), a richly decorated Insular Gospel book of about 690, whose place of manufacture — Echternach, Northumbria (Lindisfarne), or Ireland — has been debated for a century; its script has been compared to the Durham Gospels, and its text to the Irish MacDurnan Gospels, while it was demonstrably at Echternach by the first half of the eighth century. This Insular Gospel book, brought to the abbey in its earliest years, made Echternach a principal centre for the diffusion of Hiberno-Saxon manuscript culture on the Continent, and it is the deep historical background against which the eleventh-century "golden" phase must be read: the same house that had preserved an Insular Gospel book of c. 690 produced, some 350 years later, one of the most lavish Ottonian-Salian Gospel books.

The decisive institutional turning point came in 973, when — at the request of the last lay abbot, Count Siegfried of Luxembourg — Emperor Otto I had the abbey repopulated with reform monks from Sankt Maximin in Trier, a key house of the Gorze (Lotharingian) monastic reform. This tied Echternach closely to Trier, and it was from Trier that the artistic models of the eleventh-century scriptorium came. The Echternach book painters of c. 1030–1046 drew heavily on the work of the Trier Master of the Registrum Gregorii (the Gregory Master), the anonymous illuminator active under Archbishop Egbert whose statuesque, classicising figures represent the height of Ottonian painting; they also drew on the Egbert Codex, on other Trier manuscripts, and on a mid-Byzantine pictorial source. It is important to note — because the "Reichenau question" is often raised for Ottonian manuscripts — that the Echternach golden Gospels do *not* belong to the much-discussed Reichenau group; their stylistic lineage runs through Trier and the Gregory Master, and ultimately through Late Antique and Byzantine models, not through the island monastery of Reichenau on Lake Constance. (The Reichenau attributions themselves remain contested for lack of documentary evidence, but that debate concerns a different family of manuscripts.)

The peak of the Echternach scriptorium, under Abbot Humbert and then his successor Regimbert (1051–1081), produced a group of large-format golden Gospel books that are the abbey's chief claim to art-historical fame. Modern scholarship treats four large Gospel books as the core surviving group, together with the closely related Bremen Pericopes:

- The **Codex Aureus of Echternach** (Nuremberg, Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Hs. 156142; binding KG 1138), c. 1030–1045/50 — the subject of this essay.
- The **Golden Gospels of Henry III / Codex Aureus Escorialensis** (also "Speyer Gospels," *Codex Aureus Spirensis*), c. 1043–1046, presented by Henry III to Speyer Cathedral, now Real Biblioteca del Monasterio de San Lorenzo de El Escorial, Cod. Vitrinas 17 — a very large book (c. 50 × 33–35 cm) with text in gold.
- The **Codex Caesareus Upsaliensis** (the "Emperor's Bible" / "Goslar Gospels"), c. 1050, presented by Henry III to Saints Simon and Jude at Goslar, now Uppsala University Library, MS C 93 — the youngest of the group, carried off to Sweden during the Thirty Years' War; the standard scholarly facsimile and commentary was published by [Carl Nordenfalk](/scholars.html#nordenfalk-carl) in 1971.

A fourth, only partially preserved, large Echternach Gospel book now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris. On the evidence of the BnF's own catalogue this is **BnF, Nouvelles acquisitions latines 2196** ("Evangelia quatuor, pars"; the *Évangiles de Luxeuil* / "Luxeuil Gospels"), a fragmentary large-format manuscript (surviving as 50 leaves, c. 405 × 287 mm) datable to the second quarter/middle of the eleventh century, whose scribe the BnF describes as "very close to copyist B of the Codex Aureus preserved at Nuremberg (Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Hs. 156142)," and which the catalogue situates art-historically between the Nuremberg and Escorial books. (A caution: a second Echternach-related Gospel book, BnF, Latin 10438, the *Évangiles de Saint-Sauveur de Metz*, is a smaller-format book usually cited as a comparandum rather than as one of the four large books; the "fourth large, partially preserved" manuscript is NAL 2196.)

To these should be added the **Pericopes (Gospel Lectionary) of Henry III** in Bremen (Staats- und Universitätsbibliothek Bremen, msb 0021, older cited form "Ms. b. 21"), with thirty-eight full-page miniatures, produced c. 1039–1043; and the abbey's own liturgical books, such as the Echternach Sacramentary and Gradual (Darmstadt, Universitäts- und Landesbibliothek, Hs. 1946), of c. 1030. The task's reference to "Uppsala Gospels" and "Luxembourg fragments" is thus captured by the Codex Caesareus and by the Echternach material dispersed across libraries (much survives in the Bibliothèque nationale de Luxembourg, including an incomplete Bible, Ms. 15, and a giant Bible, Ms. 264). All of the large golden Gospels are stylistically dependent on the Trier Gregory-Master tradition transmitted at Echternach.

## Iconographic programme and illumination

The decorative architecture of the Codex Aureus is exceptionally systematic, and its designer clearly intended the reader to approach the sacred text through a long, deliberate sequence of splendour. As one art historian observed, the planner of the book "was in no hurry to bring his reader to the text." Each of the four Gospels is prefaced by the same ordered series of display openings: two pages summarising the Gospel; two pages imitating textiles (the "carpet" or "curtain" pages); four pages of narrative scenes arranged in three registers per page; a full-page evangelist portrait; two pages of decorative text; and finally a full-page initial that begins the Gospel proper. The book opens, before Matthew, with the *Maiestas Domini* (Christ in Majesty), the evangelist portraits and the ten canon-table pages that organise the Eusebian concordance of the Gospels within classicising architectural frames of arches and columns.

The **textile pages** are among the most extraordinary features of the manuscript, and the GNM singles them out as belonging "to the most unusual things to be found in book painting." Executed in a highly accomplished technique, these double pages imitate, with near-trompe-l'œil fidelity, the patterns and material of Byzantine or Oriental woven silks. Their significance is twofold. Aesthetically, they demonstrate the painters' virtuosity. Symbolically and politically, silk was the quintessential luxury import and the quintessential imperial gift — the kind of precious stuff exchanged at the highest diplomatic level since Charlemagne — so that painting silk into the Gospel book both signalled the book's supreme value and evoked the imperial world of gift-exchange to which the Echternach golden Gospels belonged. The purple grounds elsewhere in the book (the imperial colour of Late Antique de-luxe codices) reinforce the same associations of majesty and sacrality; text on purple in gold explicitly recalls the imperial purple manuscripts of Late Antiquity.

The **narrative cycle** is the manuscript's most historically significant pictorial feature. Laid out in three horizontal registers to a page (one, two, or sometimes three scenes per register), it presents the Life and Ministry of Christ across the pages preceding the four Gospels — and it does so as a continuous programme, distributing the life of Christ across the four prefatory sequences without concern for whether a given episode actually occurs in the Gospel it precedes. The pages before Matthew run from the Annunciation to the "Feast in the House of Levi"; those before Mark cover miracles from the Wedding at Cana to the Samaritan's thanks after the Cleansing of the Ten Lepers; those before John cover the Passion, Ascension and Pentecost. The pages before Luke are the most remarkable: four full pages given over to four of the parables of Jesus — the Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard, the Parable of the Wicked Husbandmen (the wicked vinedressers), the Parable of the Great Banquet, and the Parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus (Dives and Lazarus). The inclusion of an extended parable cycle was, by the eleventh century, becoming unusual, and it is one of the manuscript's distinctive contributions.

The parable pages reward close reading as narrative machines. The Dives and Lazarus page (folio 78r) tells the parable in three sequential registers: the top shows the sick beggar Lazarus dying at the rich man's door; the middle shows Lazarus's soul carried by angels into the bosom of Abraham; the bottom shows the rich man's soul dragged to Hell and tormented. The colour scheme reinforces the moral reading — cooler blues for the redemptive scenes, red for the fires of Hell — so that the images function as a legible visual argument as well as a decorative sequence. On one accounting the narrative pages together comprise forty-eight framed images with about sixty individual scenes, an unusually large cycle for a medieval Gospel book.

The **evangelist portraits** and the **Maiestas Domini** descend, via the Trier Gregory Master, from Late Antique author-portrait and majesty types, though the Echternach painters translated these models into a more ornamental idiom; the incipit pages and full-page initials ("Liber" opening Matthew, "In principio" opening John, and so on) are constructed as architectures of gold interlace. Most of the miniatures are attributed to two principal hands — a "workshop master" and a presumed pupil — with a third, cruder painter contributing some of the narrative scenes; the compositions and underdrawings are thought to be largely the master's, so that the changes of executant are not conspicuous. The overall style has been both praised and criticised. [C. R. Dodwell](/scholars.html#dodwell-charles-reginald)'s verdict is characteristic: the Echternach style "produced some vigorous and cheerful patterns, as in the St Luke, but it could descend to fussiness, as in the Christ in Majesty, where the strength of the composition has been frittered away by the ornamental vagaries of the Echternach artist"; in the evangelist portraits, he complained, "the ornamental bands of the Sainte-Chapelle Gospels are degraded into decorative garrulity, and there is so little weight and structure under the draperies that they might be covering mere inanimate cushions." This tension between narrative power and ornamental excess is itself a recurring theme of the manuscript's critical reception.

The **canon tables** (ten decorated pages) present the Eusebian apparatus — the system of numbered concordances allowing a reader to find parallel passages across the four Gospels — within columned and arched frames of an antique architectural type, ornamented with geometric and acanthus borders. Their derivation from Late Antique models is one strand of the larger argument (see §6) that the Echternach programme reaches back, through its Trier and Byzantine intermediaries, to Early Christian and Late Antique picture sources.

### Maiestas Domini: Christ in Majesty with the symbols of the four Evangelists and the four major prophets

The folio is conceived as a full-page frontispiece of great chromatic ambition. The leaf is dyed a deep murex-toned purple across its outer margins, reserving a broad field of undyed parchment within which the painted panel is set — a deliberate evocation of late antique and Byzantine imperial codices, and a visual claim to the prestige of purple-and-gold bookmaking that the manuscript's very designation (*codex aureus*) asserts.

The painted field is enclosed by a rigid rectangular frame composed of a gold band overlaid with a repeating wedge-and-step motif in dark blue and red, edged by fine red and green fillets. This pattern imitates cloisonné metalwork rather than any organic ornament, and its four corners are punctuated by gold discs that read as rivet-heads or setting-bosses, so that the frame presents itself as a jewelled plaque affixed to the page. Against the frame's outer edge, four further roundels break the boundary and project into the reserved white margin — an elegant device by which the Evangelist symbols are made to transgress the enclosure of the composition, mediating between the sacred field and the space of the beholder.

At the centre, Christ is enthroned within a pointed mandorla, a vesica whose apex and base are drawn to sharp angles and whose interior is entirely gilded, framed by concentric bands of green, red and white. He is shown youthful and beardless, long-haired, with a cross-nimbus; the right hand is raised in benediction with the two-fingered Latin gesture, while the left steadies an open codex resting against the knee, its written lines rendered in fine brown script. He wears a pale blue-green tunic and a deeper blue-green mantle, both articulated by vermilion clavi and hem-bands, the drapery organised into the taut, calligraphic loops and nested V-folds characteristic of Echternach painting. He is seated on an architecturally conceived throne with a bolster cushion, his bare feet resting on a low suppedaneum. Around the inner edge of the mandorla runs a gold-on-gold inscription, legible only under raking light; it cannot be transcribed with confidence from a photographic reproduction and requires collation against the original or a high-resolution facsimile.

Beyond the mandorla the ground is a saturated lapis blue, entirely unmodulated, which throws the gold of the mandorla and the roundels into maximum relief.

The four prophets occupy the diagonal quadrants, each set within a large circular field of olive-green bounded by a jagged, flame-like gold edge that functions as a compressed aureole. All four are nimbed, barefoot, and seated on low benches with footstools, bent forward over sloping lecterns in the posture of the writing author. Each holds a stylus or pen and unrolls a scroll — a pointed distinction from the Evangelists, who elsewhere in the manuscript write in codices, and one that marks the prophetic word as anterior and unfulfilled. Isaiah, upper left, appears aged and grey-bearded in ochre and lilac; Jeremiah, upper right, is enveloped in a broad crimson mantle; Ezekiel, lower left, wears orange over pale blue; Daniel, lower right, youthful and beardless, is draped in blue over rose.

The four Evangelist symbols, by contrast, are compressed into smaller roundels on the axes, each winged, nimbed, and shown half-length holding or supporting a book: the eagle of John at the summit, the lion of Mark at the left, the ox of Luke at the right, and the angel of Matthew at the base. Their placement — John crowning the composition, Matthew beneath the feet of Christ — is not the canonical Gospel order but a hierarchical one, elevating the evangelist of the Logos to the vertical axis above the enthroned Word.

#### Iconographic reading

The page constructs a fourfold-by-fourfold concordance. The tetramorph derives ultimately from the theophany of Ezekiel 1:4–14 and its Johannine recasting in Revelation 4:6–8, and its association with the four Evangelists is patristic commonplace by the Carolingian period. What distinguishes this composition is the deliberate pairing of that quartet with the four major prophets, so that the Old Covenant witnesses occupy the outer, diagonal positions and the New Covenant witnesses the cardinal ones. The arrangement stages an exegetical argument in purely spatial terms: prophecy encircles and prepares, the Gospel converges upon and confirms, and both radiate from the enthroned Christ who is the object of the one and the subject of the other. The prophets write turned inward, their gaze directed toward the mandorla; the symbols face outward. Revelation is thus shown moving from promise to fulfilment to proclamation along a single centrifugal axis.

</figure>

The folio forms the recto half of a purple opening, facing the *Maiestas Domini* of fol. 2v — of which the outer roundels are just visible at the left edge of the photograph. The two pages were plainly conceived as a single visual and textual unit: the enthroned Christ on the verso is the image to which the verses on the recto refer, and the reader encounters them simultaneously.

As on the facing leaf, the parchment is dyed a saturated purple across the outer margins, with a broad band of reserved white parchment isolating the painted field. The panel itself is enclosed within an unusually deep, multi-layered border. Reading from the outside inward, one passes through a band of pink and green foliate petals — a flattened, symmetrically splayed acanthus — then a gold band carrying the same wedge-and-step pattern in vermilion and dark blue found on fol. 2v, then a second foliate band, and finally a plain gold fillet. The repetition of the metalwork motif across the opening binds the two pages together, and its layering here produces the effect of a jewelled tabula set within a stepped architectural surround rather than a painted frame.

Four roundels are placed on the cardinal axes, each overlapping and breaking the outer border so as to project into the reserved margin — precisely the device used for the Evangelist symbols opposite. Each contains a half-length youthful figure, nimbed, in a pale tunic and rose mantle, and each is identified by a titulus in gold running around the circumference: **IVSTITIA VIRTVS** above, **TEMPERANTIA VIRTVS** at the left, **FORTITVDO VIRTVS** at the right, **PRVDENTIA VIRTVS** below. The four cardinal virtues thus occupy exactly the positions held by the four Evangelist symbols on the verso, establishing a structural rhyme across the opening.

Within this frame stand two large angels on a green ground broken by a low rocky outcrop, their bare feet emerging at the base of the composition. Both are haloed in burnished gold, and their wings — rendered in rose with pale blue coverts and tightly scrolled shoulder-volutes — spread upward and outward above the tablet, filling the upper register. Their gestures are restrained: each raises the inner hand beside the tablet in a gesture of presentation or acclamation rather than physical support. The left angel wears a white-green tunic with a rose mantle drawn across the body; the right is dressed in the reverse arrangement, deep rose over pale green. The two are near-mirror images, and their draperies fall in the same taut, calligraphically looped folds seen in the Christ opposite.

They present a rectangular tabula, itself doubly framed: an outer purple band bearing a delicate white foliate scroll, and within it fourteen horizontal ruled compartments of purple, each carrying a line of gold rustic capitals. The gold has oxidised and abraded unevenly, and in reproduction much of the text is legible only in part.

#### The inscription

The verses are dedicatory or explanatory, glossing the image on the facing page and the purpose of the book. What can be made out with reasonable confidence, with lacunae marked, runs *approximately*:

> PRIMA FRONTE LI[BRI] / RESIDET RE[X] […] / HINC POSITVS PRIMVS / QVIA NON PRECE[DIT] FAMVLVS / CVNCTORVM REGVM / REX EST D(EV)S ATQ(VE) […]ORVM / ET ILLI D(OMI)NO QVI SERVIT CELI[…] / QVISQVIS CONIVNGI / SIB(I) VVLT ET CONSOCIARI / QVOD IVBET ISTE LIBER / AGAT […] CRIMINE LIBER / H[…] PERVENIAT / SAECLA PER OM[NIA] VIVAT

The sense, so far as it can be recovered, appears to be: at the front of the book the King is enthroned, placed first because no servant goes before his lord; God is King of all kings; whoever wishes to be joined and made a companion to that Lord should do what this book commands, free of sin, and so attain [the heavenly reward] and live through all ages. The final line, `SAECLA PER OMNIA VIVAT`, is clear.

#### Iconographic reading

The programme of the opening is unusually tightly argued. Christ enthroned on the verso is encircled by the prophets who foretold him and the Evangelists who proclaimed him; the recto answers with the moral consequence — the four cardinal virtues, the pagan-philosophical quartet absorbed into Christian ethics through Ambrose and Gregory, which frame the injunction that the reader should *do what this book commands*. Revelation on one side, conduct on the other. The angels are cast in their normal function as bearers and heralds of divine text, and the tabula they hold is deliberately made to look like an inscribed metal plaque, assimilating the dedicatory verses to the language of monumental epigraphy and of the treasure binding that encloses the codex.

The choice of the *cardinal* rather than the theological virtues is worth noting: it locates the book's moral claim in the sphere of governance and right ordering, which suits a manuscript produced for and by imperial patronage.

### Incipit Page to the Preface of St Jerome

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Codex-Aureus-of-Echternach/image 4.jpg" alt="Incipit Page to the Preface of St Jerome">
  <figcaption>Incipit Page to the Preface of St Jerome, Nurenberg, Germanisches National Museum, Hs. 156142, fol. 3v.</figcaption>
</figure>

The folio marks the transition from the manuscript's pictorial prologue to its text, and it does so with the full apparatus of the display page. The leaf is again purple-dyed, and here the dye extends across the whole outer field, with a band of reserved parchment — now a pale silvery-lilac rather than white — forming a second, inner margin around the painted panel. The effect is of a framed panel mounted on a coloured ground, and the reader arriving from the angels of fol. 3r encounters no pictorial figure at all: the page is given over entirely to written words made monumental.

The panel is enclosed by a broad ornamental border of interlocking fret, built from stepped and hooked units in vermilion, pale slate-blue and lilac, set against gold and articulated by fine white highlights along one edge of each element. The shading is systematic rather than decorative: each unit is lit consistently from the same side, so that the band reads as a genuinely three-dimensional lattice of overlapping ribbons or inlaid tesserae standing proud of the surface. At intervals along the border, small rectangular gold fields carry a faint incised or lightly drawn rinceau, now much worn. The motif is a variant of the classical meander, filtered through the cloisonné vocabulary that governs the frames of the preceding folios, and it makes the same claim: that the page is to be understood as an object of goldsmith's work.

Within, the ground is a cool green — the same olive-toned green used for the prophets' aureoles and the angels' terrain — scattered with small trefoil sprigs alternately in orange and rose. These are placed with studied irregularity and serve to animate a field that would otherwise read as inert; the device belongs to the repertoire of late antique and Byzantine textile design, and its use here reinforces the sense that the inscription is displayed against a hanging cloth of honour.

Six horizontal bands are laid across this ground, each framed by a stepped moulding in rose and vermilion that gives the bar a shallow relief, as though each were a separate metal strip fixed to the panel. Each carries one line of the incipit in gold-ground display capitals:

> INCIPIT / PRAEFATIO / S(AN)C(T)I HIERONIMI / PRAESBITERI / IN LIBRVM / EVANGELIORV(M)

The letters are square capitals of a deliberately antiquarian cast, generously spaced and reserved in white or silver against the burnished gold. Their condition varies markedly across the page: lines one, three and five remain crisp, while the gold of lines two, four and six has oxidised and darkened, so that the letterforms there survive largely as shadows. The final line ends with a horizontal stroke above the V marking the suspended *-M*, and *PRAESBITERI* preserves the parasitic S common in early medieval spellings of *presbyter*.

The text is the rubric announcing Jerome's prefatory letter to Pope Damasus, conventionally cited by its opening words *Novum opus*, in which Jerome defends his revision of the Latin Gospels against the anticipated charge of tampering with scripture. Its inclusion is entirely standard in Latin Gospel books; what is not standard is the scale and expense of its presentation here.

The hierarchy of the opening sequence is instructive. Fol. 2v gives the theophany, fol. 3r the dedicatory verses held by angels, and fol. 3v the announcement of a mere preface — yet that announcement receives gold ground, dyed purple parchment, and a fully worked ornamental border. The logic is not one of textual importance but of threshold. This is the page at which the codex passes from image into script, and the makers have marked the passage with the same materials used for the *Maiestas* itself, so that the reader crosses from one register to the other without any diminution of splendour. Jerome's name, occupying the central and most legible band, is accorded a monumentality closer to a dedicatory inscription in stone than to a scribal rubric.

The design also demonstrates the Echternach workshop's characteristic strategy of treating the parchment page as an assemblage of distinct materials: dyed textile ground, metal strips, inlaid border, sprinkled fabric. Nothing on the page is presented as ink on skin.

### Decorated Initial B opening Jerome's Letter to Pope Damasus

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Codex-Aureus-of-Echternach/image 5.jpg" alt="Decorated Initial B opening Jerome's Letter to Pope Damasus">
  <figcaption>Decorated Initial B opening Jerome's Letter to Pope Damasus, Nurenberg, Germanisches National Museum, Hs. 156142, fol. 4r.</figcaption>
</figure>

The folio is the answering half of the opening begun on fol. 3v, and the pairing is deliberate: the verso announced the preface in six horizontal bands of capitals, the recto delivers its first word in a single monumental letter. The reader passes from statement to enactment across the gutter.

The material scheme is continued exactly. The leaf is purple-dyed, though here the dye has taken more warmly, producing a rose-madder tonality; within it a broad band of pale lilac parchment is reserved, and the painted panel sits at the centre of this double surround. As on the preceding folio, the ground of the panel is the same cool green scattered with small orange trefoil sprigs, so that the two pages read unmistakably as a single unit of design.

The border, however, has been changed. In place of the illusionistic fret of fol. 3v, three concentric ornamental bands now enclose the field. The outermost carries a row of pale green rosettes on a rose ground, alternating with fine white detailing; the innermost repeats the motif in vermilion and blue, each blossom set against a lobed blue calyx. Between them runs a plain gold fillet edged in orange. The border is thus vegetal rather than architectonic, and its lightness has a clear compositional purpose: it yields precedence to the initial, where the metalwork frame of the previous page would have competed with it.

The initial **B** occupies almost the entire height of the panel and is the page's whole argument. Its stem is a broad gold shaft, its bowls two great gold rings, and the whole is modelled in burnished gold with contour lines drawn in orange-red that describe the ribbon's turnings and give the letter a rounded, metallic body. The two bowls are filled with dense foliate scrollwork — gold acanthus tendrils uncoiling from central rosettes against alternating grounds of rose and lapis blue — while the shaft is inset with two narrow panels of white interlace on a rose ground. At the head, foot, and mid-point of the letter the ornament changes register: there the gold resolves into knotted interlace terminating in zoomorphic heads, small beaked or snouted creatures whose jaws grip the adjoining strands. The bowls meet at a clasp-like rose boss, and the terminals flare into palmette flourishes.

The vocabulary is worth naming precisely. The bowls belong to the Carolingian and Ottonian tradition of gold acanthus rinceau; the interlace panels and the biting animal terminals belong to the Insular repertoire, transmitted to Echternach through its Anglo-Saxon foundation and the earlier Insular books held there. Their combination within one letter is characteristic of Echternach's display initials, and it is a synthesis rather than a juxtaposition: the interlace is subordinated to the letter's structure, disciplined into the joints and terminals, while the classicising foliage governs the open fields.

The remainder of the text is disposed around the initial with careful economy. In the right margin the continuation runs vertically in orange rustic capitals, broken into short stacked groups: **E / A / TO / PAPAE / DA / MA / SO**. Along the lower edge, in larger and more widely spaced capitals of the same orange, stands **HIERONIMVS**. The full reading is therefore *B(eato) papae Damaso Hieronimus* — "To the blessed Pope Damasus, Jerome" — the salutation of the letter *Novum opus*, in which Jerome justifies to his patron the revision of the Latin Gospels.

The page belongs to a well-defined Ottonian type in which the opening word of an important text is enlarged into a quasi-architectural object and the rest of the phrase reduced to a marginal armature around it. The hierarchy is unambiguous: gold and full ornament for the initial, plain orange for the words, with the addressee's name and the author's name given the two most legible positions. That Jerome's name is set out along the base in the largest of the secondary capitals continues the emphasis already given it on fol. 3v, where it occupied the central band.

More broadly, the four folios examined so far form a graduated approach to the Gospel text. The *Maiestas* on 2v establishes the theological subject; the angels' tabula on 3r states the book's purpose and moral claim; 3v names the text about to begin; 4r begins it. The workshop maintains a constant material vocabulary — purple ground, green field, gold, sprinkled trefoils — while varying the border ornament from page to page, so that the sequence reads as continuous without becoming monotonous.

### From Annunciation to Herod's Court: The Infancy Cycle

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Codex-Aureus-of-Echternach/image 6.jpg" alt="From Annunciation to Herod's Court: The Infancy Cycle">
  <figcaption>From Annunciation to Herod's Court: The Infancy Cycle, Nurenberg, Germanisches National Museum, Hs. 156142, fol. 18v.</figcaption>
</figure>

The folio presents a full-page narrative miniature articulated into three superimposed registers, each framed in burnished gold and separated by horizontal bands bearing tituli in gold rustic capitals against a coloured ground. The painted field is set within a broad margin of rose-stained parchment — an economical evocation of imperial purple that is characteristic of the manuscript's luxury register — with an inner border of plain vellum functioning as a mount. The tituli are now partly abraded; legible fragments include references to the Creator issuing forth from the Virgin and to the Spirit's inflaming presence, together with the salutation of the Virgin, but any full transcription should be established against the facsimile rather than from photographic reproduction.

- Upper register

Two Lucan episodes are juxtaposed under a continuous architectural canopy. At the left, beneath a triple arcade from which a lamp is suspended, the archangel Gabriel — identified by the inscription GABRIHEL — advances with a cross-staff towards the Virgin, labelled S(ANCTA) MARIA, who responds with the open-palmed gesture of acceptance. The basket of coloured wool at her feet invokes the apocryphal tradition, transmitted through the *Protevangelium*, of Mary's spinning of the Temple veil, a motif that binds the Incarnation to the imagery of weaving. A columned aedicula divides this scene from the Visitation at the right, where Mary and Elizabeth embrace cheek to cheek upon a green mound flanked by towered city walls — a formula of Byzantine derivation transmitted to the Ottonian West through Italian and Greek intermediaries.

- Middle register

The Nativity occupies the left half. The Christ Child, tightly swaddled, lies in an elevated manger set beneath a domed arch, with the ox and the ass above him in adoration; below, the Virgin reclines on a mattress within a curtained architectural enclosure, while Joseph is seated at the extreme left in the pensive attitude — head supported on hand — that conventionally marks his exclusion from the mystery. The stacked disposition of manger and couch, rather than the coplanar arrangement of later Western practice, again betrays a Middle Byzantine compositional source. To the right unfolds the Annunciation to the Shepherds, captioned PASTORES: a haloed angel with cross-staff and outspread wings turns towards two shepherds who gesture in astonishment, while a compressed choir of angels crowds the upper right corner. The flock — sheep, goats and dogs rendered in the same pink-and-white palette as the human figures — animates the lower edge of the field.

- Lower register

The final scene stages the Magi before Herod (Matthew 2:1–8). Beneath a richly articulated palace façade with tiled roof, turrets and a suspended lamp, three crowned Magi approach from the left, preceded by a chamberlain and gesturing in colloquy. At the centre, three scribes and elders bend over open codices, consulting the prophetic texts at Herod's command; the king himself sits enthroned at the right, sceptre in hand, in an attitude of authority undercut by the anxious animation of his gesture. A guard with spear and almond-shaped shield closes the composition. The crowned Magi and the kite shield are both features of interest for dating and for the manuscript's relation to contemporary imperial imagery.

Stylistically the page exemplifies the mature Echternach manner: elongated figures with small heads and emphatic silhouettes, drapery articulated by parallel white highlights over saturated grounds of vermilion, rose, orange and viridian, and an architecture that functions less as space than as a system of narrative punctuation, dividing and framing episodes without pretending to spatial coherence.

### Gifts, Dreams and Departures: The Magi Cycle

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Codex-Aureus-of-Echternach/image 7.jpg" alt="Gifts, Dreams and Departures: The Magi Cycle">
  <figcaption>Gifts, Dreams and Departures: The Magi Cycle, Nurenberg, Germanisches National Museum, Hs. 156142, fol. 19r.</figcaption>
</figure>

The recto continues the infancy cycle begun on the facing verso, retaining the same tripartite structure: three registers of unequal height enclosed within a gold fillet, separated by inscribed bands, and set against a broad field of rose-tinted parchment. The continuity of format across the opening confirms that the two folios were conceived as a single visual argument rather than as discrete pages.

- Upper register

The Adoration of the Magi. Three crowned kings advance from the left across a narrow green groundline, each bearing a vessel: the foremost holds a lidded flask, the second a shallow footed bowl, the third — represented in the deepest obeisance, his knees bent and torso inclined — a covered cup. Their differentiation is achieved through age (the leading figure beardless, the two behind bearded) and through the graduated intensity of their gesture, a device that converts the group into a sequence of increasing reverence. A gate tower surmounted by a star separates the approaching procession from the Virgin, who is enthroned at the right beneath a gilded arch against a green ground; she holds the Child frontally on her lap, her hand raised to steady him. The titulus above refers to the three gifts of the Magi as testimony to the Lord, though the opening word is abraded and should not be transcribed from reproduction.

- Middle register

Two moments of the Magi's return are compressed into a continuous field of lapis blue. At the left, beneath a domed tower, the three kings lie asleep in a single wide bed, their heads aligned on a bolster, the foremost with eyes closed and hand raised in the gesture that conventionally signals a dream received; an angel descends steeply from above, wings extended, addressing them. At the right the same three ride out on horseback, turning and gesturing to one another in colloquy — a narrative device that renders visible the deliberation Matthew leaves implicit. The scene is doubly labelled MAGI, once for each moment, an unusually explicit acknowledgement of continuous narration. The titulus is here largely legible and states that, warned from heaven, they returned by a direct route; the substitution of a *straight path* for the Gospel's *another way* is a point worth remarking, since it shifts the emphasis from evasion of Herod to providential guidance.

- Lower register

The final scene unfolds beneath an extended basilican façade with tiled roof, blind arcade and flanking towers. Three figures occupy the foreground: at the left a bearded man in a green mantle, staff in hand, turns back with an open-palmed gesture; at the centre the Virgin, veiled and slightly inclined, carries an object in her covered hands; and at the right, beneath a drawn curtain and beside a low altar-like block, a bearded man in blue and ochre holds the Christ Child against his shoulder, a second, smaller veiled head appearing immediately behind him. The reading of this register is not self-evident from the image alone. The curtained enclosure, the altar block and the transfer of the Child into the arms of an older bearded man point towards a Presentation in the Temple, with Simeon receiving the Child, Anna behind him, and Joseph at the left.

The page displays the same idiom as the verso: elongated, small-headed figures whose volumes are established by white striations over saturated grounds; horses rendered in improbable pinks and greys that function as chromatic accents rather than descriptions; and architecture used syntactically, to segment and punctuate rather than to situate. The Magi's crowns, spurred boots and riding equipment reflect eleventh-century courtly usage and merit attention in any discussion of the manuscript's imperial resonances.

### From the Angel's Command to the Jordan: Narrative Acceleration

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Codex-Aureus-of-Echternach/image 8.jpg" alt="From the Angel's Command to the Jordan: Narrative Acceleration">
  <figcaption>From the Angel's Command to the Jordan: Narrative Acceleration, Nurenberg, Germanisches National Museum, Hs. 156142, fol. 19v.</figcaption>
</figure>

The verso maintains the tripartite scheme of the preceding pages but marks a decisive narrative turn: with its lowest register the cycle passes from the infancy proper to the threshold of Christ's public ministry. Three registers of gold-framed fields are separated by inscribed bands in blue rustic capitals on burnished gold; here, exceptionally, the third band carries two hexametric lines rather than one, a redoubling that signals the compression of two independent episodes into a single register.

- Upper register

Two Matthaean episodes are set side by side without an intervening architectural partition, joined instead by a continuous green groundline. At the left, within a domed structure, Joseph lies asleep on a couch while an angel swoops down towards him against a ground of burnished gold — the only gold field in the miniature, and a deliberate marker of divine intervention, since both adjacent scenes are given landscape or coloured grounds. At the right unfolds the Flight into Egypt: the Virgin, haloed and veiled, is seated sidesaddle on a broad chair-like saddle, holding the Child frontally against her breast, while a bearded man in a rose mantle strides ahead, staff in hand, drawing the donkey's rein and turning back towards the group. The banded ground of green, ochre and blue behind the group is one of the few concessions to landscape in the entire cycle. The titulus states that Joseph, as the angel commands, arose and departed.

- Middle register

The Massacre of the Innocents, introduced by a titulus announcing that the crowd of infants is put to death. At the left, beneath a tiled roof and a drawn curtain, Herod sits enthroned in orange and blue, sceptre in one hand, the other extended in the gesture of command; a guard with a sword stands behind him. The right two-thirds of the field are given over to the slaughter itself, articulated by three inscriptions — CARNIFICES for the executioners, INFANTES for the children, MATRES for the mothers. Two soldiers dominate the centre, one raising a broad sword above his head, the other seizing a child by the arm; beneath their feet the bodies of the slain are heaped along the lower edge in a dense frieze of pale limbs. At the extreme right two mothers respond in contrasting registers of grief: one bends forward with arms outstretched towards the child being torn away, the other sits collapsed and inward-turned, her own infant clutched to her. The distribution is rhetorically calculated — command at one end, consequence at the other, with the act of violence held at the centre.

- Lower register

Two scenes divided by a slender tower. At the left, Christ Among the Doctors: the young Christ, cross-nimbed and seated on a low dais, unrolls a scroll held jointly with a bearded elder to his right, while a bank of six doctors, tightly overlapped and gesticulating in dispute, occupies the left half beneath a tiled roof from which three lamps are suspended. At the right, the Baptism of Christ: John the Baptist, labelled S(ANCTVS) IOHANNES and clad in a spotted mantle evoking camel hair, lays his hand on the head of Christ, who stands immersed in the Jordan; the water rises about him in a piled, scalloped mass rather than lying flat, according to the Byzantine convention that renders the river as a heaped body. Above, the Dove descends within a medallion set against a segment of red sky. The two tituli read as a distich: the first observes that Christ, learning, listens to the doctors though he knows all things; the second that Christ washes us from guilt in the wave of the Jordan.

The page is the most rhetorically varied of the opening. The painter modulates ground colour semantically — gold for the angelic apparition, blue for the massacre, rose for the Temple — and permits himself, in the Innocents, a density of overlapping bodies unmatched elsewhere in the cycle. The figures retain the Echternach idiom of small heads, elongated proportions and white-striated drapery, but the emotional register of the mothers, and the compressed disputation of the doctors, indicate an artist working comfortably beyond the repertory of hieratic frontality.

## Art-historical significance

The Codex Aureus of Echternach occupies a pivotal place at the hinge between Ottonian and early Salian book art. Chronologically it sits at the very end of, or just after, the Ottonian dynasty (whose last ruler, Henry II, died in 1024), and it inaugurates the golden Gospels of the Salian imperial scriptorium at Echternach; it is thus routinely described as a major example of Ottonian illumination even though, strictly, it falls at or just beyond the dynasty's end. Its importance is threefold.

First, in the sheer scale and coherence of its decoration it is one of the two or three most lavishly illuminated manuscripts of its era — the facsimile literature claims it carries more pictorial decoration than any other manuscript of the period except the Golden Gospels of Henry III — and its systematic prefatory architecture (summary, textile, narrative, portrait, incipit, initial) became a template for the later Echternach books. The narrative cycle in particular, with its Late Antique-derived register format, transmits an Early Christian mode of continuous pictorial narration into the eleventh century.

Second, the manuscript is a key document of the relationship between Ottonian-Salian art and its Late Antique and Byzantine models. The dependence of the Echternach painters on the Trier Gregory Master is decisive here: the Gregory Master's own style, as the Italian scholarship emphasises, consisted precisely in an intense reworking of Late Antique and Byzantine figural sources, converting the spirit of classical composition into the pictorial conception of the tenth century — the so-called Ottonian renaissance. The Echternach golden Gospels inherit this classicism at one remove; the painted silks and purple grounds, and the parable and miracle cycles, all point back to Late Antique and Byzantine prototypes. The Byzantine dimension is doubly present: in the pictorial models the painters used, and in the person of the Empress Theophanu, the Byzantine princess whose donation the cover was, and whose presence in Ottonian art has itself been the subject of scholarship (Hiltrud Westermann-Angerhausen's study of "traces of Theophanu" in Ottonian treasure art).

Third, the book is an instrument of imperial ideology and of the politics of the Ottonian-Salian *Reichskirche* (imperial church). Its very materials — gold script, purple grounds, painted imperial silks, a treasure cover displaying the imperial donors Otto III and Theophanu among the saints — proclaim the fusion of sacred and imperial authority. Its large format announces its function as an object of ecclesiastical and imperial representation. The programme on the cover, associating the ruling house directly with the abbey's saints and with the four-fold diffusion of the Gospel, is a visual statement of the alliance of throne and altar on which the imperial church system rested. And the manuscript's role as the admired model that prompted Henry III's commissions for Speyer and Goslar makes it a node in the network by which the Salian dynasty used luxury Gospel books to bind its principal religious foundations to itself. In the broader literature on Ottonian book illumination — pre-eminently Henry Mayr-Harting's *Ottonian Book Illumination: An Historical Study* (an expanded version of his 1987 Slade Lectures at Oxford) — such books are read exactly as showing "how religion and political ideology were intertwined in Ottonian culture from about 950 to 1050."

## Provenance and later history

For most of its life the Codex Aureus remained at Echternach. It was there that Henry III is thought to have seen it, and there it stayed through the later Middle Ages and the early modern period. The dispersal came with the French Revolutionary Wars. During the War of the First Coalition, Luxembourg was conquered and annexed by Revolutionary France (becoming part of the *département des Forêts*), and the abbey was suppressed in 1795/1796. The monks fled, carrying the manuscript and other portable treasures with them; the codex travelled by way of Mainz to Erfurt (the reference literature specifies the Benedictine house on the Petersberg at Erfurt).

The next stage is well documented but carries a persistent nominal confusion that must be flagged. In 1801 the manuscript was acquired by Duke Ernst II of Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg<a href="../../../endnotes.html#fn-ernst-ii-saxe-gotha-altenburg" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a> — this is the reading of the German scholarly literature (the [Kahsnitz](/scholars.html#kahsnitz-rainer)/Rücker commentary) and of the Gotha Research Library, which records that Duke Ernst II bought some fifty valuable medieval manuscripts from Echternach and Murbach through the enterprising French Benedictine Jean-Baptiste Maugérard (1735–1815). Some English-language and facsimile sources garble the ducal name as "Ernst II of Saxe-Coburg and Altenburg"; this appears to be an error, and the correct attribution is to the House of Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg. Through dynastic inheritance the manuscript passed into the collection of the House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha (which after the First World War was placed in a foundation), and it remained there until after the Second World War.

The modern acquisition is the last major episode. The ducal house resolved to sell the manuscript for financial reasons — German accounts connect the sale to the loss of the family's Gotha properties under Soviet occupation, forcing a distress sale. The then duke wished the book to stay in Germany, and its purchase was arranged jointly by the German federal government and the Länder, with Bavaria taking the lead, so that its new home would be the Germanisches Nationalmuseum in Nuremberg. German Wikipedia (echoing the museum's acquisition account) gives the sale as 9 May 1955, by the House of Coburg-Gotha to the GNM, "for financial reasons," at a price of 1.1 million Deutsche Mark; the price is reported variously, however (at least one German reference source gives 1.5 million DM), so the exact figure should be confirmed against the primary GNM acquisition record. The manuscript has remained in Nuremberg ever since, catalogued as Hs. 156142, with the treasure binding inventoried separately as KG 1138 and displayed in the museum's permanent medieval galleries; the manuscript block and the cover are deliberately kept and conserved apart so that each can be preserved under optimal conditions.

## Historiography and scholarly debate

The Codex Aureus has been studied continuously since the late nineteenth century, and the shape of the scholarship illuminates several enduring controversies.

- The nineteenth- and early twentieth-century foundations

The manuscript and its cover entered the art-historical literature with Wilhelm Vöge's studies of the "German school" of painting around the turn of the first millennium (1891) and of a "German carver of the tenth century" (1899), and with Adolf Goldschmidt's fundamental catalogue of Carolingian and Ottonian ivories (vol. 2, 1918), which classified the central Crucifixion plaque and judged its surviving colour to be non-original. Carl Nordenfalk's early intervention — "Neue Dokumente zur Datierung des Echternacher Evangeliars in Gotha" (*Zeitschrift für Kunstgeschichte* 1, 1932) — signals how early the dating question was joined.

- The three principal specialists

Three scholars stand out as most closely associated with the study of this specific manuscript. Peter Metz produced the first monographic treatment, *Das Goldene Evangelienbuch von Echternach im Germanischen Nationalmuseum zu Nürnberg* (Munich: Prestel, 1956), issued in English as *The Golden Gospels of Echternach* (New York: Praeger, 1957), which remains the foundational full-length study in English and the basis of most later summaries. Rainer Kahsnitz directed the modern scholarly reappraisal: he was the editor of and principal commentator on the 1982 facsimile and its accompanying volume (with Ursula Mende and Elisabeth Rücker), *Das Goldene Evangelienbuch von Echternach: Eine Prunkhandschrift des 11. Jahrhunderts*, whose essays on the Trier–Echternach background, the binding, and the picture programme set the terms of current understanding. Carl Nordenfalk, finally, shaped the study of the whole Echternach golden-Gospels group and of its Trier sources: beyond his 1932 dating article, his work on the Master of the Registrum Gregorii (the Gregory Master) and his 1971 facsimile-commentary on the Codex Caesareus Upsaliensis provide the comparative frame within which the Codex Aureus is now placed. (Other important contributors — [Karl Oettinger](/scholars.html#oettinger-karl) on the ivory carver, Gunther Wolf and Hiltrud Westermann-Angerhausen on the cover's date and patronage, Peter Lasko and C. R. Dodwell and Henry Mayr-Harting in the general histories, and Anja Grebe in the 2007 GNM exhibition catalogue — are drawn on throughout this essay and listed in the bibliography, but the three specialists most directly identified with the manuscript itself are Metz, Kahsnitz and Nordenfalk.)

- The dating controversy

The central chronological debate concerns whether the manuscript belongs to c. 1030–1045/50 (the mainstream view, held by the GNM, the BnL, Grebe and most recent scholarship, and consistent with production under Abbot Humbert) or to 1053–1056 (the later dating preferred by Metz and followed by Lasko, which would place it after Humbert's death). The later dating has not persuaded most subsequent scholars, but it remains part of the record and cautions against treating any single narrow date as settled.

A distinct debate surrounds the central ivory plaque. Its style differs markedly from the elongated repoussé figures around it, and its origin has been much discussed. Metz regarded the ivory as contemporary with the rest of the cover (i.e. c. 985–991); Lasko dated it, like the text in his view, to 1053–1056; [John Beckwith](/scholars.html#beckwith-john) attributed it and related pieces to a workshop "possibly at Trier in the last twenty years of the tenth century." Most strikingly, Karl Oettinger's 1960 study, "Der Elfenbeinschnitzer des Echternacher Codex Aureus und die Skulptur unter Heinrich III. (1039–56)," connected the ivory carver to sculpture of the reign of Henry III — implying a mid-eleventh-century date for the ivory and thus decoupling it from the late-tenth-century metalwork. The disagreement is unresolved and turns on stylistic judgement: whether the ivory is a late-tenth-century component of the original Trier cover or a later insertion.

The cover's original destination. Whether the cover was made for Echternach from the outset, or for another Trier manuscript (e.g. the Sainte-Chapelle Gospels) and only later associated with Echternach, or presented by Egbert to the imperial family and thence to Echternach, remains contested; Wolf's 988/Cologne hypothesis is influential but not proven. Scholars agree only that cover and text were united after the fact, most likely around or soon after 1031.

A longer-running art-historical question — how far the Echternach programme derives from Late Antique picture sources — is inseparable from the study of the Trier Gregory Master, whose classicism the Italian scholarship ([Liana Castelfranchi Vegas](/scholars.html#castelfranchi-vegas-liana); the Treccani encyclopaedias) analyses as a deep reworking of Late Antique and Byzantine sources. For the Codex Aureus specifically, the register-format narrative cycle and the canon-table and portrait types are the principal evidence adduced for a Late Antique pictorial ancestry mediated through Trier.

The 2009 Oltrogge–Fuchs study reoriented the debate toward materials and process, demonstrating that the manuscript was substantially overpainted during its own production and thereby complicating any simple account of "hands" and "campaigns." This scientific work — non-invasive pigment and technique analysis of every decorated page — represents the current frontier of research on the manuscript and has refined understanding of both its palette and its genesis.

## Facsimile editions and digitization

The principal scholarly facsimile is the two-volume edition produced in 1982 by the publishers Müller und Schindler (Stuttgart) with S. Fischer (Frankfurt am Main), also issued through Coron Verlag (Zürich), edited by Rainer Kahsnitz, with a commentary volume by Kahsnitz (with contributions by Ursula Mende and Elisabeth Rücker). It is one of the most elaborate facsimiles in the history of the genre: the leaves were individually trimmed to match the original, the gilding of both script and miniatures was applied by hand in genuine 23½-carat gold after the manner of medieval monastic workshops, the silk binding cloth was specially woven. The Princeton University Library catalogue records that the facsimile was published in a "facsimile edition of 850 copies... in three versions with the same book-blocks, but different bindings: the standard edition, the semi-deluxe and the luxury edition"; the luxury binding reproduces the original treasure cover, with a replica ivory Crucifixion, gems in filigree, and enamelled plaques. A later partial facsimile — *Das Goldene Evangelienbuch von Echternach: Faksimile-Teilausgabe*, comprising twelve leaves reproduced in 22-carat gilding, with a commentary by Johannes Pommeranz — was issued by Coron/Müller und Schindler in 2015.

The standard monograph remains Peter Metz's *The Golden Gospels of Echternach* (1957; German original 1956). The modern GNM exhibition catalogue by Anja Grebe, *Codex Aureus. Das Goldene Evangelienbuch von Echternach* (Darmstadt: Primus Verlag / Wissenschaftliche Buchgesellschaft, 2007), accompanied the 2007–2008 exhibition and is the most accessible up-to-date treatment; the technical study by Doris Oltrogge and Robert Fuchs, *Die Maltechnik des Codex Aureus aus Echternach: ein Meisterwerk im Wandel* (Nuremberg: Verlag des Germanischen Nationalmuseums, 2009), is the essential source on materials and painting process. The manuscript is also catalogued in Hardo Hilg's catalogue of the Latin medieval manuscripts of the GNM.

The manuscript is presented as a digital facsimile on the GNM's own digital library ("Digitale Bibliothek") under the shelfmark Hs. 156142, and the GNM's library pages advertise a "Codex Aureus Epternacensis – digital" version; independent scholarly accounts confirm that the complete codex can be viewed there. (A caveat: because the GNM digital-library viewer is a JavaScript application, the precise technical specifications — total image count, viewer software, and whether a IIIF manifest is exposed for the manuscript block — could not be verified from the page's static content and should be checked in a live browser.) The treasure binding is independently catalogued in the GNM Objektkatalog (KG 1138) and is available there with a IIIF (Presentation API v2) manifest comprising seven images of the cover, published under a Creative Commons BY-NC-ND 4.0 licence. The fragmentary related manuscript in Paris (BnF, NAL 2196) is digitised on Gallica.

## Italian scholarship and exhibition treatments

Italian-language scholarship treats the Codex Aureus chiefly within general accounts of Ottonian art and illumination, and within the study of its Trier model, the Gregory Master. Liana Castelfranchi Vegas's *L'arte ottoniana intorno al Mille* (Milan: Jaca Book, 2002; ISBN 978-88-16-40579-0) is the standard Italian synthesis of Ottonian art around the year 1000, emphasising the extraordinary vitality of the "minor arts" — illuminated codices, ivories and goldsmith's work — as the era's supreme achievement, and situating Echternach within that panorama alongside Reichenau, Trier, Cologne and the Lombard centres. The Treccani *Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale* treats the manuscript directly in its articles on "Miniatura" and on the "Maestro del Registrum Gregorii": the former identifies the *Codex Aureus Epternacensis* (Nuremberg, Germanisches Nationalmuseum, 156142) as bearing the decisive imprint of the Gregory Master and locates the Echternach scriptorium's peak in the 1030s–1040s under Abbot Humbert and the protection of Henry III; the latter analyses the Gregory Master's style as an intense reworking of Late Antique and Byzantine figural sources, the fountainhead of the Echternach manner. The Italian illuminated-manuscript magazine *Alumina – Pagine Miniate* has also published on the codex. Italian Wikipedia and the Italian Catholic encyclopaedia Cathopedia carry descriptive entries consistent with the German and English scholarship (136 folios, 446 × 310 mm, gold text, Ottonian illumination, model for Henry III's commissions).

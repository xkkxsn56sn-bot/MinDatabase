## A Chapel at the Heart of Florentine Painting

On the right side of the chancel of Santa Croce, second in the row of chapels that open onto the eastern wall of the transept, stands the funerary chapel of the Peruzzi, one of the great banking dynasties of medieval Florence. It is a tall, narrow space — roughly twelve metres in height — closed by a quadripartite vault and lit by a single lancet window, and it contains what survives of one of the most consequential mural cycles of the Italian Trecento: six scenes from the lives of the two Saints John, the Baptist and the Evangelist, painted by [Giotto di Bondone](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html) and his workshop at the height of the master's maturity. Facing one another across the chapel, the two triads of narratives — the Baptist on the left wall as one enters, the Evangelist on the right — once formed, with a now-dispersed altarpiece, a vault bearing the symbols of the four Evangelists, an archivolt peopled with eight busts of prophets, and painted figures on the window wall, a coherent decorative whole of extraordinary ambition. The six scenes of the two walls are illustrated in the section on the Peruzzi Chapel in the entry on [Giotto di Bondone](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html#the-peruzzi-chapel-a-masterpiece-painted-on-dry-ground).

What the visitor sees today is a ruin, and an eloquent one. The cycle was executed, almost uniquely in Giotto's surviving oeuvre, largely *a secco* — on dry plaster rather than into the fresh intonaco of true fresco — and this technical choice, whatever its motivation, condemned the paintings to a fragility that the subsequent centuries exploited without mercy. Whitewashed in the eighteenth century, scraped back into visibility in the 1840s, aggressively repainted, then stripped of those repaints in the mid-twentieth century, the murals survive as worn, abraded fields of colour in which Giotto's design nonetheless remains legible with startling force. Indeed, it is precisely this double condition — material wreckage and compositional grandeur — that has made the Peruzzi Chapel so magnetic an object of study. Ultraviolet examination carried out between 2009 and 2013 revealed beneath the opaque surface a wealth of modelling and detail invisible to the naked eye, prompting the Florentine restoration institute that led the campaign to announce the recovery of a 'secret Giotto'.

The chapel's importance, however, was never in doubt among painters. For the artists of the Florentine Renaissance, the Peruzzi Chapel was a school. The young Michelangelo climbed into Santa Croce to draw from the *Ascension of Saint John the Evangelist*, and the sheet that records his study survives in the Louvre; the figures of Masaccio at the Brancacci Chapel are unthinkable without the gravity and spatial breadth that Giotto achieved here. Even the chapel's literary afterlife testifies to its aura: when E. M. Forster, in *A Room with a View*, sent Lucy Honeychurch into Santa Croce without a Baedeker, it was before 'the Giottos' of Santa Croce — by tradition, the Peruzzi Chapel among them — that he staged her education in looking.

What is actually known about the chapel runs from the documents of the Peruzzi family and the testimony of the early sources, through the long controversy over the cycle's date, to the iconography of the two walls, the peculiarities of the technique, the melancholy history of its restorations, and the scattered fragments, panel paintings and drawings that once belonged to its orbit. The layers of evidence have to be kept distinct: what the archives attest, what the early biographers report, what modern diagnostics have demonstrated, and what remains — as so much here does — a matter of scholarly inference.

## The Peruzzi: Money, Memory and the New Church

The Peruzzi were not nobility of the sword but of the ledger. By the end of the thirteenth century their trading and banking company, with branches reaching from England to the Levant, stood among the two or three most powerful financial organisms in Europe; the chronicler Giovanni Villani<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giovanni-villani" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, who was himself for a time a shareholder of the firm, would later count them with the Bardi among the 'columns of Christendom' whose loans sustained kings and popes alike. Their houses clustered in the quarter immediately west of Santa Croce, where the curving line of today's Piazza de' Peruzzi still preserves the footprint of the Roman amphitheatre around which the family's properties were built. The Franciscan church rising at the edge of their neighbourhood was thus, in the most literal sense, their parish church, and the family's investment in it was an act at once of piety, of civic self-assertion, and of prudent provision for the afterlife — a consideration never trivial for men whose profession skirted the Church's condemnation of usury.

The documentary anchor of the chapel's history is a will. In 1292 Donato di Arnaldo Peruzzi, recorded alive until 1299, left by testament the sum of 200 lire to the friars of Santa Croce, designated for the construction of a family chapel in the new church. The bequest is remarkable above all for its date: the present basilica, traditionally said to have been begun on a design by [Arnolfo di Cambio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Arnolfo-di-Cambio.html), was founded only in 1294 or 1295, so that Donato was endowing a chapel in a building that did not yet exist. The will has accordingly been recognised in the scholarly literature as the earliest surviving document that can be connected with any extant family chapel in Florence — a distinction first given prominence by [Eve Borsook](/scholars.html#borsook-eve), who published the relevant acts in the appendix of her 1965 monograph on the chapel, and it shows how early the great mercantile families moved to secure burial rights in the transept of the Franciscan church, precisely where proximity to the high altar promised the greatest spiritual benefit. The chapels flanking the Cappella Maggiore were the most coveted real estate in the building, and the Peruzzi obtained the second on the right, beside what would later become the [Bardi Chapel](/Content/Churches/Cappella-Bardi.html) — so that, by an irony of urban history, the two greatest banking houses of Florence, partners and rivals in life and joint victims of the same catastrophic bankruptcy, face eternity side by side.

Who, then, commissioned the murals? Here the documents fall silent, and the gap between the 1292 bequest and the painted decoration — a generation at least — must be filled by inference. Donato died too early to have dealt with Giotto; the money he left was for walls, not for paintings. Family tradition recorded by the Peruzzi themselves, and transmitted through the genealogical literature of the nineteenth century, names Giovanni di Rinieri Peruzzi, who died in 1358, as the donor of the mural decoration, and this identification — unverifiable in any surviving contract, but chronologically plausible and widely accepted — would make the cycle the project of the generation that brought the company to its zenith in the 1310s and 1320s. It has been suggested that the small heads set within the ten hexagonal frames of the ornamental borders that divide the scenes may portray members of the family, a hypothesis that the ruined state of these details allows neither to confirm nor to dismiss. One further archival curiosity deserves mention with all due caution: the Florentine burial and prosopographical records register a member of the family named, precisely, Giotto di Arnoldo Peruzzi, documented between 1292 and the 1340s. The name proves nothing about the commission — Giotto was not an uncommon name — but it is at least a suggestive emblem of the world of personal proximity, shared neighbourhood and intertwined fortunes within which such a commission was negotiated.

The sequel belongs to economic history, but it shadows the chapel's meaning. In the 1340s the defaults of Edward III of England on the enormous war loans advanced by the Florentine companies, compounding structural weaknesses at home, dragged the Peruzzi company into bankruptcy in 1343, with the Bardi following in 1346; Villani, ruined himself, described the collapse in apocalyptic tones. The family survived, diminished, and retained patronage rights over the chapel for centuries — a continuity that would matter again, for better and worse, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. But the paintings themselves remain a monument of the brief season in which Florentine merchant capital stood at the summit of the European economy and chose, as the instrument of its memory, the greatest painter alive.

## The Question of Chronology

No contract, payment record or chronicle entry dates the painting of the Peruzzi Chapel. Everything we can say about its chronology rests on three kinds of evidence, none decisive on its own: the early biographical tradition, the comparative logic of style, and, most recently, the material findings of technical examination. The earliest testimony is that of Lorenzo Ghiberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lorenzo-ghiberti" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, who recorded in his *Commentarii*, around the middle of the fifteenth century, that Giotto had painted four chapels and four polyptychs in Santa Croce — a statement of capital importance, since it implies that the two surviving cycles, Bardi and Peruzzi, are the remnants of a far larger Giottesque presence in the church. It was Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, in the *Lives* of 1550 and 1568, who first identified the four chapels by family: that of the Bardi with the stories of Saint Francis<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a>, that of the Peruzzi with the two Johns, and the lost decorations of the Giugni and Tosinghi-Spinelli chapels. Vasari singled out in the Peruzzi Chapel the dancing of Herodias's daughter and the promptness of the servants at Herod's table, and the resurrection of Drusiana with the Evangelist's ascent into heaven — praise that confirms the scenes were then fully visible, but offers nothing for their date.

Modern scholarship has therefore argued from style, and the argument has turned above all on the relation between the two neighbouring chapels. The Bardi cycle possesses an external anchor that the Peruzzi lacks: it includes Saint Louis of Toulouse<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-louis-toulouse" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a>, canonised in 1317, which establishes a terminus after which that cycle must fall. For the Peruzzi there is no such fixed point, and the twentieth century consequently produced a genuine oscillation. An older line of criticism, strong especially in the German and Anglo-American literature, considered the Peruzzi murals contemporary with or later than the Bardi frescoes, pushing them into the 1320s and even towards the early 1330s; a few voices went further still, associating their boldest spatial inventions with the experience of Giotto's Neapolitan years. Against this stood a reading, which gathered force from the 1960s onward and is particularly associated with Italian scholarship, that reversed the order: the Peruzzi Chapel came first, in the middle years of the 1310s, and the Bardi followed a decade later. The stylistic case for priority rests on observations that any attentive visitor can repeat. In the Peruzzi scenes the fictive architecture is built for a spectator standing at the chapel's entrance, the orthogonals of both walls converging towards a viewpoint outside the pictorial field — a unified, almost scenographic conception; in the Bardi Chapel the constructions are calibrated for a viewer within the space. The Peruzzi buildings, moreover, are expansive and sliced by the frame as though the city continued beyond it, while the Bardi settings return to a more contained, stage-like economy.

Within this broad realignment, [Ferdinando Bologna](/scholars.html#bologna-ferdinando)'s *Novità su Giotto. Giotto al tempo della cappella Peruzzi* of 1969 occupies a special place, both for its method and for its precision. Bologna set out from a little-studied panel of the Baptist's story, then in Dresden, which he connected to the dispersed altarpiece of the chapel; arguing for the substantial unity of panel and murals, and weaving together stylistic, typological and historical considerations, he proposed for the whole Peruzzi enterprise the remarkably tight date of 1317–18. He further argued — and here the book's title reveals its ambition — that the 'Peruzzi moment' was a watershed for Florentine and Tuscan painting at large, studied at once by the young Lorenzetti and [Bernardo Daddi](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Bernardo-Daddi.html), and even by older contemporaries such as the [Saint Cecilia Master](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Maestro-di-Santa-Cecilia.html) and [Pacino di Bonaguida](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Pacino-di-Bonaguida.html). Not all of Bologna's connections have held, but his insistence that the chapel marks a distinct and datable phase of Giotto's development, rather than a late appendix to it, has proved durable.

The present consensus, insofar as one exists, settles on the first half of the 1310s or shortly after. The catalogue of the Opera di Santa Croce proposes a date around 1310–1311; the most authoritative recent connoisseurship has gravitated towards 1310–1315, a bracket that also accommodates the related glass and panel painting of the church's east end; Italian heritage literature often prints the slightly later span of 1318–1322. The honest summary is that the cycle belongs to the decade after Giotto's return from [Padua](/Content/Churches/Cappella-degli-Scrovegni.html) and before the Bardi Chapel — that is, between roughly 1310 and the early 1320s — with the weight of current opinion towards the earlier end. It should finally be said that chronology here is not an antiquarian refinement. On it depends whether the Peruzzi Chapel is read as the laboratory in which Giotto's late style was invented — the source from which Taddeo Gaddi, Maso di Banco and ultimately Masaccio drew — or as a consequence of developments worked out elsewhere. The technical campaign of 2009–2013, undertaken precisely as 'a reconsideration of Giotto's technique, chronology, and patronage', was designed in part to bring new material evidence to this old question, and its implications are discussed below.

## A Programme for Two Johns

The pairing of the Baptist and the Evangelist on facing walls of a single funerary chapel is unusual, and its logic repays attention. The chapel's dedication embraced both saints — Vasari, writing when the altar furnishings had already changed, remembered it as dedicated to the Baptist, while the modern literature generally gives precedence to the Evangelist — and the choice almost certainly operated on several registers at once. If Giovanni di Rinieri Peruzzi was indeed the donor of the murals, then John the Evangelist was his name-saint, and the Evangelist's wall, with its serene miracles and its bodily assumption into heaven, becomes a personal profession of hope in salvation. John the Baptist, for his part, was the patron of Florence itself, the saint on whose feast the city's identity was annually performed and whose image stamped the florin<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-gold-florin" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a> on which the Peruzzi fortune was built; he was also, in Franciscan devotion, a figure insistently paired with Francis, the new herald of penance. A banking family thus inscribed into its burial chapel, in a single gesture, its own name, its city, and its allegiance to the order that housed its tombs. Scholarship has long recognised this interlacing of the devotional and the civic: a well-known American study published in *Renaissance Quarterly* in 1988 went so far as to read the cycle's sumptuous palaces, feasts and crowds as a deliberate mirror of the 'earthly city' of mercantile Florence — an interpretation that perhaps presses the imagery too hard, but which responds to something real in the frescoes' worldliness.

The two walls are built to answer one another. Each is divided into a lunette and two rectangular registers beneath, read from top to bottom, and the narrative arcs are set in deliberate counterpoint. Both lunettes concern annunciations of a kind: on the Baptist wall the angel appears to Zacharias at the altar of incense to announce an impossible birth; on the Evangelist wall the aged John, asleep on rocky Patmos, receives the visions of the Apocalypse. Both middle registers turn on the saint's power manifested in life — the naming of the infant John that looses Zacharias's tongue, the raising of Drusiana outside the walls of Ephesus. And the lowest registers confront two deaths that could not be more unlike: the Baptist's severed head carried into the obscene festivity of Herod's banquet, and the Evangelist rising bodily from his open grave into the embrace of Christ. The visitor standing between the walls is thus placed between martyrdom and assumption, between the wages of the world and the promise of heaven — an eloquent station for the tombs of bankers.

The narrative fields were only part of a larger decorative organism, much of it now lost or ruined. The webs of the quadripartite vault carried the symbols of the four Evangelists; the entrance archivolt still bears eight busts of prophets; and the ornamental bands that frame the scenes — simulated marble inlays and cosmatesque geometries of considerable refinement — include ten small hexagonal fields with the small heads noted above. On the window wall, flanking the lancet, survive the ghosts of standing figures, among them what appears to be John the Evangelist and a sainted bishop. An altarpiece completed the ensemble, and its probable identity is discussed in a later section.

One further dimension of the programme deserves notice: its geography. The painted settings are not generic. On the Evangelist wall the episodes unfold at Ephesus, and Giotto builds for Drusiana's miracle a crenellated city wall and a skyline of domes and towers that contemporaries could read as an evocation of the exotic East; a German study has indeed proposed that the architecture of the Peruzzi scenes was meant to conjure Ephesus as that of the Bardi Chapel's Trial by Fire conjures Cairo — an attempt, however filtered through convention, at representing the specific elsewhere in which sacred history occurred. Whether or not one accepts so pointed a reading, the ambition is unmistakable: these are stories staged in a world of real places, real buildings and real social rituals, observed with an appetite for the concrete that the fourteenth century had not seen before.

## The Baptist Wall

The left wall as one faces the window — the north wall of the chapel, towards the Cappella Maggiore — carries the three episodes from the life of the Baptist, selected with a severity that is itself significant. From the rich narrative tradition of the Precursor's life Giotto retained neither the preaching in the wilderness, nor the Baptism of Christ, nor the arrest or the prison: only the annunciation of his birth, the birth itself, and the catastrophe of Herod's banquet. The cycle thus opens and closes the Baptist's story at its two thresholds, and leaves everything between to the viewer's knowledge.

In the lunette, the *Annunciation to Zacharias* unfolds within and around a polygonal temple structure of remarkable complexity, its altar sheltered beneath a canopied ciborium. The aged priest stands at the altar of incense, censer in hand, as Gabriel appears beside him to announce that the barren Elizabeth will bear a son; outside the sanctuary, figures wait and converse, their inattention measuring the privacy of the miracle. The architecture, with its oblique recessions and its projecting porches, already announces the chapel's governing principle: buildings are not backdrops here but inhabitable structures, cut by the frame and angled into depth, which assign each figure a believable station in space.

The middle register compresses two moments of the Gospel narrative into a single domestic panorama: the *Birth of the Baptist* and the *Naming*. On one side Elizabeth rests in a curtained bed, attended by women who busy themselves with the practical rituals of the birth chamber; Giotto surrounds the bed with the furniture of a Florentine household — the chests ranged along the wall, the jugs standing in a niche — observed with a fidelity that the Opera di Santa Croce's catalogue rightly singles out as a manifesto of the painter's realism. On the other side sits Zacharias, struck dumb for his disbelief, writing on a tablet the name that will loose his tongue — 'John is his name' — while the swaddled infant is presented to him. The two episodes share one continuous architecture, so that the eye moves from the mother's bed to the father's tablet as through the rooms of a single house, and the theological point — the name divinely given, the prophecy fulfilled — arrives wrapped in the textures of ordinary bourgeois life.

The lowest register is the wall's dramatic summit and one of the most influential compositions of the fourteenth century: the *Feast of Herod*. Under the arcades of a palatial loggia the king's table is laid; at its end a musician bows a small vielle — the 'violetta' whose careful description the sources never tire of praising — and before the table a kneeling soldier presents the severed head of the Baptist on a charger. Salome, who has danced for this wage, appears again at the right, delivering the head to her mother Herodias in an adjoining chamber, the continuous architecture once more binding successive moments into one spatial truth. It is precisely this profane vivacity — the music, the service, the courtly protocol persisting around an atrocity — that gives the scene its moral charge. The horror is nowhere underlined; it is seated at dinner. Painters returned to this invention for more than a century: its descendants run from [Andrea Pisano](/Content/Artists/XIV-c/Andrea-Pisano.html)'s bronze doors for the Florentine Baptistery, through the Riminese and Florentine panel painters of the mid-Trecento, to Donatello's relief for the Siena font and Filippo Lippi's choir at Prato.

Across all three registers the condition of the surface demands honesty from the describer. The faces are abraded, whole passages of drapery survive only as preparatory layers, and the once-rich palette has sunk to a chalky evenness; the greens of the earths and the blues laid a secco have suffered most. Yet the UV documentation of 2009–2013 demonstrated that far more of Giotto's modelling survives beneath the opacified surface than the eye can register, and even in ordinary light the essentials endure: the grave largeness of the figures, draped in garments whose fall has repeatedly been called classical; and the unhurried intervals between them.

## The Evangelist Wall

The facing wall gives the Evangelist's story in three movements drawn from the apocryphal Acts of John as digested by the *Golden Legend*: the vision on Patmos, the raising of Drusiana at Ephesus, and the saint's death and bodily ascension. Here too the selection is pointed. Nothing of the Evangelist's youth, nothing of the Last Supper or Calvary where the Gospels place him; only the aged John of Asia Minor — seer, wonder-worker, and the one apostle whom tradition exempted from the corruption of the grave.

The lunette of *Saint John on Patmos* is, compositionally, the strangest and most modern image in the chapel. The exiled apostle sleeps seated on the rocky island, his head on his hand, while around him, suspended in the upper air, materialise the figures of his Apocalypse — among them the visionary woman and the dragon that threatens her child, and the angelic reapers of the harvest of the end of time. Giotto renders the island from above, a wedge of rock in a sea whose surface he troubles with patterned waves: the Opera's catalogue aptly speaks of a bird's-eye view, and the effect, with the small sleeping figure at the centre of a vast emptiness, is without real precedent in Italian mural painting. The scene also poses the cycle's most delicate problems of legibility, for the apparitions were laid in largely a secco over the blue of the sky, and what survives of them hovers at the threshold of visibility; several identifications remain open, and the ultraviolet photography of 2009–2013 recovered here some of its most spectacular hidden detail.

The central register, the *Raising of Drusiana*, was for posterity the most celebrated image of the chapel. Outside the walls of Ephesus the funeral procession of the pious widow Drusiana, who had longed to see John before her death, meets the returning apostle. John extends his right hand, and the dead woman, still in her shroud, rises on her bier to adore him; between the two groups — the bearers and mourners on one side, John and his companions on the other — the gesture of command crosses a charged interval of empty space. The catalogue of the Opera di Santa Croce justly draws attention to the 'sculptural vigour' of that outstretched hand. A small kneeling figure in contemporary dress, generally taken for a donor, inserts the patron family discreetly into the sacred event.

The lowest register gives the *Death and Ascension of Saint John the Evangelist*. According to the legend, John, having reached extreme old age, descended living into the grave prepared beside the altar of his church; when the tomb was opened it held no body. Giotto paints the moment of translation: within a lofty vaulted interior, the apostle rises from the open grave at the centre, borne upward on a stream of light towards the half-figure of Christ who leans down from a mandorla of glory, surrounded by the heavenly host, to grasp his beloved disciple. Around the gaping tomb the witnesses crane, kneel, shield their eyes; several of these figures — the bending onlookers seen from behind, the amazed elders at the right — became canonical studies of the human body under astonishment. The composition as a whole, with its continuous architecture and its vertical rush at the centre of a horizontal frieze, remained a touchstone for Florentine painters of the following two centuries.

Seen together, the two walls disclose the full range of Giotto's late rhetoric: the intimate (the birth chamber), the courtly (Herod's table), the visionary (Patmos), the forensic (Drusiana's miracle before witnesses), the triumphant (the Ascension). And they disclose, too, the structural intelligence that binds six fields into one room. The light within the painted scenes falls consistently as though from the real window of the chapel; the colours of the two cycles answer one another across the void. It is this unity of conception — more than any single figure — that constitutes the chapel's deepest lesson, and the one its Renaissance students most completely absorbed.

## A Masterpiece Painted on Dry Ground

The habit of calling the Peruzzi paintings 'frescoes' is so entrenched that it is worth pausing over the fact that, strictly speaking, they are not. In buon fresco the painter works into a patch of freshly laid lime plaster — the giornata, the day's work — while it is still damp, so that the pigments are bound into the carbonating lime itself and become, chemically, part of the wall. In painting a secco, by contrast, the colours are applied with an organic binder onto plaster that has already set: the paint sits upon the surface rather than within it. Giotto, like every muralist of his age, normally combined the two, executing the body of a scene in fresco and adding finishes, blues and refinements a secco. What makes the Peruzzi Chapel exceptional — the point was already evident to the restorers who uncovered it in the 1840s, and every technical examination since has confirmed it — is that here the proportion is inverted: the cycle was carried out largely on dry plaster over a fresco ground, the only case of its kind among Giotto's surviving mural works. [Cecilia Frosinini](/scholars.html#frosinini-cecilia), who coordinated the Opificio delle Pietre Dure's recent investigations, has nonetheless cautioned that the blanket label simplifies a subtler reality: the examinations revealed notable differences of procedure from one part of the cycle to another, and the contamination of the surfaces by successive restorations has made a definitive scientific characterisation of the original technique genuinely difficult.

Why Giotto proceeded this way is a question the sources never answer, and the modern explanations fall into two families that are not mutually exclusive. The first is practical: painting on dry plaster emancipates the workshop from the tyranny of the giornata. Fresco demands that each day's patch be finished before it dries and that the design be partitioned in advance along its seams; a secco work can proceed in broad campaigns, be corrected, left and resumed — a flexibility that would have mattered to a master whose services were contested by half the courts of Italy. The second explanation is aesthetic: on a dry ground, with an organic medium, the painter commands effects closer to those of panel painting — denser shadows, subtler transitions, more saturated and more varied colour — and it has repeatedly been suggested that in the Peruzzi Chapel Giotto was deliberately pursuing a refinement of surface and tone that fresco could not give. The ultraviolet images, discussed below, lend this old intuition unexpected support. If this was an experiment, it was an experiment in making a chapel glow like an immense polyptych.

The price of the experiment was permanence. Paint bound with organic media holds to the wall only as long as its binder survives; it is vulnerable to damp, to mechanical abrasion, and above all to exactly the treatments — soaking, scraping, solvents — that the nineteenth century would inflict. Much of what is lost in the Peruzzi Chapel was lost not gradually but in a few catastrophic seasons of cleaning, when layers that fresco would have withstood came away with the whitewash. What remains in many passages is the preparatory stage rather than the finished skin: terre verte underlayers where flesh once was, bare plaster where azurite skies have fallen, the ghost of a design whose completion must now be supplied by instruments or imagination.

It is here that the technique rejoins the question of chronology and of the two chapels' relation, and here too that a persistent error in the popular literature needs correcting. One frequently reads that the neighbouring Bardi Chapel was painted in the same way, or else wholly in true fresco; the technical campaigns of the Opificio show a difference of degree rather than of kind. The Bardi cycle is built on a fresco ground laid in giornate, with extensive finishing a secco, but with far more of the work done in true fresco — which is precisely why its nineteenth-century uncovering proceeded so much more easily, and why its surfaces, for all their losses, retain a coherence the Peruzzi walls lack. The two chapels thus document, in adjacent spaces and within at most a decade or two, two different conceptions of what mural painting should be; and whichever came first, the contrast is one of the most instructive technical juxtapositions that the entire Trecento affords.

## Oblivion, Rediscovery, Repainting, Removal

For roughly a century the chapel ceased to exist as a work of Giotto at all. In the first half of the eighteenth century, in the course of the general refurbishment of the transept of Santa Croce according to the taste of the age, the painted walls of the Peruzzi Chapel were covered with whitewash; the operation is not precisely documented, but the literature converges on a date before the 1750s, and a restoration of the chapel recorded in 1714 has sometimes been proposed as the occasion. It is worth being exact about what this was and was not. The whitewashing was not an act of singular vandalism against Giotto's memory, nor — as a popular retelling has it — a capricious 'redecoration' by the family alone; it belonged to a systematic modernisation that treated the trecento murals of the whole church as outdated wall-covering. The Bardi Chapel suffered the same fate, and so, irrecoverably, did whatever survived in the Giugni and Tosinghi-Spinelli chapels.

The nineteenth century reversed the judgment. In the climate of the Risorgimento, which enthroned Giotto as the founding father of Italian art, the knowledge — kept alive by Vasari's text — that his paintings slept under the lime of Santa Croce became intolerable. The Peruzzi family itself took part in the reawakening: already in 1826 Vincenzo Peruzzi was contemplating a new decoration of the chapel, and it was under the family's patronage that the recovery of the originals was eventually undertaken. The campaign to uncover the murals began in 1841, entrusted to the painter-restorer Antonio Marini, and proved, in the words of the modern technical literature, exceptionally long and difficult — precisely because the paintings, being bound a secco, clung to the whitewash that was being removed. Marini worked with the mechanical and chemical arsenal of his time, scrapers and solvents, and the operation that resurrected the cycle also mutilated it: original surfaces came away with the lime, and what emerged was a wounded ruin. Marini then did what every restorer of his generation considered a duty — he repainted. Losses were filled, contours reinforced, whole passages reconstructed in a diligent Purist approximation of Giotto, so that the chapel could be read from the floor as a complete decoration. He continued to attend to the chapel until his death in 1861; meanwhile, in 1852–1853, Gaetano Bianchi performed the analogous uncovering of the Bardi Chapel, where the true fresco technique made the task far kinder to the original.

It was this hybrid object — Giotto's wreck completed by Marini's brush — that the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries knew, reproduced, and loved. The engravings and photographs of the period, and the eloquent pages of the Victorian critics, describe compositions considerably more legible than anything Giotto's own hand had left; when E. M. Forster's Lucy Honeychurch stood in Santa Croce in 1908, 'the Giottos' before her were in substantial measure the Giottos of Antonio Marini. The scholarly history of the chapel in this period is thus inseparable from the history of its falsification, and more than one critical judgment about Giotto's late style was formed, unknowingly, on nineteenth-century paint.

The reckoning came after the Second World War, with the triumph of the modern doctrine of restoration: the conviction that only the authentic original, however fragmentary, has the right to the wall. Between 1958 and 1961 [Leonetto Tintori](/scholars.html#tintori-leonetto), working under the supervision of [Ugo Procacci](/scholars.html#procacci-ugo) and the Florentine Soprintendenza, removed from the Peruzzi Chapel everything that was not Giotto — Marini's integrations, the oil-based retouchings, the accumulated repaints of a century — and consolidated what remained, leaving the losses as neutral, undisguised lacunae. The same principles were applied to the Bardi Chapel in 1957–58 (1957–59 according to the Opera di Santa Croce), where Bianchi's far more extensive reconstructions were likewise sacrificed. The operation was documented by Procacci in an official report and furnished the material basis for the first modern monograph on the chapel, Eve Borsook's *Giotto: The Peruzzi Chapel* of 1965, which fixed the state of knowledge for a generation. The result on the walls, however, was austere to the point of desolation: journalists would later describe the stripped paintings as faint and anaemic, a patient uncovered but not healed. The de-restoration of the two Giotto chapels remains to this day a locus classicus in the ethics of conservation — the place where the nineteenth century's duty to complete and the twentieth century's duty to tell the truth confront each other on facing walls — and no account of what the visitor now sees is honest without it: the Peruzzi Chapel's present nakedness is itself an artefact of history, the deliberate choice of 1958–1961 no less than the involuntary legacy of 1841.

## The Invisible Giotto: Diagnostics in the Twenty-First Century

After 1961 the chapel entered a long convalescence of study rather than intervention, and the decisive new chapter opened in 2009, when the Opificio delle Pietre Dure, with the support of the Getty Foundation and in collaboration with Villa I Tatti, the Harvard University Center for Italian Renaissance Studies, launched a campaign of wholly non-invasive diagnostics on the Peruzzi walls. A team of more than a dozen conservators and scientists, working from scaffolding that filled the twelve-metre height of the chapel, set out to map the condition of the paintings millimetre by millimetre — the surviving original, the losses, the residues of every earlier treatment — using the full battery of imaging techniques then available.

The campaign's most famous result arrived, by the team's own account, partly by chance. When the surfaces were examined under ultraviolet light, the ruined paintings underwent a transformation that startled the examiners themselves: passages opaque, faded and illegible to the naked eye sprang into dense, contrasted visibility. Cecilia Frosinini, who coordinated the project, described to the Corriere della Sera the moment at which everything uncertain on the walls became suddenly clear and full of contrast; the superintendent of the Opificio announced to the press the recovery of 'un Giotto segreto'. The scientific explanation, as Frosinini has set out in her research reports, lies in the chapel's anomalous technique: the organic binding media that Giotto used for his a secco painting, interacting with particular pigments, fluoresce under ultraviolet radiation, so that even where the visible colour has perished, the invisible residue of the medium still holds the image. The very choice that doomed the paintings' surface thus preserved, beneath it, a latent record of their finish. What the UV photographs restored to sight was not a different Giotto but an incomparably finer one: fully modelled faces where the eye sees blurs, the chiaroscuro that gives the figures back their three-dimensionality, ornament, textiles and architectural detail of panel-painting delicacy. The researchers made the inevitable observation that they were seeing, for the first time in centuries, something close to what Masaccio and Michelangelo had seen.

The documentation programme that followed was systematic. The entire cycle was photographed in diffused visible light and the two chapels' techniques were studied comparatively, with one scene from each chosen as an exemplary case — the *Apparition at Arles* for the Bardi, the *Raising of Drusiana* for the Peruzzi — precisely because each concentrates the technical features and the pathologies of its cycle. Frosinini carried the analysis forward during research fellowships at the Center for Advanced Study in the Visual Arts in Washington in 2012 and 2013, framing the material as the basis for a study of the master's late working methods — the organisation of the worksite, the division of labour with the shop — that the written sources will never supply. Multispectral acquisitions were subsequently carried out with the collaboration of a bioimaging institute, and the results reached the public in spectacular form at the exhibition *Giotto, l'Italia* at Palazzo Reale in Milan (September 2015 – January 2016), where a full-scale virtual evocation of the chapel allowed visitors to watch the walls pass from their present state into their ultraviolet richness — the nearest thing to time travel that the technology of the moment allowed, and the partial fulfilment of the team's early hope of a 'virtual chapel' for the general public.

It must be stressed, against a misunderstanding the press coverage sometimes encouraged, that none of this constituted a restoration: nothing was added to or removed from the walls, and the Peruzzi Chapel today looks as Tintori left it. The campaign's purpose was knowledge — a complete condition map as the precondition of any future treatment, and a body of evidence for the historians. Its sequel, for the moment, has unfolded next door: between June 2022 and September 2026 the Opificio carried out, with the Opera di Santa Croce, the full study and restoration of the Bardi Chapel, unveiled on 11 September 2026 for the Franciscan centenary, drawing on the comparative knowledge of the two cycles that the 2009–2013 campaign created. At the Bardi, moreover, the restorers chose this time to reintegrate the painted framing architecture for the sake of legibility, leaving the lost figures unreconstructed, so that the two chapels, stripped alike at mid-century, no longer present the same face. The Peruzzi Chapel, more fragile and more enigmatic, waits with its dossier complete; whatever is eventually decided, it will be decided in the light — in every sense — of what the ultraviolet lamps revealed.

## The Dismembered Ensemble: The Altarpiece and the Furnishings

Ghiberti's notice that Giotto supplied Santa Croce with four altarpieces as well as four chapels invites the obvious question: where is the Peruzzi altarpiece? Since the mid-twentieth century the most credited answer has pointed across the Atlantic. The North Carolina Museum of Art in Raleigh preserves, as a gift of the Samuel H. Kress Foundation received in 1961, a five-part polyptych of tempera and gilded gesso on poplar, just over a metre high and two and a half metres wide, showing the blessing Christ at the centre between Saint John the Evangelist, the Virgin, Saint John the Baptist and Saint Francis; the museum dates it around 1310–1315 and exhibits it under the name by which scholarship has long known it, the 'Peruzzi Altarpiece'. The identification rests on converging circumstantial evidence rather than on any document. The cast of saints is exactly what the Peruzzi Chapel requires — the two Johns of the dedication flanking Christ and the Virgin, with Francis for the church that housed the chapel — and the polyptych's width is compatible with the chapel's altar; the proposal that the five panels, dispersed and reunited in the Kress collection, originally formed one altarpiece destined for the chapel was advanced in the mid-twentieth-century literature and has been repeated, with varying degrees of conviction, ever since. The honest formulation remains conditional: it cannot be excluded — many would say it is likely — that the Raleigh polyptych stood on the Peruzzi altar; it cannot be proved.

The polyptych's attribution has oscillated in step with the general fortunes of Giotto connoisseurship. Earlier critics gave it to a pupil; the Kress catalogues to Giotto and assistants; Ferdinando Bologna, in the 1969 study already discussed, went panel by panel, keeping the Christ, the Baptist and the Francis for the master himself and conceding workshop collaboration in the Virgin and the Evangelist — a distribution that captures well the work's uneven but at best very high quality. The association of the Dresden Baptist panel with the altar has remained debated, and the more recent tendency, arguing from style, has been to push the Raleigh polyptych back towards 1310 — with evident consequences, if the Peruzzi connection holds, for the dating of the murals themselves. However the details are resolved, the broader picture is coherent: panel painting, wall painting and glass were conceived at Santa Croce as a single Giottesque campaign, and the surviving stained glass of the church's east end has in fact been dated to the early 1310s precisely by comparison with the Peruzzi murals.

Of the chapel's own medieval furnishings, almost nothing else remains in place. The lancet window now holds glass that is itself a distinguished exile: a window brought from the Giugni Chapel — one of the two lost Giotto chapels — and attributed to Pacino di Bonaguida, its foreign provenance betrayed, as the Opera's literature notes, by the heraldry at its summit. The altar and its dossal are modern arrangements. And the chapel's later history as a family mausoleum has left one conspicuous nineteenth-century intruder: the monument to Vincenzo Peruzzi, gonfaloniere of Florence, carved by Odoardo Fantacchiotti between 1847 and 1860 and set against the right wall — a work exactly contemporary with Marini's repainting, and a reminder that the family whose chapel had been whitewashed in the eighteenth century was also the family that paid, in the nineteenth, to have its Giotto back. The Peruzzi Chapel as the Trecento knew it — murals, altarpiece, glass, tombs and liturgical apparatus composing one instrument of memory — must today be reassembled in the mind, from a wall in Florence, a polyptych in Raleigh, and the testimony of Ghiberti that there was once far more.

## Fortuna: From the Trecento Workshops to Michelangelo

The influence of the Peruzzi Chapel began before its scaffolding came down. Giotto's own Florentine school was formed in large part on these walls: Taddeo Gaddi, who painted the neighbouring Baroncelli Chapel in the 1330s, and Maso di Banco, whose Saint Sylvester cycle in the Bardi di Vernio Chapel of the same church carries the Peruzzi manner of grave figures within ample painted architecture to its most refined conclusion, are both unthinkable without it. Individual filiations of the kind Bologna proposed for the Lorenzetti, Daddi and the older painters named above are inevitably arguable; the general claim is not. The compositional types invented here — the Herod feast, the public miracle before a city wall, the ascension bursting vertically through a horizontal frieze — became common property of Tuscan painting within a generation.

The chapel's second life began with the Quattrocento's return to Giotto. The tradition that Masaccio studied the Peruzzi murals is not documented by any text, but it has seemed compelling to virtually every historian who has set the Brancacci Chapel beside the Evangelist wall: the weighted stance of the figures, the unified light, the architecture entered rather than ornamented — the shortest path from Giotto to Masaccio runs through this room. For Michelangelo, uniquely, the document exists. A sheet in the Louvre, generally dated to the early 1490s when the young sculptor was frequenting the Florentine churches sketchbook in hand, copies in pen two of the standing witnesses from the *Ascension of Saint John the Evangelist* — the draped, monumental bystanders at the right of the scene — translating Giotto's figures into the probing cross-hatched idiom of the new age. Condivi and Vasari both record that Michelangelo's apprenticeship included the copying of Giotto and Masaccio; the Louvre drawing shows the claim being enacted, and shows too what Michelangelo took: not the composition but the gravitas, the body as a column of draped weight. When the drawing travelled to the great Michelangelo exhibitions of recent decades, it carried the Peruzzi Chapel with it as an invisible lender.

The subsequent critical fortune of the chapel is a barometer of Giotto studies generally. For Vasari it was one proof among many of Giotto's universal renewal of painting; for the eighteenth century it was expendable wall-surface; for the Risorgimento, a national relic to be exhumed at any cost; for the Victorian pilgrims who followed Murray and Baedeker into Santa Croce, a station of the aesthetic education. In the twentieth century the chapel acquired a new and paradoxical centrality: as the attribution wars over Assisi divided scholarship into 'Giotto' and 'non-Giotto' camps, the Santa Croce chapels stood with Padua in the small canon of works whose substance no serious party doubted, and thus became part of the very yardstick by which the contested works were measured. That a cycle so ruined should serve as a standard of authenticity is an irony the Peruzzi Chapel wears lightly; it is also the reason why every gain in its legibility — Tintori's honesty, Frosinini's ultraviolet — reverberates far beyond its walls, into the definition of what 'Giotto' means.

## Conclusion

The Peruzzi Chapel asks of its students a double discipline: to see what is there, and to reason rigorously about what is not. What is there is a space of modest architecture transfigured by six narrative fields whose design, even in ruin, exhibits the fullest maturity of the painter who refounded European painting: figures of classical weight set in architecture that behaves like the world, a light that obeys the chapel's own window, a narrative economy that trusts the essential gesture — Zacharias's stylus, the Evangelist's extended hand — to carry the whole burden of meaning. What is not there is almost everything else: the finished surface, dissolved by technique and restoration; the altarpiece, in Raleigh if the prevailing hypothesis holds; the original glass; the documents that would date the enterprise; the two lost companion chapels that would let us judge it, with the Bardi, as one movement in a four-part Giottesque symphony at Santa Croce.

Between these two poles, the epistemic registers have to be kept distinct, because the chapel's literature, more than most, suffers when they blur. It is documented that Donato di Arnaldo Peruzzi endowed a chapel in the unbuilt new church in 1292, and that the murals were uncovered from whitewash beginning in 1841 and stripped of their repaints in 1958–1961. It is the testimony of the early sources — Ghiberti, then Vasari — that Giotto painted four chapels and four altarpieces in Santa Croce, of which this is one survivor. It is tradition, plausible but unproven, that names Giovanni di Rinieri Peruzzi as donor of the paintings. It is scholarly consensus, resting on style, that the cycle belongs to the years between Padua and the Bardi Chapel, with current opinion favouring the earlier 1310s; and it is demonstrated fact, owed to the diagnostics of 2009–2013, that the cycle was executed in a mixed technique, largely a secco, and that an astonishing quantity of its lost refinement survives, latently, in the fluorescence of its media. By the same token, certain hardy statements of the popular literature should be retired: the paintings are not frescoes in the strict sense, being largely a secco; the Bardi Chapel, though also finished extensively a secco, is painted far more in true fresco than this one; no document says that Giotto painted here 'in about 1320'; and the eighteenth-century whitewashing was an episode of general refurbishment, not a singular act of family philistinism.

In the end the chapel's deepest significance may lie precisely in its battered condition. Nowhere else can one watch so clearly the entire life-cycle of a medieval masterpiece: the mercantile piety that willed it into being before its walls existed; the experimental audacity of its making; the changing devotions that buried it; the romantic nationalism that exhumed and falsified it; the modern conscience that stripped it back to truth; and the twenty-first-century instruments that now read, beneath a surface the eye has given up, the finish that Michelangelo saw. The Peruzzi Chapel is at once a work of Giotto and a seven-hundred-year lesson in what time, money, piety, error and science do to works of Giotto. Few rooms in Europe teach so much.

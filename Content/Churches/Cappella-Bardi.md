## Introduction: A Chapel at the Heart of European Painting

To pass from the south transept of Santa Croce through a tall Gothic arch into the first chapel to the right of the chancel is to enter one of the foundational rooms of European painting. The Bardi Chapel, dedicated to Saint Francis of Assisi<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-francis-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a>, preserves the cycle of the saint's life that [Giotto di Bondone](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html) painted for the city's greatest banking dynasty at the height of his maturity, certainly after 1317 and, in the judgement of most scholars, in the course of the 1320s. Six scenes survive within the chapel — two lunettes, two middle fields and two lower fields facing one another across the narrow space — and a seventh composition, the Stigmatization, is painted on the transept wall above the entrance arch; the chapel also holds allegorical figures in the vault and standing saints on the window wall. It is a modest room by the standards of Giotto's earlier undertakings: a single rib-vaulted bay, tall and narrow, lit by lancet windows above the altar. Yet within it the painter condensed everything he had learned about narrative, about the weight of the human figure, and about the architecture of feeling, into a meditation on the poverty saint commissioned, with an irony that has never ceased to exercise historians, by men whose fortune rested on the lending of money. The seven scenes are illustrated in the section on the Bardi Chapel in the entry on [Giotto di Bondone](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giotto-di-Bondone.html#the-bardi-chapel-the-life-of-saint-francis-in-seven-scenes).

The chapel's claim on our attention has always been double. It is, first, a primary document of Giotto's late style, the fullest surviving statement of his Florentine mural painting together with the neighbouring [Peruzzi Chapel](/Content/Churches/Cappella-Peruzzi.html); the early sources are unanimous that Santa Croce was the theatre of his most admired work in the city. Lorenzo Ghiberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lorenzo-ghiberti" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, writing in the middle of the fifteenth century, recorded that Giotto painted at the church of the Frati Minori "quattro capelle e quattro tavole" — four chapels and four panels — without troubling to name them. Giorgio Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a>, a century later, supplied the names: the chapel of the Bardi with the stories of Saint Francis, the Peruzzi with the two Saints John, the Giugni with the martyrdoms of the Apostles, and the Tosinghi–Spinelli, on the north side, with stories of the Virgin. Of these four only the Bardi and Peruzzi cycles survive; the other two were lost, and their disappearance has made the two remaining chapels, standing side by side at the head of the south transept, the twin pillars on which every account of Giotto's final manner must rest.

It is, second, a document of extraordinary resilience. Whitewashed in the early eighteenth century, pierced by funerary monuments in the early nineteenth, rediscovered and exuberantly repainted in the 1850s, stripped back to its authentic remains in the late 1950s, the cycle has lived as many lives as the controversies it depicts. Its most recent life began in June 2022, when the Opificio delle Pietre Dure opened a four-year campaign of study and restoration that concluded on 11 September 2026; the chapel reopened to the public the following day, in the eighth centenary year of the saint's death — the octocentenary of the Transitus of Francis, who died at the Porziuncola in October 1226. The campaign has changed, in points both small and fundamental, what can responsibly be said about the chapel: about its technique, its workshop, its colours, even the objects its figures hold. An account written today must therefore do two things at once. It must give an account of the chapel as the critical tradition has understood it — its patrons, its programme, its sources, its place in Giotto's career — and it must register, with appropriate caution, what the new findings confirm, correct or overturn. Throughout, what is documented has to be distinguished scrupulously from what is attributed, what is hypothesis and what is legend, resisting the accretion of confident error that gathers around famous monuments like oxalate on a fresco.

## The Bardi and the Ethics of Wealth

The family that gave the chapel its name stood, in the opening decades of the fourteenth century, at the summit of European finance. The Compagnia dei Bardi was among the largest merchant-banking houses the medieval West produced, with branches and correspondents distributed across Italy, France, England, Flanders and the Mediterranean, and with clients who included the papacy and the crowns of Naples and England. Together with the Peruzzi and the Acciaiuoli, the Bardi formed the trio that contemporaries and later historians alike would treat as the pillars of Florentine banking — and whose spectacular collapse in the 1340s, precipitated by the insolvency of Edward III of England amid the opening campaigns of the Hundred Years War, would become a byword for the fragility of credit. The Bardi failure is generally placed in 1346, following that of the Peruzzi; the enormous loan figures that circulate in the popular literature derive from the chronicle tradition and cannot be verified from surviving account books, and they are deliberately omitted here. What matters for the chapel is that at the moment of its decoration, a generation before the crash, the family's wealth and standing in Florence were at their height.

That standing was inscribed physically into Santa Croce. The Bardi held the patronage rights of no fewer than four chapels in the basilica — a concentration without parallel among the Florentine families who colonised the church's east end — of which the chapel of Saint Francis beside the chancel was the most prestigious. The individual patron of the Franciscan chapel is usually identified as Ridolfo di Bartolo de' Bardi, head of the company in these years; it must be said plainly that this identification, though overwhelmingly probable, is not documented by any surviving contract or payment. The attribution of the patronage rests on the family's known rights over the chapel and on the testimony of Vasari, who in the 1568 edition of the *Vite* calls it the chapel "di messer Ridolfo de' Bardi," adding, with his eye for the practical topography of the church, that it was the one "where the bell-ropes are". The Opera di Santa Croce itself, in its current materials, qualifies Ridolfo as the *likely* patron, and that is the honest formulation.

The choice of subject has always carried a charge. That a dynasty of bankers should pay for the most ambitious Florentine cycle of the life of the apostle of poverty — a saint who stripped himself naked in the public square rather than touch his father's money — is a paradox so pointed that it has shaped the entire interpretative literature on the chapel. The paradox should not, however, be overdrawn into anachronism. The great mendicant churches of the Italian cities were built and furnished precisely by mercantile wealth; the friars were the confessors, preachers and, not infrequently, the arbiters of conscience of the banking class; and the moral theology of the age offered the men of the *compagnie* a well-worn path by which the profits of exchange, always shadowed by the suspicion of usury, might be laundered before God through restitution, almsgiving and pious foundation. A chapel in the family's own parish basilica, endowed for masses and adorned with the life of the order's founder, was at once an act of devotion, an instrument of salvation, and a public statement of the family's place in the city. Whether it was also, more specifically, an act of expiation for usury is a seductive reading that the documents neither confirm nor exclude. [Rona Goffen](/scholars.html#goffen-rona), whose 1988 monograph remains the fullest study of the chapel's meaning, situated the commission within exactly this field of tension between Franciscan poverty and Florentine money, and her account of how the cycle negotiates that tension will concern us below. Here it is enough to insist on the epistemic layering: the family's patronage of the chapel is secure; Ridolfo's personal role is probable; the motives are interpretation.

One further circumstance deserves notice. The Bardi were not decorating a neutral space but claiming a position. The chapels flanking the chancel of Santa Croce were the most honorific burial and commemorative sites in Franciscan Florence, and the families who held them were engaged in an unmistakable, if decorous, competition of magnificence. That both turned to the same painter, the most famous in Italy, and that the two cycles answer one another across the transept in scale, in format and in ambition, is the precondition for everything the following sections describe.

## Santa Croce, the Chancel Chapels and the Question of Date

The basilica of Santa Croce, begun in the last years of the thirteenth century on the site of earlier Franciscan churches and traditionally associated with the design of [Arnolfo di Cambio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Arnolfo-di-Cambio.html), was conceived on a scale that announced the Friars Minor as a central institution of the Florentine commune. Its east end departs from the long apsidal choirs of northern Gothic: a vast transept opens behind the nave, and along its eastern wall a row of ten chapels flanks the tall polygonal chancel, five on each side, each the property and the responsibility of a patrician house. The arrangement turned the head of the church into a map of the city's wealth. Alberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-alberti-family" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> in the chancel itself, Bardi and Peruzzi in the places of honour immediately to its right, Baroncelli at the end of the south arm, and a procession of other banking and mercantile names along the row: the friars' church of poverty was, from the moment of its construction, a monument raised and maintained by profit. The paradox noted in the previous section was thus structural before it was iconographic, and no family embodied it more completely than the Bardi, whose chapel stands first at the chancel's right hand.

Into this setting Giotto was called not once but four times. Ghiberti's laconic "quattro capelle e quattro tavole" and Vasari's enumeration of the Bardi, Peruzzi, Giugni and Tosinghi–Spinelli chapels have already been cited; nothing beyond the early descriptions allows the reconstruction of the two lost cycles. A word of caution is needed about chronology. A date of 1318 for the beginning of Giotto's work in Santa Croce circulates widely in the popular literature and is sometimes fathered on Ghiberti; Ghiberti gives no date at all, and no document records the commission or the execution of any of the four chapels. The claim should be retired.

What can be said about the date of the Bardi Chapel rests on a single fixed point and a cluster of converging arguments. The fixed point is the figure of Saint Louis of Toulouse<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-louis-toulouse" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> on the window wall: the Angevin prince-bishop was canonised in 1317, and his presence among the chapel's saints establishes that year as a firm *terminus post quem* for the decoration — or at least for that wall. Beyond this, certainty ends and estimation begins. The institutional literature of the recent restoration frames the possibilities widely, from a bracket beginning in 1317–21 to a date approaching Giotto's return from Naples in 1333; the Opera di Santa Croce's own chapel page prefers "certainly after 1317 and possibly before the end of 1325," while its captions print c. 1317–21. The scholarly mainstream, as the technical literature accompanying the restoration summarises it, places the cycle in the 1320s, and several contributors to the Opificio's diagnostic volume treat Giotto's departure for Naples in 1328 as the probable *terminus ante quem*. A minority position has argued for a date after the Naples years; the attribution of that view to specific publications is entangled in the secondary literature and is not asserted here. The working formula is "c. 1325, after 1317", a formula that sits within every responsible bracket.

The relative chronology of the two surviving chapels is a separate and livelier question. The older critical tradition was divided: an influential study of the mid-1960s argued that the Bardi preceded the Peruzzi, reading the Peruzzi's bolder spatial experiments as the later development, while the prevailing modern view reverses the order, placing the Peruzzi Chapel first — in some formulations as early as the first half of the 1310s — and understanding the Bardi as the more concentrated, more austere statement of a style already fully formed. The stylistic arguments cut both ways, and the technical evidence now available complicates rather than settles them: the two chapels share a working method in which painting on fresh plaster is extensively supplemented by finishing on the dry wall, differing in degree rather than in kind, as the section on the late style will describe. What is not in doubt is that the two cycles were conceived within a single artistic horizon, by the same master and largely the same workshop, for two allied families in adjacent spaces; whichever came first, each was painted in full consciousness of the other.

## The Space and the Dossal

The chapel itself is a single Gothic bay, tall and narrow, closed by a rib vault and lit from the east by lancet windows above the altar. Figures published at the time of the recent restoration give its height as about twelve metres against a width of little more than four and a depth of about five and a half; these dimensions derive from press materials rather than from a published survey and are recorded here with that caveat. The proportions matter more than the numbers. The room is a vertical shaft of space, and Giotto's response to it — stacking two broad horizontal fields and a lunette on each side wall, so that the narrative reads as a calm architecture of rectangles rising to the pointed crown — is itself an act of design. The painted framing system, with its fictive cosmatesque bands and its continuous moulded cornices, binds the six interior scenes into a single envelope; the restorers of 2022–26, who recovered its logic and re-presented it in a lightened, legible tone, describe the effect as that of a great ciborium erected around the worshipper.

The chapel opens directly beneath the campanile — Vasari's chapel "where the bell-ropes are" — and the tower's company has not been kind. A lightning strike on the bell tower in 1512 sent water through the fabric and inflicted lasting damage on the window wall, which remains the most compromised surface in the chapel; the fragmentary state of its painted saints, discussed below, owes much to that single night. Of the chapel's original furnishings little survives in place. The screening grille that once closed the entrance was removed around 1613. Of the two stained-glass windows, the rear light is not original to the chapel at all but was transferred from the Velluti Chapel after the Second World War. The nineteenth century contributed, and the twentieth century removed, the pair of neoclassical cenotaphs whose installation and extraction left their own scars on the frescoes, as the conservation history in the section on fortune and conservation will relate.

On the altar stands — or stood, until the restoration required its temporary removal to the transept — one of the most important panels of the Florentine Duecento: the great dossal of Saint Francis flanked by twenty scenes of his life and miracles, painted around 1245–50, within a generation of the saint's death. The panel's authorship has its own historiography in miniature. Long given to [Cimabue](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Cimabue.html) in the older literature, then assigned to an anonymous convenience-master christened the [Maestro del San Francesco Bardi](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Maestro-del-San-Francesco-Bardi.html), it is attributed in the Opera di Santa Croce's current catalogue to [Coppo di Marcovaldo](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Coppo-di-Marcovaldo.html), the most powerful Florentine painter of the mid-thirteenth century; a tradition records that it may have been commissioned by the Tedaldi family. Two facts about it are easily misstated and worth fixing. First, the panel is roughly three-quarters of a century older than the frescoes that surround it: Giotto did not paint his cycle around this image, nor was the dossal conceived for this room. Second, its presence on the Bardi altar is documented only from 1595; where it stood in the church before that date is uncertain. The dialogue that every modern visitor experiences — the hieratic, wide-eyed Francis of the Duecento icon, ringed by his miracles in the old narrative shorthand, confronting the grave, corporeal Francis of Giotto's walls — is therefore a dialogue arranged by the late sixteenth century, not by the fourteenth. It is no less instructive for that. The juxtaposition has become part of the chapel's meaning, and the restoration's decision to return the dossal to the altar preserves it.

The dedication of the chapel to Saint Francis himself, rather than to a secondary Franciscan devotion, completes the claim of precedence noted earlier: the Bardi had secured not merely a chapel but the chapel, and the decoration they paid for treats its subject with corresponding gravity.

## The Narrative Programme: Six Scenes in Three Registers

The six interior scenes are disposed with a symmetry so deliberate that the programme can be read as a set of paired meditations before it is read as a sequence. On the left wall, descending, stand the Renunciation of Worldly Goods, the Apparition at Arles, and the Death and Ascension of Saint Francis; on the right wall, descending, the Approval of the Rule, the Trial by Fire before the Sultan, and the Visions of Fra Agostino and of Bishop Guido of Assisi. The chronological thread thus zig-zags across the chapel — Renunciation to Approval, Arles to Egypt, deathbed to the visions that attended the death — but the governing logic is vertical and antiphonal. The two lunettes set the saint's two decisive submissions face to face: on the left he strips himself of the world, on the right the Church receives and sanctions what he has founded. The middle register pairs two manifestations of his word and presence — among his own friars gathered in chapter at Arles, and before the unbelievers at the court of the Sultan. The lowest register, at eye level where the beholder stands closest, is given entirely to the threshold of death: the body mourned on one wall, the spirit witnessed on the other. The arrangement is not a chronicle but an argument, and the argument moves downward from vocation through mission to transitus.

The textual foundation of the cycle is Bonaventure<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-bonaventure" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>'s *Legenda maior*, the official life composed in the early 1260s and imposed by the order, which in 1266 decreed the suppression of the earlier biographies. Every episode in the chapel has its warrant there, and several details are intelligible only through it. In the Renunciation, the young Francis, having returned his very clothes to his father, stands naked but for the mantle in which the bishop of Assisi wraps him — Bonaventure's image of the Church covering the newborn religious life — while Pietro di Bernardone, restrained by a bystander, strains toward his son across a gulf that Giotto renders as empty painted sky. In the lunette opposite, Francis and his first companions kneel before the enthroned pope, whose identity is read in two ways. The materials of the 2026 restoration read him as Innocent III<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-innocent-iii" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> and title the scene the verbal approval of the first Rule, which the *Legenda maior* places in his pontificate. Earlier criticism read the scene, for the most part, as the confirmation of the Rule by Honorius III<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-pope-honorius-iii" class="footnote"><sup>8</sup></a> with the bull of 1223, assimilating it to the corresponding scene at Assisi. The restoration has added a sly piece of possible corroboration for the first reading: cleaning revealed that the carpet on which the friars kneel bears an eagle motif, and it has been observed in the first published accounts that the eagle figured in the arms of the counts of Segni, Innocent's family — a coincidence "perhaps not accidental," though it should be weighed as a suggestion, not a proof.

The middle register turns on presence. At Arles, while Anthony of Padua preaches to the friars assembled in chapter, Francis — absent in the body, hundreds of miles away — appears at the centre of the room with arms extended in the form of the cross, visible to one friar alone. Giotto stages the miracle inside a single boxed interior whose rear wall, it now emerges, originally carried a painted crucifix: the lead-mapping campaign of the recent restoration recovered its ghost beneath the surface, invisible to the naked eye, so that the cruciform saint once appeared against the image of the Crucified himself. Opposite, before the Sultan of Egypt, Francis offers to walk through fire to prove the truth of the Gospel while the Sultan's own priests withdraw from the challenge; the enthroned ruler presides from the centre, caught between the fire the saint is ready to enter and the sages who decline it. The pairing could not be more pointed: the word of Francis among those who believe, and among those who do not.

The lowest register is the emotional centre of the chapel and the part of the cycle that posterity has most loved. In the Death and Ascension, the saint's body lies on its bier surrounded by the grieving community: friars kiss the wounded hands and feet, others throw up their arms or press their faces close, a kneeling layman — traditionally identified with the sceptical knight Jerome of Bonaventure's account — probes the wound in the side, and above, almost unnoticed by the mourners, the soul is borne to heaven by angels. Vasari already singled out the weeping friars for praise. Facing it, the Visions of Fra Agostino and of Bishop Guido compress into one field the two supernatural attestations of the death: the dying friar Agostino, far away, suddenly speaks to announce that he will follow Francis, while the bishop of Assisi, absent on pilgrimage, sees the saint appear to him.

## The Stigmatization above the Entrance

The seventh scene stands apart, and its apartness is the point. On the transept wall above the chapel's entrance arch, facing outward into the church, Giotto painted the Stigmatization of Saint Francis on La Verna: a field of roughly four metres square in which the kneeling saint, alone on the mountain, receives the wounds of Christ from the crucified seraph that plunges toward him on a diagonal of light. In the spandrels beside the arch appear two busts traditionally identified as Adam and Eve, the first parents whose fall the new *alter Christus* answers. The placement converts the scene into a frontispiece. Before entering the room where the saint's earthly life unfolds in historical time, the visitor is shown the one event that lifts that life out of history: the miracle, dated by the sources to 1224, two years before his death, in which Francis was conformed bodily to the Crucified. Bonaventure, in the *Legenda maior*, made the seal of the stigmata the keystone of his whole interpretation of Francis, and the chapel's designers made it the keystone of the architecture of images — literally overhead as one passes beneath.

The composition belongs to a series. Giotto and his workshop treated the Stigmatization repeatedly: in the fresco of the Legend cycle in the Upper Church at Assisi, whatever position one takes on that cycle's authorship; in the signed panel from San Francesco in Pisa, now in the Louvre, where the scene fills the main field above three predella episodes; and here at Santa Croce, in what is by general consent the latest and most distilled of the versions. The Bardi Stigmatization strips the event to its essentials — saint, mountain, seraph, the astonished torsion of the body receiving the rays — and in doing so states in miniature the whole aesthetic of the chapel, which is an aesthetic of subtraction. Comparison among the three versions has long served scholars as a laboratory for the problem of Giotto's development, and the Bardi fresco's economy is regularly cited as the mark of the late style.

Its material history diverged from that of the interior. Because it stands outside the chapel proper, the Stigmatization escaped some of the vicissitudes of the six scenes within and suffered others of its own. It was recovered from under its covering layers in a separate campaign from the interior frescoes, completed by Gaetano Bianchi only in 1869, a decade and a half after his work inside the chapel; and when [Leonetto Tintori](/scholars.html#tintori-leonetto) stripped Bianchi's repaintings from the interior walls at the end of the 1950s, the Stigmatization was left untouched. Its modern restoration came only in 2011–13, when the Opificio delle Pietre Dure treated it under the direction of [Cecilia Frosinini](/scholars.html#frosinini-cecilia) — a campaign that served, in retrospect, as the proving ground for the far larger undertaking of 2022–26 within the chapel. The scene thus carries a different stratigraphy from its six companions, a fact worth remembering whenever its surface is compared with theirs.

Seen from the crossing today, the ensemble reads as its designers intended: the mountain of La Verna overhead, the arch beneath it opening into the narrow luminous room where the stories wait, and, at the end of the axis, the old dossal's frontal Francis gazing back down the chapel toward the visitor. No other surviving Giottesque ensemble preserves this complete rhetorical machine — exterior proclamation, interior narration, altar icon — and much of the Bardi Chapel's authority in the history of art rests on that completeness.

## Rethinking Assisi: The Mature Giotto and the Franciscan Narrative

Every episode painted in the Bardi Chapel had been painted a generation earlier in the nave of the Upper Church at Assisi, within the twenty-eight-scene Legend of Saint Francis; and whatever position one takes in the interminable dispute over the Legend's authorship — the "Assisi question", which need not be reopened here — the Bardi cycle reads, scene for scene, as a deliberate and radical revision of the Assisi solutions. The comparison is the most instructive in all of early Italian painting, because it shows the same iconographic tradition rethought by a supremely self-conscious artist at the far end of his career.

The first revision is quantitative and therefore structural. Assisi narrates in twenty-eight scenes; the Bardi Chapel in six, with a seventh outside. Everything anecdotal falls away: the youthful prodigies, the dreams and prophecies, the sermon to the birds, the Greccio crib, the posthumous miracles that occupy the Legend's final bays. What remains is a skeleton of institutional and spiritual essentials — vocation, approbation, mission, death — and the selection itself is an interpretation, as the section on Bonaventure and the poverty dispute will argue. The Bardi programme has no room for the picturesque Francis of popular devotion; its Francis founds, is confirmed, testifies, and dies.

The second revision is compositional, and it can be followed episode by episode. In the Assisi Renunciation the scene splits into two crowded blocks, the furious father's party and the bishop's, divided by a void of sky; the Bardi Renunciation keeps the bipartite scheme but calms it, enlarging the figures, thinning the crowds, and letting the confrontation of father and son carry across the interval with a gravity the earlier version diffuses among onlookers. In the Assisi Apparition at Arles, Francis appears at the doorway of the chapter house, hovering at the margin of the gathering; in the Bardi version he materialises at the very centre of the room, arms out in the cruciform gesture, and the architecture closes around the assembly like a reliquary. The Trial by Fire is reorganised around its true protagonist: at Assisi the composition reads from the fleeing priests toward the saint; in the Bardi fresco the Sultan is enthroned at the centre, monumental and frontal, and the scene becomes a tribunal of belief with the ruler as its pivot. And the Death of Saint Francis, for which Assisi offers separate scenes of the death, the mourning of the Clares and the funeral, is condensed at Santa Croce into a single horizontal frieze of grief around the bier.

The third revision is one of temperature. The Assisi Legend, whoever painted it, is an art of circumstantial persuasion: it multiplies witnesses, furnishings, towers and townscapes, as though the truth of the stories depended on the density of their setting. The Bardi cycle withdraws that apparatus. Its architecture is sparer and holds the figures closer; its casts are smaller; its palette, even allowing for the losses described below, is graver. The narrative mode has shifted from demonstration to meditation. Scholars have described this shift in many vocabularies — as the difference between an official pictorial biography addressed to pilgrims and a funerary meditation addressed to a family and its friars; as the maturation of Giotto's own classicism; or, for those who deny Giotto the Assisi cycle altogether, as the difference between two artistic generations. There is no need to adjudicate among these framings. What the walls themselves establish is that the Bardi designer knew the Assisi solutions intimately and corrected them point by point, and that the corrections all move in one direction: toward concentration, toward silence, toward weight.

## The Late Style: Space, Gravity and the Painter's Craft

The Bardi Chapel is the canonical exhibit of Giotto's late manner, and its stylistic character can be stated in a sentence before being qualified in a page: large, slow figures, bound into compact groups by glance and gesture, stand within shallow architectural chambers scaled to their bodies, under a light that models form broadly and a palette that refuses display. The exuberant spatial invention of the [Paduan years](/Content/Churches/Cappella-degli-Scrovegni.html) is disciplined here into something closer to relief sculpture. Interiors — the bishop's precinct of the Renunciation, the consistory of the Approval, the chapter house of Arles, the Sultan's hall, the death chamber — are boxes opened toward the beholder, their rear walls running parallel to the picture plane, their function less to describe places than to press the figures forward and hold them still. Within these chambers the drama is carried almost entirely by the human body: by the set of a shoulder, the inclination of a head, the grave choreography of hands. The mourning friars of the Death scene, each absorbed in a distinct and particularised grief, are the summit of this art.

To this long-standing critical picture the restoration of 2022–26 has added, for the first time, a detailed account of how the walls were actually made — and in one respect it has corrected what much of the literature has asserted. The convenient antithesis by which the Bardi Chapel was "true fresco" and the Peruzzi Chapel "painted a secco" can no longer be maintained as a difference in kind. The Bardi cycle is indeed built on a genuine fresco foundation: the plaster was laid in *giornate*, whose seams the diagnostics have mapped. But upon that foundation Giotto worked extensively on the dry wall, using an organic binder identified as most probably egg, in order to widen his palette beyond the fresco-stable pigments, to adjust tones and spatial effects after the plaster had set, and to organise the stages of work more freely. Azurite — the blue that cannot survive application on fresh lime — was laid a secco; and it is exactly these dry finishes that time has treated most cruelly, so that much of the cycle's original chromatic and atmospheric refinement survives only in traces. The Bardi is thus a fresco-based practice generously completed a secco, the Peruzzi an execution still more largely dry. The identified palette is sober and canonical — earth pigments, lime white and lead white, azurite, cinnabar — and the flesh of the finest heads is built, in the restorers' description, from small touches of ochres and oxides over a *verdaccio* underlayer, the greenish preparation of Tuscan tradition.

The diagnostics also recovered the working sequence with unprecedented concreteness. Thermography located the putlog holes of the original scaffolding, allowing its reconstruction: the platform was first erected at the height of the mid-lunettes to paint the vault, then lowered register by register, and each level was completed in every respect — painting through to the application of the metal leaf — before the scaffold descended. Underdrawing laid out each scene and served to plan the *giornate*, which are larger where backgrounds run uninterrupted and smaller where faces and figures demanded concentration. The fictive architecture of the framing system was constructed with incised lines and snapped, colour-charged cords, a geometry that the restoration has digitally mapped for the first time. Most touchingly, the cleaning exposed small trial brushstrokes — visible today in the Death of Saint Francis — by which the painters tested how a colour would change in drying; they were meant to disappear beneath the secco finish and are legible now only because that finish is lost. And beneath Giotto's plaster the investigators found the remains of an earlier, probably geometric, painted decoration: the chapel was not a blank shell awaiting him, and his campaign replaced a first adornment of which almost nothing else is known.

Finally, the technical study has given sharper contours to the question of hands. The examiners distinguish faces modelled with soft, fused transitions from others drawn with broad red contours and a harder, almost wooden chiaroscuro — differences consistent with the collaborative workshop practice that every scholar has assumed for a master at the height of fame, and that the section on the vault and workshop pursues on the vault and the window wall.

## Vault, Altar Wall and Workshop

The narrative scenes do not exhaust the chapel. Overhead, the four webs of the rib vault carry roundels with allegorical figures of the Franciscan virtues: Poverty, Chastity and Obedience survive, while the fourth figure is fragmentary to the point of illegibility — the suggestion that it represented Francis himself in glory, completing the triad of vows with their exemplar, is a reasonable hypothesis and no more. The vault proved one of the revelations of the recent restoration. Its surface lay under a thick layer of calcium oxalate, removed in part with laser instruments, and the cleaning did not merely brighten the figures but changed what they are. Obedience, who for a century and a half appeared to hold a red book — an attribute painted in by Gaetano Bianchi in his nineteenth-century reconstruction and knowingly left in place by the de-restorers of the 1950s — proves in fact to offer a double yoke, the proper emblem of the vow of submission; and in the small lunette above the window a hitherto unnoticed figure in the attitude of blessing has emerged within the field of Chastity. The episode of Obedience's yoke is worth dwelling on for a moment, because it is a parable of conservation history in miniature: a restorer's guess, materialised in paint, survived two subsequent campaigns and entered the descriptive literature as fact, until instrumental analysis returned the image to itself.

The entrance arch carries its own population: eight saints in medallions along the intrados. A claim circulating outside the institutional literature holds that these figures were lost and that what we see are nineteenth-century silhouettes; the point could not be verified against the restoration's published materials and is not repeated as fact. The window wall, finally, is the chapel's most damaged and most discussed secondary surface. In painted niches flanking the lancets stand four sainted figures of the Franciscan family, of whom three survive in whole or in part: Saint Louis of Toulouse and Saint Clare<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-chiara-assisi" class="footnote"><sup>9</sup></a> on the left, Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, fragmentary, on the right; the fourth figure is lost, and the proposal that it represented Saint George — hardly a Franciscan, and therefore a suggestion requiring explanation if true — is likewise not asserted. Louis of Toulouse, beside Clare and Elizabeth, assembles around the founder the royal and female sanctity of the order's first century.

The question of workshop participation runs through all of these surfaces. Critical opinion has long held that the master's own hand is most fully present in the narrative scenes and least in the ancillary decoration, and within the window wall the figure of Saint Clare is usually judged the one executed with the greatest direct participation of Giotto himself. The technical findings summarised in the previous section — the coexistence of softly fused and harshly contoured modelling — now give that connoisseurship a material correlate without yet permitting a map of named hands; the full publication of the restoration data, announced but not yet issued, may allow more. It should be said that the presence of assistants diminishes the cycle not at all. The organisation of a large decorative enterprise through a trained workshop under the master's design was the universal condition of Trecento mural painting, and the unity of conception across every surface of the chapel — from the vault's virtues to the smallest medallion — is itself the signature of a single directing intelligence.

## Bonaventure, John XXII and the Poverty Dispute

No Franciscan image of the 1320s was painted in a vacuum, and the Bardi cycle least of all. The decade of its probable execution was the decade in which the century-long quarrel over the meaning of Franciscan poverty reached its catastrophe. The rigorist wing of the order, the Spirituals, who insisted on the restricted use of goods — the *usus pauper* — as essential to the vow, had been condemned and broken in the years around 1317–18, with four friars burned at Marseilles; and in 1322–23 Pope John XXII<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-john-xxii" class="footnote"><sup>10</sup></a>, in the constitutions *Ad conditorem canonum* and *Cum inter nonnullos*, dismantled the juridical fiction by which the order owned nothing and declared it heretical to assert that Christ and the apostles had possessed nothing at all. The order's self-understanding, built for a century on the ideal the chapel celebrates, was in open crisis precisely when the Bardi — bankers, of all patrons — were having that ideal painted.

The cycle's textual conservatism must be read against this turbulence. Its source, Bonaventure's *Legenda maior*, was not a neutral biography but a text composed to stabilise the saint's image at an earlier moment of internal strife. The Bardi programme follows it faithfully and selectively: of all that Bonaventure offers, the chapel chooses the episodes in which Francis's radicalism is received, ordered and ratified by the Church — the bishop's mantle in the Renunciation, the papal approval of the Rule, the hierarchy of chapter and mission — and omits the thaumaturgic and visionary material in which later rigorism found its warrant. Rona Goffen, in the monograph that made this argument canonical, read the chapel as a programmatic statement of the Conventual position: her Bardi Francis is the purposeful, controlled hero of Bonaventure's narrative, a founder whose poverty is perfected in obedience, and the cycle, on her account, speaks for the moderate, propertied, papally loyal Franciscanism with which a great banking family could comfortably associate itself — indeed, whose theology of licit wealth the family's own confessors administered. On this reading the papal approval of the lunette, the prominence of the enthroned Sultan as a figure of ordered sovereignty, and the sheer institutional gravity of the whole ensemble all pull in one direction.

The reading has proved durable, and it remains the indispensable starting point; but it has not gone unchallenged, and the challenges deserve recording. Some scholars have objected that the neat opposition of Spiritual and Conventual on which it rests is too blunt an instrument for the actual texture of Franciscan life in the 1320s — one recent critic has dismissed the binary outright as "an exceedingly simple ideologeme" — and that reading a fresco cycle as a party manifesto underestimates both the conventionality of its iconographic choices and the plurality of its audiences: friars in choir, family members at their chapel, lay visitors in the transept did not all see the same argument. Others note that the emotional core of the cycle, the wall of grief around the dead saint, resists ideological translation altogether. A prudent position, and the one adopted here, is layered. That the cycle derives from Bonaventure is documented by its every episode. That its selection emphasises ecclesial obedience over charismatic radicalism is an observation about the walls themselves, verifiable by anyone who compares the programme with the fuller repertories. That this emphasis was intended as an intervention in the poverty controversy, and that the Bardi meant the chapel to baptise their banking, are interpretations — powerful, economical with the evidence, and unprovable. The chapel accommodates them without being exhausted by them.

It is worth adding that the dossal on the altar, though a later arrival in the room, now participates in this field of meaning. The Duecento panel surrounds its Francis with exactly the miracle-rich, wonder-working narrative that Giotto's selection suppresses; the sixteenth-century decision to place it here created, however unintentionally, a visible dialogue between the two Franciscanisms that the fourteenth century had set against each other. Few rooms allow the history of a saint's image to argue with itself so audibly.

## Fortune and Conservation History: From the Trecento to Tintori

The afterlife of the Bardi frescoes begins immediately, in the next generation of Florentine painting. Taddeo Gaddi, Giotto's closest collaborator, carried the chapel's lessons into the Baroncelli Chapel at the end of the same transept; Maso di Banco, in the Bardi di Vernio Chapel a few bays away, distilled the late style's gravity into the Sylvester cycle. The Death of Saint Francis proved the most fertile single invention. Its horizontal frieze of mourners around the bier became the standard Florentine formula for the death of a saint, reworked across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; the most famous homage, as has often been observed, is Domenico Ghirlandaio's Obsequies of Saint Francis in the Sassetti Chapel at Santa Trinita, painted in the 1480s while Giotto's prototype was still visible on the wall. One persistent conflation should be corrected here: the celebrated early drawing by Michelangelo in the Louvre, copying figures from Giotto in Santa Croce, reproduces the Ascension of Saint John the Evangelist in the Peruzzi Chapel, not a Bardi scene, and no secure Michelangelo copy after the Bardi frescoes is known. The young Michelangelo surely studied these walls too; the surviving sheet simply does not show it.

Then came the long eclipse. At some point in the early eighteenth century the frescoes were covered with whitewash — a fate they shared, a little later, with the Peruzzi cycle, and which reflects the period's changed taste and the practical demands of mourning decoration and sanitation in a working church rather than any singular act of vandalism. The date sometimes given, around 1714, finds no support in the sources; the firm documentary anchor is a note of 1730 by Giovanni Gaetano Bottari, in his edition of Borghini's *Il Riposo*, recording the Bardi paintings as already hidden while the Peruzzi frescoes could still be seen. "Before 1730," or "in the first decades of the eighteenth century," is all the evidence allows. Two further corrections follow. The whitewashing was not occasioned by the installation of the funerary monuments, which came almost a century later: the cenotaph by Giuseppe Salvetti was set into the left wall in 1812, that by Niccolò Gaspero Maria Paoletti into the right wall in 1818, both cut into walls already blank. And the claim, circulating in the popular literature, that the chapel was plastered over at the behest of Cosimo I in connection with Vasari's renovation of the church, finds no support in the institutional sources and should be abandoned.

Rediscovery came in 1851, when soundings made in view of a planned redecoration of the chapel revealed painting beneath the lime. The uncovering and restoration were entrusted to Gaetano Bianchi, who worked in the chapel in 1852–53 — assisted, in the Opera di Santa Croce's rueful phrase, by the clumsy help of some of the friars — and an inscription beside the altar commemorates the completion in 1853; the young Odoardo Borrani, later a Macchiaiolo, served in Bianchi's workshop. The campaign was heroic and ruinous in equal measure. The mechanical removal of the whitewash abraded the painted surface everywhere; the extraction of the two cenotaphs exposed great voids where the frescoes were wholly lost; and Bianchi, according to the practice of his age, repainted the losses in integrative style and unified the whole under a broad tempera patina, so that the chapel reopened as a plausible, continuous, and substantially nineteenth-century ensemble.

A century later the pendulum swung with equal force. In a campaign begun in the summer of 1957 and concluded, according to the sources, between the end of 1958 and 1959 — the institutional accounts themselves waver among 1957–58, 1957–59 and 1958–59 — Leonetto Tintori, working under the direction of the Soprintendenza in the person of [Ugo Procacci](/scholars.html#procacci-ugo), removed Bianchi's repaintings and returned the cycle to its authentic remains. The choice was rigorous to the point of austerity: the stripped losses, some five thousand lacunae great and small, were left as fields of neutral tone, and the chapel assumed the fragmentary, honest, visually demanding aspect that three generations of visitors have known. Where Bianchi's reconstructions stood on his own new plaster, they were not destroyed but detached by *strappo* and preserved as historical documents, today divided between Santa Croce and the deposits of the Soprintendenza — a decision far ahead of its time in its respect for the history of restoration itself. Time, however, overtook the campaign's own materials: the acrylic-bound fillings and the fixatives applied in the 1950s discoloured over the following half-century, dulling the surface and confusing the legibility that Tintori had sought, and it was this slow degradation, together with the desire to understand the cycle with modern instruments, that set the stage for the campaign of 2022.

## The Restoration of 2022–2026 and the Octocentenary

The campaign that has just concluded was the most thorough interrogation the chapel has ever undergone, and its genesis was long. Its scientific foundation was laid in 2009–10, when a diagnostic survey of both Santa Croce chapels — the *Progetto Giotto*, supported by the Getty Foundation and published in 2017 under the editorship of Cecilia Frosinini — mapped the techniques and the condition of the murals without touching them. The full campaign on the chapel's interior was conceived by [Marco Ciatti](/scholars.html#ciatti-marco), the Opificio delle Pietre Dure's late superintendent, and carried forward after his death by the Institute under its present superintendent, with a team led by two of its senior wall-painting restorers and a scientific committee chaired by a former head of the Florentine museums, on which Frosinini herself sat. Work began in June 2022 — a few accounts say July — with interim findings presented to the public on 19 and 20 September 2024; the completion, initially forecast for 2025 and then for January 2026, finally came with the presentation of results on 11 September 2026 and the reopening of the chapel on 12 September. The return of his greatest Florentine cycle was conceived as Florence's contribution to the octocentenary. The cost exceeded one million euros, borne by the Opificio and the Opera di Santa Croce with the ARPAI association, the Fondazione CR Firenze, and private donors gathered through the #Giving4Giotto campaign — a funding structure, mixing institutional and civic money, that the Bardi themselves would have recognised.

The campaign's discoveries have been woven through the preceding sections. Two aspects remain to be described: the physics and the philosophy. The physics was a collaboration beyond the normal boundaries of restoration: the X-ray fluorescence mapping that retrieved the Arles crucifix was conducted with the Florence section of the National Institute for Nuclear Physics, and the vault's blanket of calcium oxalate yielded in part to laser ablation. The philosophy, more consequential still, governed what the restorers allowed themselves to restore. Tintori's five thousand neutral lacunae posed the question unavoidably: should the twenty-first century reconstruct what the twentieth had refused to? The answer was differentiated and exemplary. The fictive architecture of the framing system — geometry, repeatable, documented by its own incisions — was reconstructed in a deliberately lighter, recognisable tone, restoring to the chapel the unifying envelope, that "great ciborium," which the fragments alone could not convey. The faces, the figures and the lost passages of narrative were not reconstructed: where Giotto is gone, he is allowed to be gone. The chapel as reopened is thus neither Bianchi's seamless fiction nor Tintori's archaeological field, but a third thing: a legible architecture of images that declares its own wounds.

Two cautions belong at the end of this account. The first is documentary: everything summarised here rests on the institutional presentations and the qualified press of September 2024 and September 2026, since the scientific publication of the campaign — announced, with study days to follow — had not yet appeared at the time of writing; details, and especially the attribution of particular findings, may be refined when it does. The second is perceptual. Four years of scaffolding have ended, and the first photographs show a chapel transformed in legibility; but no photograph substitutes for the room. The visitor who now stands in the reopened chapel, with the dossal returned to its altar and the virtues legible overhead for the first time in living memory, sees more of Giotto's intention — though not more of Giotto's paint — than any visitor since the eighteenth century.

## Conclusion: The Bardi Chapel in the History of Painting

The Bardi Chapel is a small room that contains a disproportionate share of the history of European painting. It contains, first, the last full statement of the artist who reinvented the art. Whatever is decided about Assisi, about Rome, about the lost cycles of Rimini and Naples, the Bardi walls — with their Peruzzi neighbours — are where Giotto's Florentine maturity can actually be seen, and what they show is an artist moving, at the end, toward subtraction: fewer scenes, fewer figures, graver intervals, a narrative art so confident of its means that it can afford silence. The boxed interiors of the Renunciation and the Approval anticipate two centuries of Florentine stagecraft; and the whole chapel, read as a single designed object from the Stigmatization overhead to the virtues in the vault, is among the earliest surviving demonstrations that a cycle of images can be composed the way an argument is composed.

It contains, second, a knot of historical meaning that no amount of looking unties. A banking dynasty on the eve of its ruin paid for the definitive image of the saint of poverty, in the decade when the Church was criminalising the radical interpretation of that poverty; the cycle they received celebrates a Francis received, ratified and ordered by the institutions their money served. Whether one follows Goffen in reading this as deliberate ideology, or prefers to see in it the ordinary gravitation of official iconography toward official texts, the chapel remains the place where Trecento Florence's deepest contradiction — mendicant ideal and mercantile reality — was given permanent visible form, and where it can still be inspected.

And it contains, third, an epitome of what Europe has done with its artistic past: admired it, imitated it, whitewashed it, pierced it with monuments, rediscovered it, falsified it in good faith, stripped it with equal conviction, and finally learned to question it molecule by molecule. The chapel that reopened in September 2026 carries all of these histories legibly on its surface, and the restorers' decision to reconstruct the frame but not the figures means that it will go on carrying them. Eight hundred years after the death of Francis and seven hundred after the death of Giotto, the room beside the chancel of Santa Croce still does what it was built to do: it sets before whoever enters a man who gave everything away, painted at the expense of men who had everything — and it leaves the beholder, as the best art does, to settle the account.

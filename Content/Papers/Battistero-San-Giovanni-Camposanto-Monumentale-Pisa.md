## Introduction: A Unified Programme in Marble

The Piazza del Duomo of Pisa — the walled compound of roughly 8.87 hectares that popular usage has christened the "Piazza dei Miracoli" or "Campo dei Miracoli" — is among the most completely realised expressions of civic and religious ambition to survive from the Italian Middle Ages. Within a single grassed enclosure, set deliberately at the north-western edge of the medieval city rather than at its centre, four monuments of white and grey marble were raised over some three and a half centuries: the Cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta (begun 1063/1064), its detached campanile (the Leaning Tower, begun 1173), the Baptistery of San Giovanni (begun 1152), and the Camposanto Monumentale (begun 1277/1278). The complex is frequently read, in both scholarly and devotional literature, as a symbolic diagram of the Christian life: the Baptistery as birth into the faith, the Cathedral as the life of the community, and the Camposanto as death and the hope of resurrection. This essay concentrates on the two termini of that programme — the Baptistery and the Camposanto — which together frame the human passage from the font to the grave and which, between them, preserve some of the most important sculpture and mural painting of the Italian Duecento, Trecento and Quattrocento.

The designation "Campo dei Miracoli" is itself a modern and to some degree accidental one, and it is worth clarifying at the outset because it bears on how the site has been received. The square's official and historical name is simply Piazza del Duomo (anciently the "Arringum Maioris Ecclesie" or "Platea Ecclesie Sancte Marie"). The phrase "miracoli" derives from a line by the poet Gabriele D'Annunzio, who in his novel *Forse che sì forse che no* (1910) evoked "il prato dei Miracoli" — "the meadow of miracles." From this poetic coinage the usages "prato dei Miracoli," "Campo dei Miracoli" and finally "Piazza dei Miracoli" spread, the last becoming dominant only in the mass-tourism era after the Second World War. Pisan purists note that "Campo dei Miracoli" is strictly a literary confusion, since that is the name of the fraudulent field in Carlo Collodi's *Pinocchio*; nonetheless the phrase has entered international currency. The nomenclature is a useful reminder that the site as we experience it is a palimpsest of medieval fabric and later — sometimes very recent — framing.

The historiography of the complex is long and unusually well documented, because the Camposanto in particular became, from the fifteenth century onward, a fixed stop on the itineraries of pilgrims, chroniclers, artists and Grand Tourists. Lorenzo Ghiberti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-lorenzo-ghiberti" class="footnote"><sup>1</sup></a> praised its frescoes in his *Commentarii*; Giorgio Vasari<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-giorgio-vasari" class="footnote"><sup>2</sup></a>, in the second edition of the *Vite* (1568), attempted the first systematic assignment of the murals to named masters. From the nineteenth century the site attracted the tools of modern connoisseurship, and in the twentieth it became one of the great laboratories of art-historical method and of conservation practice — above all after the catastrophe of 1944. A recent digital-humanities project at the University of Zurich, which has assembled and edited the written sources on the Camposanto from the Middle Ages to the present, has made it possible to distinguish with new precision between documented fact and inherited legend, and the present essay leans on that distinction throughout.

## Urban, Political and Religious Context: The Ambition of a Maritime Republic

The monuments of the Piazza cannot be understood apart from the wealth and self-image of the Pisan maritime republic at its zenith. Between the eleventh and the late thirteenth century Pisa was one of the pre-eminent naval and commercial powers of the western Mediterranean, with colonies, trading posts (*fondaci*) and privileges extending from Sardinia, Corsica and the Tuscan archipelago to the Levant, Constantinople and North Africa. The city's prosperity was tied directly to a series of anti-Islamic naval campaigns undertaken, often in concert with Genoa, over the eleventh and early twelfth centuries: expeditions against Bona (1034) in present-day Algeria, the celebrated raid on the harbour of Palermo (1063), the campaign against al-Mahdiya in Tunisia (1087), and, most gloriously in Pisan memory, the Balearic expedition of 1113–1115.

These enterprises were not merely lucrative; they furnished the ideological and material capital for the building programme in the Piazza. The Cathedral itself was begun, according to the tradition recorded on the façade inscriptions and by later chroniclers, with the tithe of the booty taken from Palermo in 1063. The building of a colossal marble cathedral, and subsequently of the largest baptistery in Christendom, outside the city walls — in an area that Pisa evidently felt no need to fortify — is often interpreted as a statement of confidence by a republic that saw itself as an *altera Roma*, a "new Rome" charged with defending Christendom. The archbishopric reinforced this posture: the archbishops of Pisa held a primacy over the sees of Sardinia and Corsica, and figures such as Ubaldo Lanfranchi<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ubaldo-lanfranchi" class="footnote"><sup>3</sup></a> (archbishop 1176–1207) participated personally in crusading ventures. It is precisely this fusion of maritime wealth, ecclesiastical primacy and civic self-glorification that gives the Piazza its distinctive character: a religious sanctuary that is simultaneously a war memorial, a treasury and a manifesto.

The turning point came in 1284, when the Pisan fleet was destroyed by Genoa at the battle of Meloria. The defeat inaugurated a long decline; the chains that had closed the mouth of Porto Pisano were carried off by the Genoese in mockery, and fragments of them are today displayed in the Camposanto — seized after the 1284 defeat and, after long dispersal, finally returned to Pisa in 1860. The republic lost Sardinia by 1326 and its autonomy altogether in 1406, when it fell under Florentine dominion. Much of the building history recounted below — the slow, interrupted completion of the Baptistery's Gothic upper storeys and of the Camposanto's galleries and frescoes — unfolds against this backdrop of contraction, so that the Piazza in its final medieval form is as much a monument to memory and lost greatness as to power.

## The Battistero di San Giovanni

### Foundation, Diotisalvi, and the Romanesque Structure

The Baptistery of San Giovanni was founded in 1152. The date is fixed by an inscription within the building recording that the work was founded "1153 mense Augusti" — August 1153 in the Pisan [calendar](/dating-systems/), which ran a year ahead of the common style, hence 1152 by modern reckoning. It replaced an earlier, smaller baptistery that had stood to the north-east of the Cathedral, in the area later occupied by the Camposanto. The founding architect is named in a signed pier inside the building: "Deotisalvi magister" (Diotisalvi). Diotisalvi is generally credited with directing construction from 1152 to roughly 1180, and to his phase belong the fundamental Romanesque conception of the building: the circular plan, the internal masonry of the pentagonal piers with their mouldings and bases, and, on the exterior, the blind arcading of the lower order and the arcaded gallery of the second order.

The Baptistery is the largest in Christendom. According to the figures published by the Opera della Primaziale Pisana and repeated in the standard reference literature, its circumference measures 107.24 metres, the masonry at the base is 2.63 metres thick, and its height is 54.86 metres; its diameter is said to equal the width of the Cathedral façade, a deliberate proportional relationship. The plan and the reference to circular, centrally planned models are commonly read as evocations of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem — the Anastasis rotunda — and, in the exterior conception, of the Dome of the Rock, both of which Diotisalvi is thought to have cited also in his church of the Holy Sepulchre in Pisa. Such Jerusalem references are entirely consistent with the crusading self-image of the commune discussed above.

Construction slowed after about 1180 and eventually halted; documents nonetheless attest that ceremonies were already being celebrated inside the unfinished building by the mid-1180s. The building history extended over nearly two centuries and passed through several hands: after Diotisalvi came masters associated with the names Guido/Guidetto and the Bigarelli, and then, in the second half of the thirteenth century, [Nicola](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Nicola-Pisano.html) and [Giovanni Pisano](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Giovanni-Pisano.html), who gave the exterior its Gothic upper register; the work concluded under Cellino di Nese in the fourteenth century.

### The Gothic Phases and the Dome

The intervention of Nicola and Giovanni Pisano transformed the Baptistery's silhouette. To Nicola, working after about 1260, is attributed the completion of the middle external loggia with its distinctive Gothic decoration of heads and monumental busts of saints and prophets; his son Giovanni worked alongside him, conventionally dated between roughly 1278 and 1284, adding the gables (cuspids) and much of the sculptural programme above the arches. The rich sculptural decoration of the exterior — many original pieces of which have been removed to the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo (reopened in 2019) and replaced *in situ* by copies for conservation reasons — includes the elaborately carved principal portal facing the Cathedral, with its Apostles, its Descent into Hell, its figure of David, and an architrave carved in two registers with Christ between the Virgin and the Baptist above and stories of the Baptist below.

The most technically remarkable feature is the roofing system: a double shell. There is an inner dome shaped as a truncated pyramid or cone (described as dodecagonal on a twelve-sided base) and an outer hemispherical calotte surmounted by a small lantern (*cupolino*), the two separated by an intermediate cavity. The hemispherical outer dome, effectively a tiburio concealing the pyramidal inner structure, belongs to the later Gothic campaign; several sources date the execution of the dome from 1358 and associate it with Cellino di Nese and a "Maestro Zibellino" of Bologna, though the details of this attribution should be treated with some caution and verified against specialist literature. The roof covering is famously asymmetrical: the side facing the sea (to the north-west) is clad in lead, while the other side is covered in red tiles — an arrangement sometimes explained by the Baptistery's supposed function as a daytime landmark or nocturnal beacon, an explanation that has the flavour of tradition and should be flagged as such.

### The Exterior: The East Portal of the Pisa Baptistery

*Attribution: Byzantinising Tuscan-Lombard workshop (lintel and portal sculpture, second half of the twelfth century, c. 1180–1200); tympanum statue of the Madonna and Child after Giovanni Pisano (early twentieth-century copy; original, c. 1300, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo, Pisa). Materials: white Apuan (Carrara) marble, grey-blue bardiglio revetment, with bichrome limestone and serpentine voussoirs.*

Of the four portals of the Pisa Baptistery, the eastern is the most sumptuous, and deliberately so: it opens directly opposite the west façade of the cathedral, on the processional axis that binds the two buildings into a single liturgical organism. Through this door the baptismal party entered; through it the newly baptised, reborn in Guido Bigarelli's font, would process towards the mother church. The portal was therefore conceived as a threshold in the full theological sense — a *porta caeli* — and its sculptural programme, executed while Diotisalvi's rotunda was still rising in the second half of the twelfth century, constitutes the most ambitious ensemble of Romanesque figurative carving in Pisa, the essential local precedent against which the later achievements of Nicola and Giovanni Pisano must be read.

The photograph embraces the whole upper portal: the double-registered marble lintel with its crowded reliefs, the deep lunette faced in dark bardiglio against which stands the white Madonna and Child, and the cascade of carved archivolts that frame the whole, set within the striped bichrome masonry of the exterior. The ensemble is the work of several hands and two distinct epochs — a Romanesque frame of the late twelfth century enclosing, in its tympanum, the echo of a Gothic masterpiece of c. 1300 — and this stratification is itself part of its historical eloquence.

The portal is spanned by a monumental architrave organised in two superimposed relief bands, both of white marble now warmly patinated by eight centuries of weather.

- The lower register

Immediately above the door, narrates the life of Saint John the Baptist — the titular of the building — in a continuous frieze that reads from left to right like an unrolled scroll. The episodes follow the Gospel sequence: the preaching of the Precursor in the wilderness, the crowds pressing about him beneath schematic trees; the Baptism of Christ in the Jordan at the centre, the dove descending vertically upon the axis of the composition; then, to the right, the tragic denouement — Herod's banquet with its table and vessels, the dance of Salome, and finally, at the far right edge, the beheading of the saint, his bent body compressed beneath the falling sword, with the presentation of the head upon the charger. The narrative is dense, the figures small and thickly grouped, their draperies incised with fine parallel striations of unmistakably Byzantine derivation; arcades and slender aediculae punctuate the scenes, providing both stage-architecture and rhythmic division. Two fragmentary inscriptions survive at the ends of the intermediate fascia, remnants of the dedicatory and invocatory texts that once addressed the entering faithful.

- The upper register

It presents, by contrast, a hieratic and timeless image: a solemn theoria of eleven haloed figures in half-length procession — Christ and the Virgin among apostles and evangelists, several displaying jewelled book-covers inlaid with small tesserae of red and dark stone — flanked at either extremity by stylised palm trees, the trees of Paradise that locate the assembly outside historical time. The contrast between the two bands is doctrinally deliberate: below, the historical economy of salvation unfolding in time through the Baptist's witness; above, the eternal court of heaven towards which baptism opens the way. The carving of this upper zone, with its calligraphic drapery lines, elongated proportions, and frontal solemnity, speaks the *maniera greca* — the Byzantinising idiom that dominated Tuscan sculpture and painting before the classical turn of the mid-Duecento — and older scholarship associated these reliefs with masters in the orbit of Bonanno Pisano and with itinerant Lombard carvers; the question of hands remains open, but a dating in the last decades of the twelfth century commands general assent.

Above the architrave opens the lunette, its field faced with courses of grey-blue bardiglio whose cool, cloudy tonality functions as a chromatic void — a marble sky — against which the tympanum sculpture stands in maximum relief. At its centre rises the standing **Madonna and Child**: the Virgin crowned and veiled, her weight subtly shifted, the mantle drawn across the body in deep diagonal folds gathered at her hip, the Child upright on her left arm, His face turned to meet hers in intimate reciprocity. The statue visible today is a substitution of the early twentieth century — the date is incised in Roman numerals upon its plinth — installed when the weathered original was withdrawn to the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. That original belongs to the ambit of **Giovanni Pisano**, c. 1300: the torsion of the figure, the Gothic sway, the psychological current passing between Mother and Child are his inventions, and even in copy they register the distance travelled by Pisan sculpture in the century that separates the tympanum from the lintel below it. The lunette thus juxtaposes, in a single vertical glance, the two poles of medieval Pisan art: the hieratic Romanesque frieze and the animated Gothic figure, the icon and the person.

The portal's frame is a display of ornamental virtuosity. The jambs are clad with pilasters carved in dense inhabited rinceaux — spiralling acanthus stems enclosing rosettes and figures — while the inner faces carry the celebrated small reliefs of the **Labours of the Months**, the calendar of human toil sanctified at the church door. The flanking columns bear composite capitals of drilled acanthus, upon which stand small sculpted groups serving as imposts. Above, the archivolts spring in receding orders: an inner order of radiating acanthus fronds, almost metallic in their crispness; a middle order of foliate panels and rosettes framed in running bands; an outer order of medallion-busts set in beaded roundels — prophets and elders keeping watch over the threshold. Beyond, the wall resumes the zebra-striped bichromy of grey serpentine and pale limestone, and the yellowish voussoirs of the inner arch introduce a third chromatic note, so that the portal reads as a polychrome crescendo gathered around the dark lunette and its white Madonna.

Read as a whole, the east portal is a compact *summa* of baptismal theology. The Months on the jambs place the entering Christian within the created order of time and labour; the Baptist's life on the lintel presents the sacrament's institution and its cost; the heavenly assembly above declares its goal; and the Virgin in the tympanum — the *porta clausa* of Ezekiel, herself the door through which God entered the world — presides over the physical door through which the faithful enter the church. Architecture, ornament, and figure collaborate in a single argument: that to pass this threshold is to pass from time into eternity.

The east portal of the Baptistery is the indispensable prologue to everything Pisan sculpture would become. Its Byzantinising friezes represent the highest ambition of the twelfth-century workshops; its ornamental orders transmit the Lombard and classical inheritance that Guido Bigarelli would refine in the font of 1246; and the Madonna of its tympanum — Giovanni Pisano's conception, even in surrogate — announces the Gothic humanism that would complete the building's exterior. Few doorways in Italy compress so much of the history of medieval sculpture into a single threshold; none serves better as the visitor's first lesson before entering the rotunda where font and pulpit await.

### The Interior: The Baptismal Font of Guido Bigarelli da Como (1246)

<figure>
  <img src="/Images/Battistero-and-Camposanto-Pisa/battistero-and-camposanto-pisa-03.jpg" alt="The 1246 octagonal baptismal font of Guido Bigarelli da Como at the centre of the Pisa Baptistery: sixteen marble panels with high-relief rosettes framed by Cosmatesque inlay, the bronze Saint John the Baptist by Italo Griselli at its centre, and Nicola Pisano's 1260 pulpit and the banded grey masonry of the rotunda behind">
  <figcaption>The Baptismal Font of Guido Bigarelli da Como, 1246: Ornament and Sacrament at the Centre of the Pisa Baptistery.</figcaption>
</figure>

Few monuments of the Italian Middle Ages articulate the sacramental theology of baptism with the architectural grandeur of the Baptistery of Pisa. Begun in 1152 to the design of Diotisalvi — whose proud inscription *Deotisalvi magister huius operis* survives on a pier within — the immense rotunda rose slowly across more than two centuries, its fabric registering the transition from the severe Romanesque of the twelfth century to the Gothic exuberance later imposed on its exterior by Nicola and Giovanni Pisano. The interior, by contrast, retains its original gravity: a vast cylindrical space wrapped in banded masonry of pale grey limestone and dark serpentine, its bichrome courses running in unbroken horizontal rhythm around the ambulatory and gallery. Light enters high and falls obliquely, so that the space reads as austere, almost undecorated — a deliberate spareness that concentrates the eye upon the one object for which the entire building exists: the baptismal font at its exact centre, placed directly beneath the apex of the dome.

It was for this focal position that the Opera di Santa Maria, in the mid-1240s, commissioned a new monumental font from Guido Bigarelli da Como, and the master completed and signed the work in 1246. The date is significant. Pisa in the 1240s stood at the height of its maritime power and deep in the Ghibelline politics of the age of Frederick II; its cathedral complex was the most ambitious sculptural workshop in Tuscany, drawing masters from Lombardy, Apulia, and beyond. The font thus belongs to the generation immediately preceding Nicola Pisano's pulpit of 1260, erected only a few metres away — a proximity that has shaped, and sometimes distorted, the critical fortunes of Guido's masterpiece ever since.

Guido Bigarelli — also documented as Guido da Como — was born into a dynasty of sculptors from Arogno, on the Lugano lake, the heartland of the itinerant Lombard builders and carvers whom later tradition would call the *maestri comacini*. These workshops, organised along familial lines and travelling wherever patronage summoned them, had for centuries supplied northern and central Italy with architectural sculpture of remarkable technical assurance. The Bigarelli clan was among the most successful of these dynasties in the thirteenth century: Guido's kinsman Lanfranco worked at Verona, while Guido himself is securely documented in Tuscany, above all in Lucca and Pisa. His most famous signed work before the Pisan font is the pulpit of San Bartolomeo in Pantano at Pistoia (1250), whose inscription proudly names him *Guido de Como*, and he has long been associated with the sculptural decoration of San Frediano in Lucca and with the ambit of the earlier master Guidetto da Como, to whom he was probably related and with whom older scholarship frequently confused him.

The Comacine formation is legible in every centimetre of the Pisan font. It appears in the supreme confidence of the ornamental carving, in the taste for dense vegetal interlace, in the small apotropaic heads that inhabit the foliage, and in the willingness to treat sculpture primarily as the enrichment of surface rather than as the vehicle of narrative. Yet Guido was no provincial repeating inherited formulae. In Pisa he absorbed the central-Italian tradition of polychrome inlay — the geometric *tarsia* of the Cosmati workshops of Rome and their Tuscan interpreters — and fused it with Lombard relief carving to produce something without exact precedent: a work in which two great decorative languages of the Italian Duecento speak simultaneously.

The font is raised upon a stepped platform of white marble, three degrees high, which lifts the basin above the pavement and obliges the visitor — as it once obliged the baptismal party — to ascend towards the water. The basin itself is a great octagon, broad enough to have permitted the collective baptisms by immersion that remained part of Pisan usage, concentrated in the solemn vigils of Easter and Pentecost when the catechumens of the city and diocese were christened together.

The choice of the octagon was anything but arbitrary. Since Late Antiquity the number eight had been the arithmetic of resurrection: the world was created in seven days, and the "eighth day" — the day of Christ's rising, the first day of the new week and the new creation — became the figure of eternity and of the soul's regeneration. Saint Ambrose had given the idea canonical formulation in the verses composed for the octagonal baptistery of Milan, and medieval builders repeated the geometry from Lateran Rome to the furthest provinces. In Pisa the symbolism is compounded: the octagonal font stands at the centre of a circular building, itself an image of the celestial sphere, so that the neophyte descended into the waters at the geometric and cosmological centre of a microcosm.

A further, remarkably practical feature articulates the interior of the basin: at four of its angles are set smaller circular wells, each just large enough for the immersion of an infant. This arrangement, found also in other Tuscan fonts, allowed several priests to baptise simultaneously during the great festal liturgies, and it preserves in stone the moment of transition between the ancient baptism of adults by full immersion and the increasingly universal baptism of newborns. The font is thus a liturgical document as much as a work of art: its very plan records the sacramental practice of the thirteenth-century Pisan church.

Each of the eight external faces of the basin is divided into two rectangular panels — sixteen in all — separated and framed by pilaster-like bands of geometric inlay. The framing system is of Cosmatesque derivation: minute triangles, lozenges, and chequers of white marble, black serpentine, and warm red stone are set in dense alternation, producing borders that shimmer with chromatic vibration. The rhythm of these bands is calculated with great subtlety; the dark ground of the outer frames throws the pale carved panels into relief, while the inner mouldings, stepped and enriched with running ornament, create a gradual transition from the flat polychromy of the frame to the three-dimensional carving of the field.

At the centre of each panel blossoms a great rosette carved in high relief — the signature motif of the whole work. These are not repetitions of a single cartoon but sixteen distinct inventions on a common theme. Concentric rings of acanthus unfurl around central floral bosses; in some panels the foliage curls outward in broad, fleshy lobes, in others it is drawn into tighter, more nervous spirals; palmettes, rosaces, and bands of imbricated leaves alternate with zones of pierced interlace so deeply undercut that the ornament seems to detach itself from the ground and float. The drill has been used with virtuosic freedom to hollow the spaces between the leaves, creating pools of shadow that give the carving its almost metallic sparkle — a technique that recalls, at monumental scale, the goldsmith's art with which the Comacine carvers had ancient affinities. Several panels are further enlivened by small heads set at the corners or nested within the foliage: human faces of serene, mask-like stillness, and leonine or grotesque muzzles whose apotropaic function — the guarding of the sacred water against the demonic — descends from a tradition as old as the Romanesque itself. Along the joints of the panels, slender carved masks and foliate bosses punctuate the composition, softening the transitions between the faces of the octagon.

The aesthetic effect of the ensemble depends on the calculated tension between two modes of vision. Seen from a distance, the font reads as an architectural object: a low, massive octagonal enclosure whose alternation of light panels and dark frames harmonises with the banded masonry of the rotunda around it. Seen from close at hand, it dissolves into an almost textile richness of detail — a marble tapestry in which no two square palms are identical. This double register, monumental and miniaturist at once, is perhaps Guido's greatest achievement, and it explains why the font holds its own even in the overwhelming spatial context of Diotisalvi's rotunda.

The basin is crowned by a broad moulded rim of reddish marble, whose warm tonality binds together the cooler whites, greys, and blacks below and provides a continuous horizontal conclusion to the composition — a chromatic device that anticipates, on a liturgical furnishing, the polychrome logic of Tuscan Gothic architecture.

At the centre of the basin rises a moulded support that once related to the covering and ceremonial apparatus of the font. Upon it now stands the bronze figure of Saint John the Baptist by Italo Griselli, installed in 1929. Griselli's Baptist, lean and hieratic, clad in the camel-skin of the desert and extending his right hand in a gesture that hovers between blessing and demonstration, was conceived in conscious sympathy with the medieval ensemble: its attenuated verticality supplies the axial accent that the broad, low octagon requires, and its dark patinated bronze stands out against the pale marbles with heraldic clarity. Purists may regret the intrusion of a twentieth-century work at the heart of a Duecento monument, yet the figure has by now entered the visual identity of the Baptistery, and it performs, in modern idiom, the ancient function of marking the font as the domain of the Precursor — the saint to whom every baptistery is implicitly dedicated.

No account of the font can ignore the object that rises immediately behind it in the visitor's view: the hexagonal pulpit carved by Nicola Pisano in 1260, a work traditionally regarded as the opening statement of a new, classicising figural art in Italy. The juxtaposition is one of the most instructive in all of Italian sculpture. Fourteen years separate the two works, yet they seem to belong to different worlds. Guido's font is the culmination of the ornamental tradition: its subject is not the human figure but the inexhaustible fertility of vegetal and geometric form, its rhetoric one of surface, pattern, and colour. Nicola's pulpit, resting on its columns and lions, deploys dense narrative reliefs whose figures are studied from Roman sarcophagi; it addresses the beholder through story and human presence.

Older criticism, reading the history of Duecento sculpture teleologically as the prehistory of the Renaissance, tended to cast Guido as the representative of a superseded "decorative" Middle Ages against which Nicola's "progress" could be measured. More recent scholarship has rightly abandoned this framing. The two works served different liturgical functions and answered different expressive demands: the pulpit is an instrument of preaching, and narrative is its business; the font is the vessel of a sacrament whose mystery was traditionally expressed through symbol, number, and ornament rather than through storytelling. Understood on its own terms, Guido's font is not a conservative foil to Nicola's innovation but the perfected statement of a parallel artistic ideal — one in which the sanctity of the water is proclaimed by transforming its container into an object of jewel-like preciousness, a *vas electionis* in marble.

The font is executed principally in white marble of Apuan provenance, with inlays of dark green-black serpentine (the *verde nero* quarried near Prato and long favoured by Tuscan builders for bichrome work) and of red stone, together with the reddish marble of the crowning rim. The carving technique combines flat relief, high relief, and true undercutting, with extensive use of the running drill in the pierced foliage. The precision of the joints between the panels and the inlaid bands testifies to a workshop accustomed to the exacting tolerances of architectural stereotomy. The state of preservation is, on the whole, remarkable for a liturgical furnishing in continuous use for nearly eight centuries: the inlays have suffered scattered losses and restorations, and the carved surfaces show the gentle abrasion of time, but the essential integrity of Guido's design — sixteen panels, their frames, and the crowning rim — survives intact, still performing its function at the centre of the rotunda for the baptisms that continue to be celebrated there.

Signed and dated at the very midpoint of the Duecento, the font of Guido Bigarelli da Como condenses into a single object several of the great currents of medieval Italian art: the technical inheritance of the Lombard travelling workshops, the Roman and Tuscan tradition of polychrome inlay, the number-symbolism of Christian architecture, and the liturgical demands of a maritime metropolis at the height of its power. It is at once a functional vessel, a theological diagram, and a display of ornamental invention scarcely surpassed in the century. Standing before it — with Nicola's pulpit rising beyond and the bichrome walls of Diotisalvi's rotunda closing the horizon — the modern visitor encounters, in a single glance, the full spectrum of thirteenth-century Tuscan sculpture: and at its centre, quietly magnificent, the marble octagon in which generations of Pisans were reborn.

A final note: it was in this font, in 1564, that Galileo Galilei is recorded to have been baptised.

### The Pulpit of Nicola Pisano (1260)

  <img src="/Images/Battistero-and-Camposanto-Pisa/battistero-and-camposanto-pisa-04.jpg" alt="Nicola Pisano's hexagonal pulpit of 1260 in the Pisa Baptistery: seven polychrome columns, three resting on crouching lions, carry trilobed Gothic arches with Virtues and prophets at the angles; above, white marble relief panels of the Presentation in the Temple and the Crucifixion flank clustered red colonnettes, with the marble eagle lectern of Saint John at the corner beside the stair">

In the year 1260 — the year of Montaperti, when Tuscany's Guelph<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-guelphs" class="footnote"><sup>4</sup></a> and Ghibelline<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-ghibellines" class="footnote"><sup>5</sup></a> factions met in the bloodiest battle of the century — a sculptor signing himself *Nicola Pisanus* completed a marble pulpit for the Baptistery of Pisa and, in the proud hexameters carved along the rim of its parapet, dated the work and praised his own hand:

> Anno milleno bis centum bisque triceno hoc opus insigne sculpsit Nicola Pisanus.

The inscription is more than a signature; it is a manifesto of artistic self-consciousness with few parallels in the thirteenth century, and it announces a work that would, by common consent of later criticism, redirect the course of Italian sculpture. Vasari, three centuries on, already saw in Nicola the first light after the medieval "darkness"; modern scholarship has dismantled that teleology, yet the pulpit remains the single most consequential sculptural ensemble of the Italian Duecento — the point at which the study of Roman antiquity, absorbed directly from the sarcophagi then standing around the Pisan Camposanto, was fused with Gothic structure and Christian narrative into a new figural language.

Nicola's origins remain a matter of scholarly discussion. Documents call him *Nicola de Apulia*, and it is widely held that he was formed in the South, within the classicising ambience of Frederick II's court workshops — the milieu of Capua's gate and its imperial busts — before migrating to Tuscany around mid-century. Whatever his formation, it was in Pisa that he found both the patronage and the antiquarian environment his art required: the Opera di Santa Maria, flush with the wealth of a maritime republic, and the open-air museum of Roman sarcophagi reused as tombs around the cathedral, whose Phaedras and Meleagers would migrate, transfigured, onto the faces of his pulpit.

The pulpit stands free in the rotunda, to the north-east of Guido Bigarelli's font of 1246, and it was conceived in deliberate dialogue with its setting. Where the font is a low, broad octagon hugging the pavement, Nicola's pulpit is a vertical structure: a hexagonal casket of narrative reliefs raised high on seven columns — six at the angles and one at the centre — so that the preacher's voice issued from an elevated tribune visible throughout the vast cylindrical space. The hexagon was a novel choice. Earlier Tuscan pulpits were rectangular boxes set against walls or screens; Nicola detached the pulpit from the architecture entirely and gave it a centralised, polygonal plan that answers the geometry of the building around it — the six-sided tribune within the octagonal font's orbit, both within Diotisalvi's circle.

The supports themselves compose a calculated hierarchy. Three of the outer columns rise directly from moulded bases; the alternate three are carried on the backs of crouching lions, each subduing its prey — a motif of Lombard-Romanesque ancestry in which the lion, image at once of Christ and of vigilant power, tramples the forces of evil. The central column stands upon a sculpted base of crouching figures — telamon-like forms among animals — a support charged with meaning: the burden of the Word is carried by the created order itself. The shafts are of richly varied materials — grey and pink granites, *breccia* and veined marbles of warm red and gold — their polychromy playing against the white Carrara of the carved zones above, and each is crowned by a capital of deeply drilled acanthus in which the Corinthian order is remembered through a Gothic sensibility: the leaves curl crisply outward, undercut so deeply that they read almost as metalwork.

Upon the capitals rest trilobed arches — their cusps a French Gothic import, among the earliest in Tuscan sculpture — framed by triangular spandrels inhabited by prophets and evangelists in half-length, unfurling their scrolls. But it is at the angles, above the capitals, that Nicola placed the figures that have become emblematic of his revolution: standing personifications, among them the Theological and Cardinal Virtues, treated as fully corporeal presences. The most celebrated is Fortitude, rendered as a nude Hercules with the lion-skin over his shoulder — a heroic male nude, weight-bearing and self-possessed, lifted almost verbatim from Roman relief sculpture and set, without apology, upon a Christian pulpit. No image better condenses the audacity of the ensemble: the classical body, long confined in medieval art to the margins or to the damned, here stands as the very figure of Christian virtue. Around him, the other corner figures — draped, grave, their heads turned with senatorial dignity — mediate between the structural zone below and the narrative casket above, functioning visually as caryatid-like accents and conceptually as the moral foundations upon which the Gospel story rests.

The tribune itself presents five rectangular relief panels of white marble — the sixth side is open for the stair — separated at the angles by clustered colonnettes of red marble and framed above and below by cornices of dense classical ornament.

Read in sequence, they unfold the economy of salvation from Incarnation to Judgment:

the **Nativity** (conflated, in the antique manner of continuous narration, with the Annunciation and the Bath of the Child, the reclining Virgin rendered with the majesty of a Roman matron on a funerary couch);

the **Adoration of the Magi** (whose enthroned Madonna famously translates the Phaedra of a Camposanto sarcophagus into the Mother of God);

the **Presentation in the Temple**, visible in the present photograph on the left face, its crowd of togate elders pressing around the altar with the gravity of a Roman procession;

the **Crucifixion**, seen here frontally, in which the dead Christ — no longer the triumphant open-eyed *Christus victor* of the earlier Middle Ages but the suffering, sagging body of the new devotion — hangs between the swooning Virgin supported by the holy women and the centurion's group confessing the Son of God, the composition organised with an almost tragic symmetry;

and finally the **Last Judgment**, dense with resurrected bodies. Above the parapet, at the corner adjacent to the stair, perches the marble eagle of Saint John, wings spread over the bookrest — the lectern proper — so that the Gospel was physically proclaimed from the evangelist's own symbol.

The style of these reliefs constitutes their historical importance. The figures are carved in high relief, in places almost detached from the ground, and they are packed shoulder to shoulder in the manner of third-century battle sarcophagi: a horror vacui that is not medieval ornamentality but Roman gravitas. Faces are individualised, bearded, weighty; draperies fall in deep troughs and ridges that respond to the bodies beneath; the drill excavates shadows between the forms so that light flickers across the surfaces with pictorial vibrancy. Nicola's people possess mass. They stand, turn, and mourn as physical beings occupying space — and it is this recovered corporeality, more than any single borrowed motif, that separates the pulpit from all Tuscan sculpture before it.

The pulpit is not merely a gallery of classical quotations; it is a coherent theological machine. Its programme ascends from the animal and telamon bases (nature and the old order subdued), through the Virtues (the moral life), past prophets and evangelists (the witnesses), to the narrative of Redemption culminating in Judgment — the whole surmounted by the eagle of the Word. Placed beside the font, it completed the sacramental logic of the building: the neophyte reborn in Guido's octagonal basin would hear, from Nicola's hexagonal tribune, the preaching of the faith into which he had been baptised. Ornament and narrative, the two great languages of Duecento sculpture, thus stood — and stand — a few metres apart, each perfected, each serving its proper liturgical office.

The Pisa pulpit founded a dynasty and a tradition. From its workshop issued Nicola's own pulpit for Siena Cathedral (1265–68), enriched and crowded under the influence of his collaborators; his son Giovanni would carve the pulpits of Pistoia (1301) and of Pisa Cathedral itself (1302–10), translating the father's Roman gravity into Gothic pathos; and [Arnolfo di Cambio](/Content/Artists/XIII-c/Arnolfo-di-Cambio.html), trained in the same bottega, would carry the new figural intelligence to Rome and to the project of Florence Cathedral. Through these channels the lessons of 1260 — the study of the antique, the weight-bearing figure, the narrative relief conceived as drama — passed into the bloodstream of Tuscan art, to be inherited, two generations later, by Giotto in paint and, two centuries later, by the sculptors of the Renaissance who recognised in Nicola their true ancestor. Few single works of the Middle Ages can claim so long a posterity; none states more clearly the moment when Italian sculpture, standing amid the ruins of Rome, first looked at them not as quarry but as school.

The single most consequential object in the Baptistery — indeed one of the founding monuments of Italian sculpture — is the pulpit carved by Nicola Pisano, signed and dated 1260. Work is generally thought to have begun around 1255–1257; the patron is usually identified as Archbishop Federico Visconti<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-federico-visconti" class="footnote"><sup>6</sup></a>, whose *Sermones* have been related to the pulpit's iconographic programme. (Some popular sources give the patron's name as Filippo Visconti; the more authoritative literature favours Federico Visconti, and the discrepancy is noted here rather than concealed.)

The pulpit is revolutionary in form. Whereas earlier Tuscan and southern Italian pulpits — including the pulpit of Guglielmo formerly in Pisa Cathedral, later transferred to Cagliari — were square or rectangular, Nicola's is hexagonal, a free-standing architectural organism in its own right. It is raised on six outer columns of granite and marble, three of which rest on crouching lions (*leoni stilofori*) in the Romanesque tradition, together with a central column whose base is carved with figures of men and animals (telamones). Above the capitals spring trilobed arches with pierced cusps; between the arches and in the spandrels stand figures of prophets, evangelists, the cardinal virtues, Saint John the Baptist and the Archangel Michael, carved almost in the round. Five marble relief panels around the parapet depict scenes from the life of Christ and the end of time: the Nativity, the Adoration of the Magi, the Presentation in the Temple, the Crucifixion, and the Last Judgement.

The pulpit's importance lies in its programmatic revival of classical form. Nicola, whose documents variously call him "Pisanus" and, in 1266, "Nichola Pietri de Apulia" (indicating a Pugliese origin, with a formation in the Hohenstaufen milieu of Frederick II in southern Italy), drew directly upon the Roman sarcophagi and antiquities that were accumulating around the Cathedral and would later be gathered into the Camposanto. The dense, sculptural, weighty figures, the classicising drapery, and above all the celebrated nude figure of Hercules — personifying Fortitude, and often described as the first integral, explicitly antique-derived nude of medieval Italian art — announce a new relationship to the ancient past.

The signed inscription beneath the Last Judgement reads:

> ANNO MILLENO BIS CENTUM BISQUE TRICENO / HOC OPUS INSIGNE SCULPSIT NICOLA PISANUS / LAUDETUR DIGNE TAM BENE DOCTA MANUS

"In the year 1260 Nicola Pisano carved this noble work; may so well-taught a hand be praised as it deserves." The self-consciousness of the signature, with its proud demand for praise, is itself a historical document of the changing status of the artist. For one influential twentieth-century assessment the pulpit was "that false dawn of the Renaissance," its innovations not immediately followed up; but its progeny — Nicola's Siena Cathedral pulpit (1265–1268), and Giovanni Pisano's pulpits at Pistoia (1301) and in Pisa Cathedral — established the pulpit as a primary vehicle of narrative sculpture in Italy.

### Acoustics and Resonance

A celebrated feature of the interior is its acoustics. The double-shell dome, combined with the smooth marble surfaces and the near-total absence of sound-absorbing decoration, gives the space an extraordinarily long reverberation, so that a single sung note appears to sustain and overlap with itself, producing the impression of a chord sung by several voices. The Opera della Primaziale's custodians perform brief vocalisations every thirty minutes to demonstrate the effect for visitors. A published acoustic study based on measurements taken inside the Baptistery in April 2019 reported a reverberation time (T₃₀) of approximately 12.8–13.0 seconds in unoccupied conditions — an exceptionally long value, commonly explained, in guidebooks and on the Opera's own pages, by the geometry of the double dome and the intermediate air cavity it encloses, though the measured evidence in fact points elsewhere.

That study — the fullest technical account yet published, by Dario D'Orazio, Giulia Fratoni, E. Rossi and Massimo Garai of the University of Bologna, "Understanding the acoustics of St. John's Baptistery in Pisa through a virtual approach," *Journal of Building Performance Simulation* 13, no. 3 (2020), 320–333 — deserves to be reported in some detail, because it replaces impression with measurement. The survey was carried out over a single night in April 2019, by permission of the Opera della Primaziale, using the exponential sine-sweep technique with two high-output dodecahedral loudspeakers and six microphones; the sources were placed on the altar, on Nicola Pisano's pulpit and in the matroneum, and sixteen receiver positions were distributed through the central area around the font, the ambulatory and the upper gallery.

Set beside a comparative dataset of centrally planned worship spaces, the result is startling. The Baptistery encloses some 23,000 cubic metres over a floor area of about 720 square metres, with a maximum internal height of some 53 metres, and returns a mid-frequency reverberation time of about 13 seconds; the Rotunda of St George at Thessaloniki manages 4.7 seconds, San Vitale at Ravenna 5.8, the Süleymaniye mosque in Istanbul — four times the volume — 5.5, and the cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow, nearly four times the volume and some ten metres taller, only 8.5. Part of the explanation is simply material: marble accounts for roughly 73 per cent of the Baptistery's internal surface, against some 35–40 per cent in the worship spaces of early Christianity, and polished marble absorbs almost nothing — an absorption coefficient of 0.01 to 0.02 across the spectrum. But material alone does not account for the anomaly, and the building remains, in the authors' word, an "outlier."

The more interesting finding concerns the mechanism, and it corrects the explanation most often repeated to visitors. In comparable domed interiors the reverberation time correlates closely with the height of the dome, sound travelling by a preferred path between floor and vault; the Baptistery departs from that regression too, which indicates that the great conical dome is not the agent. The calibrated computer model made the point precisely. An analysis of free paths — the distances travelled by sound rays between successive reflections — shows a maximum of about 30 metres, corresponding to the vault over the central nucleus: no ray descends from the conical dome to the pavement, and there are accordingly no focusing effects of the kind that make whispering galleries.

The peaks in the distribution fall instead at about 5 metres, the width of the ambulatory and of the matroneum between the columns and the outer wall, and at about 14.5 metres, their height. What prolongs the sound, in other words, is not the dome but the two annular volumes that girdle the interior at ground and gallery level: acoustically coupled to the central space through the screen of columns, they absorb energy, hold it, and release it back through the intercolumniations with a delay, producing a slow, multi-slope decay rather than the simple exponential of a single room. The simulations of the proposed original designs confirm this by contrast. The reconstructions of De Fleury and Boeck, which posit a dome open at the apex, differ from the present building only at low frequencies; but Franca Manenti Valli's reconstruction, with a markedly lower elevation and a shallower gallery, yields a substantially shorter reverberation, because in it the sub-volumes cease to behave as separate reservoirs and merge with the main space. The intermediate cavity between the two shells of the roof, it should be added, is a closed annular void reached only by a service stair, and plays no part in this at all.

Two further results bear on how the building was used. Simulated with a congregation of some 150 standing around the font — the disposition implied by the medieval rite of baptism by immersion, in which candidates entered through the west door and left by the east towards the Cathedral — the reverberation falls to about 10 seconds, and the reverberance actually perceived by those present drops further still: an early decay time of 9.3 seconds when the celebrant speaks from the altar, but only 7.2 seconds from the pulpit, with a centre time (a proxy for intelligibility) of 580 milliseconds against 378, and a lateral energy fraction of 0.40 against 0.22.

The two liturgical stations are thus acoustically differentiated in exactly the way the liturgy requires: prayer and litany from the altar arrive enveloping and diffuse, the sermon from Nicola's pulpit comparatively clear and directed. As for singing, the Bologna team recorded, in anechoic conditions and with four professional singers of the Bolognese Gregorian choir, the *Kyrie* of Guillaume de Machaut's *Messe de Nostre Dame* — a cantus-firmus setting roughly contemporary with the completion of the Baptistery's roof — and analysed its temporal structure. The slow, syllabic, unmeasured emission of such music carries its information at very low modulation frequencies (below about 2 Hz) and shows an effective autocorrelation duration of 100–200 milliseconds, against 40–80 for a Baroque anthem and 20–40 for radio speech; by Ando's rule of thumb this implies a "preferred" reverberation of roughly 5 seconds for chant, 1.5 for the Baroque piece and 0.7 for speech.

The honest conclusion is a qualified one: at 13 seconds the Baptistery exceeds even the optimum for chant, and by every modern index of speech transmission it is simply unusable — but such indices, calibrated on the rhythms of ordinary speech, are meaningless when applied to a repertory of this kind, and the building is far better matched to the music of its own century than the standard figures suggest. It should be stressed, finally, that no document proves that baptisteries were used for music at all; the custodians' vocalisations are a modern rite, and the acoustic itself is best treated as what the acousticians call intangible heritage — a property of the fabric that can be measured today but whose medieval experience must be inferred.

### Subsidence, Restoration and Conservation

Like its neighbours, the Baptistery rests on the unstable alluvial subsoil of the Pisan plain and leans slightly — its inclination directed toward the Cathedral, to the east. The building's present appearance owes a great deal to nineteenth-century restorations, carried out between 1841 and 1856 under the direction first of Alessandro Gherardesca and subsequently of Pietro Bellini, who reworked damaged portions according to the then-prevailing theories of stylistic restoration; the interventions of that period must be borne in mind when reading the building's surfaces. More recent conservation has concentrated on the summit of the dome and the *cupolino*, which, being less protected against weathering, has been particularly susceptible to degradation, with visible effects on the interior.

Both halves of that sentence deserve to be unpacked, and the first of them can be given real precision, because the ground beneath the Piazza is among the most intensively investigated in the history of soil mechanics — the by-product of eleven years' work by the International Committee for the Safeguard and Stabilisation of the Leaning Tower, whose findings were summarised by its chairman Michele Jamiolkowski in 2001.

Beneath a made ground some three metres thick, dense with archaeological material from the third century BC to the seventh century AD, lies Horizon A: roughly seven metres of estuarine sandy and clayey silts laid down in tidal conditions, with a thin bed of fine sand at its base which grows siltier and thinner from north to south across the Piazza. Below that, the marine deposits of Horizon B begin with the layer the Pisans call the *pancone* — a soft, sensitive, highly compressible clay of medium to high plasticity — followed by a stiffer clay, a thin sand and, to a depth of about forty metres, an almost normally consolidated clay; beneath all this Horizon C, twenty to twenty-five metres of fine to medium dense sand.

The water table stands one to two metres below the surface. The contact between Horizon A and the *pancone* runs horizontally under the Piazza as a whole, which makes it an excellent datum: under the Tower it is dished into a depression implying some 2.5 metres of settlement at that interface, and about 3.0 metres overall, with a 0.4-metre upheaval of the clay on the south side — evidence that at some point during construction the Tower came close to outright bearing-capacity failure. The Baptistery, the second-heaviest object on the same compressible ground, is out of plumb toward the east; the figure usually repeated is about 0.6 degrees, some half a metre over its full height, though nothing remotely comparable to the Tower's instrumental record has been published for it, and the number should be treated as an order of magnitude rather than a measurement.

The nineteenth-century restoration, too, needs to be set in a larger frame, for it was not an operation on the Baptistery alone but part of a wholesale re-imagining of the enclosure. Alessandro Gherardesca (1777–1852) pulled down the minor buildings that had accumulated against the monuments — a wing of a monastery, the little church of San Ranierino at the street corner — in order to isolate the four masterpieces in open ground. The result is the grassed expanse that visitors now take for a medieval given: the *prato* from which, three-quarters of a century later, D'Annunzio would draw his phrase about miracles is itself a Romantic invention, an idealised reconstruction of the medieval square in the taste of the age and, in the judgement of recent scholarship, in line with contemporary British fashion.

That the impulse was archaeological as much as picturesque is shown by the most notorious of Gherardesca's works here, and it is a cautionary tale worth recording in full. In 1838 he excavated around the base of the campanile the sunken walkway known as the *catino*, some three metres wide, so that visitors might see the column bases and the upper foundation that the centuries had buried. Because the trench lay below the water table it required continuous dewatering, and geotechnical opinion holds this responsible for the sharp increase in the rate of the Tower's tilt after the plumb-line measurements taken in 1818 by the English architects Edward Cresy and George Ledwell Taylor; the pumping was halted for good only in 1935, when the plinth was grouted and a waterproof *catino* substituted. The desire to display the monuments had come close to destroying one of them.

What was done to the Baptistery itself proceeded in the same spirit. The external campaign ran from 1841 to 1848 under Gherardesca, with the works on site directed by the master mason Giovanni Storni, and it went far beyond consolidation: several portals were rebuilt and much of the decorative apparatus renewed, and — the loss that matters most — numerous sculptures by Nicola and Giovanni Pisano were taken down, in particular the statues set at the summit of the first order, above and within the wimpergs. They were replaced by new figures that made no attempt to imitate the medieval manner, and the originals were almost all lost, save those that survive today in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo, opened in 1986 under Guglielmo De Angelis d'Ossat and reopened after refurbishment in 2019.

The removals did not pass unopposed: they were denounced by Pisan men of letters of the day, among them [Carlo Lasinio](/scholars.html#lasinio-carlo) — an irony worth pausing over, since it is Lasinio's engravings that now serve as the primary record of the Camposanto frescoes destroyed a century after his death. Pietro Bellini (1817–1866), the municipal engineer and Gherardesca's most distinguished pupil, took the interior in hand between 1850 and 1856 — the south portal, already radically reworked in 1837 and 1841, was rebuilt in 1850 — and in 1853, the year after his master's death, was given charge of the wider works in the Piazza. His intervention inside was chiefly subtractive: the removal of the post-medieval furnishings, together with the installation of new glass. A programme of frescoes for the central space was projected and never carried out. The consequence is that the celebrated bareness of the interior, and with it the acoustic described in the previous section, is in substantial part a nineteenth-century artefact — a restored austerity that the visitor experiences as medieval simplicity.

The glazing that Bellini introduced has a history of its own, and one that carries the building into the twentieth century's catastrophes. In the wake of the restorations of 1841–1865 a cycle of historiated windows was installed in fifteen of the sixteen openings of the first order, painted in the second half of the century by Guglielmo Botti (Pisa, 1829–1906), a pupil of Tommaso Gazzarrini and Giuseppe Bezzuoli who from 1853 experimented with firing techniques that he believed had recovered the lost procedures of the late Gothic glass-painters. The cycle was gravely damaged by the aerial bombardment of February 1944 — the same season of attacks on Pisa that would, in July of that year, set the roof of the Camposanto alight — and further degraded by long exposure.

Restoration was entrusted to the Centro Conservazione e Restauro "La Venaria Reale" at Turin, working under the direction of the Istituto Centrale per il Restauro in Rome, in a campaign concluded in 2018; four of the panels proved beyond recovery and were replaced by new windows commissioned through an international competition won by the Grosseto glass-painter Francesco Mori, dedicated to San Ranieri, Paul VI, John Paul II and Giuseppe Toniolo. The published counts — sixteen windows, fifteen glazed, ten judged restorable, four remade — do not fully reconcile in the press accounts, and the reader is referred to the volume that documents the campaign, *Arte vetraria nel Battistero di Pisa. Restauri e nuove opere*, edited by Gabriella Garzella and others (Pisa: Pacini, 2020).

The modern conservation regime differs from the nineteenth century's in method as much as in scale. Its context is hydrogeological before it is architectural: intensive and extensive pumping from the deep sands of Horizon C in the twentieth century drew the piezometric level in the lower aquifer down by metres within a single year, inducing a downward flow of water that produced a general subsidence of the Piazza — a modern, man-made aggravation of a medieval problem. Against that background the Tower was closed to the public in January 1990 and stabilised over the following decade by highly controlled underexcavation of soil from beneath its northern edge, an operation that recovered some 1,800 arc-seconds of tilt, about a tenth of the maximum recorded in 1993, and returned the building approximately to the position it had held at the beginning of the nineteenth century — which is to say that the twentieth century's most celebrated feat of geotechnical engineering consisted, in effect, in undoing Gherardesca.

Instrumental monitoring of the Tower's inclination, begun in 1911, had shown the rate of tilt rising from about three arc-seconds a year in the 1940s to five or six by the late 1980s. For the Baptistery the regime is one of continuous, undramatic maintenance by the Opera della Primaziale. The most recent campaign, designed and approved in 2020 and co-financed by the Fondazione Pisa, began in the spring of 2021 under the Opera's technical director Roberto Cela, with the Florentine firm Cellini executing the work.

It addressed first the intrados of the dome and the small vaults of the matroneum, whose plaster — its binder exhausted, the surfaces stained and cracked, flaking away in the sheets the restorers call *distacchi a cartella*, and pierced by reopened putlog holes — was cleaned back to sound substrate, re-adhered by grout injection, and made good with lime and eco-pozzolana mortars entirely free of cement, selected for chemical, physical and elastic-mechanical compatibility with the original supports. That phase was presented as complete in April 2024, whereupon the temporary structures were lowered to the ground floor for the marbles, columns, piers, plasters and doors, a further two years' work deliberately staged so as to avoid another prolonged closure. Reversibility, compatibility, minimal intervention and full documentation: the principles are the exact inverse of those that governed the 1840s, and the fabric of the Baptistery is in consequence a stratigraphy of restoration philosophies quite as legible as its stratigraphy of building campaigns.

## The Camposanto Monumentale

### Foundation Traditions: Legend and Documented Fact

No feature of the Camposanto is more famous, or more in need of critical handling, than the tradition of its foundation upon holy earth. According to the "legend of the holy earth," the Pisan fleet, returning from a crusade, brought back shiploads of soil from Golgotha (Calvary), which was strewn over the cathedral's new burial ground so that Pisans might be interred in earth from the site of the Crucifixion; a further belief held that bodies buried in this soil would decompose within twenty-four hours. In most versions the protagonist is Archbishop Ubaldo Lanfranchi, and the act is variously dated to around 1200 or, in some accounts, 1203, following the Third or Fourth Crusade (the sources are inconsistent as to which).

  <figcaption>The Camposanto Monumentale and the Piazza dei Miracoli, Pisa.</figcaption>
</figure>

Critical scholarship requires that legend and documented fact be firmly separated here. The archival and structural evidence indicates that the foundation of the *building* dates not from the end of the twelfth century but from 1277, and that the legend of the holy earth is a later accretion. As the University of Zurich source project has shown, the "legend of the holy earth" begins to circulate in written sources only from the later fourteenth century; the earliest documentary references to the project of a new cemetery date from 1260, when the incoming head of the Opera del Duomo, Guido Spadaio, took an oath that included the promise to build a new cemetery — a promise repeated by his successors. The purpose was practical and civic: to gather into one dignified, enclosed space the burials that had for centuries accumulated in the open ground around the Cathedral, and to clear that ground for sacred and municipal ceremonies. Legends of foundation upon imported Holy Land soil are, moreover, attached to comparable sites elsewhere in Europe, which further undercuts the historicity of the Pisan tradition. The Golgotha narrative is therefore treated here as a meaningful medieval myth of sanctification — one that, as recent scholarship stresses, decisively shaped the iconography of the frescoes — rather than as documented history, and the Lanfranchi attribution is flagged as legendary.

### The Building of the Cloister from 1277

The decisive event is documented precisely. On 19 June 1277, shortly before his death, Archbishop Federico Visconti donated the parcel of land that the Opera required — an area of gardens and vineyards between the city walls (built 1155–1161) and the public way running from the Lion's Gate to the northern flank of the Cathedral. Whoever entered the completed cemetery through the main door read an inscription recording the historical circumstances of its foundation: in the year 1278, in the time of Archbishop Federico Visconti and the podestà Tarlato, under the operaio Orlando Sardella, "master Giovanni" built this. This "master Giovanni" is generally identified with the Pisan architect Giovanni di Simone (though some older tradition proposed a "Giovanni di Nicola," and the alternative is noted). Construction was slowed by the crisis following the defeat at Meloria in 1284; as late as 1358 the foundations of the northern side had not yet been dug.

Crucially, the building visible today has little in common with the first project. As the documents show, the earliest scheme (associated with Giovanni di Simone) consisted of an open, walled burial field — for which the new name *campus sanctus*, "holy field," was coined, first attested in a document of 1278 — with a sanctuary, the *ecclesia campi sancti* dedicated to the Holy Trinity, erected at the south-east corner to supply the liturgical infrastructure the detached burial ground otherwise lacked. Only under the operaio Giovanni Rossi (in office 1319–1332) was this first project abandoned in favour of the revolutionary masterplan that governs the building as it stands: four enormous roofed galleries arranged around a central open lawn on the model of a cloister, but on a giant scale and conceived as public space, providing hundreds of stable, gridded burial places under cover, while the treed central lawn offered other modes of interment. The Church of the Trinity was largely dismantled, only a few years after its completion, to make way for this scheme.

  <img src="/Images/Battistero-and-Camposanto-Pisa/battistero-and-camposanto-pisa-07.jpg" alt="The Façade of the Camposanto Monumentale">
  <figcaption>The Façade of the Camposanto Monumentale, Pisa.</figcaption>
</figure>

From the 1320s the work proceeded steadily; large parts of the eastern and southern galleries were complete by the early 1330s, when the first frescoes were begun there. The exterior façade toward the Piazza, faced in white marble with a regular order of slender pilasters crowned by round arches — deliberately Romanesque rather than Gothic, to harmonise with the Cathedral's flanks — was completed with two portals corresponding to the main entrances of the Cathedral and Baptistery. The much-admired leaden roof was finished toward the end of the fourteenth century. (The number of blind arches on the exterior is given differently in different sources — commonly 43, but sometimes 48 — and the discrepancy is noted.) The main portal is surmounted by a Gothic tabernacle executed by Lupo di Francesco, containing a Virgin and Child with saints by a follower of Giovanni Pisano.

### The Gothic Tracery and the Chapels

The interior galleries acquired their present "Gothic" appearance only in the fifteenth century. Between 1451 and 1463 the wide round-arched openings between the galleries and the central lawn were transformed by the insertion of filigree marble tracery, creating the great mullioned *quadrifora* windows; these were originally intended as frames for stained glass meant to protect the galleries from the weather, a first series of which was installed in the eastern part of the southern gallery before being transferred to the Cathedral around 1490.

The Camposanto contains three chapels. The oldest is the Cappella Ammannati, dating from around 1360, named for the wall-tomb of Ligo Ammannati, a professor of medicine at the University of Pisa. The man commemorated is known to us above all through his tomb. Ligo Ammannati was a professor of medicine at the University of Pisa, and the documents record that it was he himself who charged his testamentary executors with providing, after his death, for the construction of a tomb along the northern arm of the Camposanto — a characteristically Trecento arrangement in which the monument was an act of the patron's own will, executed posthumously by his fiduciaries.

  <img src="/Images/Battistero-and-Camposanto-Pisa/battistero-and-camposanto-pisa-08.jpg" alt="The tomb of Ligo Ammannati">

The contract survives in its essentials. On 8 June 1359 the commission for the tomb of the "maestro medico" Ligo Ammannati, destined for his funerary chapel in the north corridor of the Camposanto, was entrusted to four masters — Francesco del fu Lippo, Colo di Coscio Mucido, Matteo di Tone, and Puccio di Landuccio — and the work was completed by 17 May 1360, as established in the documentary study of Michele Luzzati (1969). The tight eleven-month span between commission and completion, and the precision of the act itself, are remarkable: the conformation and decoration of the tomb correspond exactly — minor details apart — to what was stipulated in the act of commission. Few Trecento funerary monuments can be checked against their contract so closely; the Ammannati tomb is, in this sense, a document in marble.

Of the four masters, only Puccio di Landuccio has acquired a scholarly profile. First securely mentioned in November 1355 among the masters who had worked on the Ponte a Mare of Pisa, and resident in the cappella of S. Maria Maggiore — which may indicate employment at the Duomo, he has been plausibly associated, on the stylistic evidence of the Ammannati tomb, with a group of Pisan sculptures close to, but not identical with, the manner of Nino Pisano: among them the Crucified Christ from the lunette of the Camposanto's south-west door (now in S. Michele in Borgo), a Madonna and Child from S. Maria della Spina (Museo Nazionale di S. Matteo), and the tomb of Archbishop Francesco Moricotti (Museo dell'Opera del Duomo). The tomb thus stands within the orbit of the Pisano workshop tradition in its late, post-Nino phase, executed by masters of the cathedral milieu rather than by a single celebrated name.

The monument is carved in white Carrara marble, and its structure is that of an arcosolium — the wall-tomb under an arch. The lower element is a tripartite sarcophagus carried on three foliate consoles: at its centre, within a trilobed arch, appears the figure of Christ in Pietà, flanked on either side by two coats of arms of the deceased. Above, a polylobed aedicule encloses the chest upon which the effigy lies, a book in hand; the whole is crowned by pinnacles and by a central gabled high relief representing a teacher in his cathedra surrounded by eight students.

The iconography deserves emphasis, for it is here that the tomb speaks most clearly. The *Cristo passo* on the sarcophagus front places the dead man's hope in the Passion; the recumbent effigy with its book declares his identity as a man of learning; and the gable above translates that identity into narrative — the *magister* in his chair, his eight *scolares* gathered about him, the lecture hall carried into eternity. This scheme belongs to the great fourteenth-century tradition of professors' tombs developed above all in Bologna for the doctors of law and medicine, where the teaching scene became the defining image of the academic dead; the Ammannati monument transplants that ideal to Pisa and to its new civic cemetery, commemorating not lineage or office but the profession of teaching itself.

The commission had consequences for the building. The wall-tomb of 1359–1360, once completed, was taken as the model for the opening of fourteen arches that were intended to serve as entrances to new chapels along the north wall; these were subsequently walled up — with the exception of the Aulla chapel — to make way for Benozzo Gozzoli's famous frescoes of Old Testament stories. The western chapel of the north corridor in which the monument stood took the name of its illustrious occupant, and the Cappella Ammannati is accordingly reckoned the oldest of the Camposanto's chapels.

The tomb was also an innovation in funerary practice within the cemetery itself. Until then, burials in the Camposanto took place either beneath the pavement, covered with marble slabs, or in ancient Roman sarcophagi reused according to the fashion of the time; the tomb of Ligo Ammannati constituted an exception — the first monumental wall-tomb of the complex, introducing into the galleries the elevated, architecturally framed sepulchre of the honoured individual. In the logic of the Camposanto's second project, where the dead were ordered in a regular grid beneath the pavement, the Ammannati arcosolium opened a new register of commemoration, one that the fourteen arches were evidently intended to multiply for other families before the fresco campaigns closed that possibility.

The monument's modern history is one of movement and recovery. The work remained in its original location until the mid-1930s, when the complex was dismantled and recomposed on the wall of the western corridor near the building's entrance, in substitution for the sixteenth-century tomb of Francesco Vegio. It was therefore standing in the west gallery when catastrophe struck the Camposanto in the Second World War: damaged, though only marginally, by the bombardment of 1944, the monument returned to its original place only in 1990, after a campaign of restoration. It thus escaped the fate of the frescoes above it, and stands today once more in the chapel that bears its patron's name — the earliest surviving statement, within the Camposanto, of the professor's tomb as a genre, and a rare Trecento monument whose contract, makers, dates, and displacements can all be followed in the sources.

The second is the Cappella Aulla which contains an altarpiece in glazed terracotta by Giovanni della Robbia (1518) and preserves the incense lamp said to be the one that hung in the Cathedral in Galileo's day and whose oscillations, according to the tradition transmitted by his disciple Vincenzo Viviani, prompted his study of the isochronism of the pendulum.

The Cappella Aulla belongs to the Monumental Camposanto of Pisa: it is situated along the north wall, on the right-hand side, in direct correspondence with the old entrance on the south side, now closed — that is, opposite the portal beneath the Gothic tabernacle that once served as the cemetery's principal door. Its architectural history is bound to the episode already encountered in the story of the Ammannati tomb: after the completion of that wall-tomb in 1359–1360, it was taken as the model for the opening of fourteen arches intended to serve as entrances to new chapels, which were subsequently walled up — with the exception of the Aulla chapel — to make way for Benozzo Gozzoli's famous frescoes of Old Testament stories. The Aulla chapel is thus the sole survivor of that abortive fourteenth-century scheme for a row of family chapels along the north gallery: the one arch of the fourteen that actually became, and remained, a chapel. Today it is closed by a small gate, and communicates on the left with the Cappella Ammannati through a small room currently closed, and on the right with the rear exit, closed to the public.

The chapel's principal ornament is its altar dossal, representing the Assumption of the Virgin among saints and prophets, by Giovanni della Robbia, of 1518 — some accounts give the execution as extending to 1520, and describe a polychrome glazed terracotta altarpiece with the Assunta in glory and four saints, together with a predella with stories of the saints. Giovanni della Robbia (1469–1529), son of Andrea and grand-nephew of Luca, inherited the great Florentine workshop and its jealously guarded art of glazed sculpture; the Pisan dossal belongs to his mature production, and its contract survives in part: the agreement stipulated that the work be executed

> in terra cocta ismaltata et invetriata et adornata ad oleum... auro brunito et a mordente

glazed and fired terracotta, enriched with oil colouring, burnished gold, and mordant gilding: a document of unusual technical precision, showing the patron purchasing not merely an image but the full chromatic apparatus of the Della Robbia manner.

The altarpiece was not made for the Camposanto at all. It stood originally in the church of San Marco in Calcesana, then in San Silvestro, and was finally installed in its present location. Its first home has its own well-documented history: San Marco in Calcesana, near the Porta Calcesana on the road to Calci, is first attested on 18 January 1139, and remained under the patronage of the nuns of San Matteo until 1387, passing thereafter to various Pisan families; from 1508 the building underwent an important campaign of restoration involving the remaking of the façade and campanile and the commissioning from Giovanni della Robbia of the altar representing the Assumption among saints and prophets.

The dossal was thus the culminating ornament of an early sixteenth-century renewal of a twelfth-century church. When the church, repeatedly suppressed and reopened between the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, was definitively deconsecrated in 1819, the altarpiece began the migration — via San Silvestro — that ended in the Camposanto, where the Aulla chapel became its refuge. Like the Roman sarcophagi in the galleries outside, the dossal is therefore a transplant: the Camposanto acting, as so often in its later history, as the ark into which the city's displaced sacred art was gathered.

From the vault of the chapel hangs the object that draws most visitors: a lamp once located in the cathedral, which legend holds to have inspired in Galileo Galilei the theory of the isochronism of the pendulum, after he watched it swing.

The tradition rests on a single, late, and much-loved source. Vincenzo Viviani (1622–1703), Galileo's last disciple and first biographer, recounts in his *Racconto istorico* — composed in 1654 and published in 1717 — the first "experiences" of the isochronism of the pendulum which his master, then a young student of medicine, would have made in Pisa between 1581 and 1584: watching a lamp set swinging in the cathedral, Galileo measured its time using the beat of his own pulse. Viviani writes seventy years after the supposed event, and no contemporary document corroborates the scene; it belongs to the same biographical dossier as the weights dropped from the Leaning Tower, and modern scholarship treats both with the same measured scepticism.

  <img src="/Images/Battistero-and-Camposanto-Pisa/battistero-and-camposanto-pisa-10.jpg" alt="The Lamp of Galileo Galilei">
  <figcaption>The Lamp of Galileo Galilei in the Camposanto of Pisa.</figcaption>
</figure>

The object itself, however, can be interrogated — and here the Aulla lamp earns its place in the story. The lamp visible today in the Duomo dates to 1587: it is therefore later than the period in which Galileo was a student and cannot correspond to the one of which Viviani speaks. The true lamp — smaller and plainer — should instead be of the same type as the one now preserved in the Monumental Camposanto, in the Cappella Aulla.

The great bronze lamp of the cathedral is the work of Vincenzo Possenti, lamp-maker and bell-founder, commissioned in June 1584, executed in brass and decorated with bronze figures — celebrated for the legend linking it to the name of Galileo; the association is so entrenched that the old 2,000-lire banknote carried Galileo's portrait on one side and, on the other, a hanging lamp — which turns out to be the present cathedral lamp: an "error" that shows how widespread Viviani's anecdote had become.

The humbler vessel in the Aulla chapel, transferred from the cathedral, is thus the better candidate for whatever lamp a young medical student may actually have watched — though the honest formulation remains conditional. And one final precision that the tradition itself requires: a pendulum is strictly isochronous only when its oscillations are of small amplitude, as Huygens discovered a few decades later — so that even the physics of the legend, like its history, is true only within limits.

The Cappella Aulla is a small room with a stratified history: architecturally, the lone realised survivor of the fourteen chapel-arches projected in the wake of the Ammannati tomb; artistically, the final home of a documented Della Robbia altarpiece of 1518, exiled from its deconsecrated mother church; and culturally, the shrine of a relic of modern science — a lamp whose claim rests on a disciple's affectionate memory rather than on any archive, but whose chronology, unlike the cathedral lamp's, at least does not contradict the tale. In its way the chapel condenses the whole later destiny of the Camposanto: no longer the burial machine of the Trecento commune, but the treasury in which Pisa deposited, century after century, the objects it could not bear to lose.

The third and grandest is the Cappella Dal Pozzo which opens at the centre of the eastern gallery of the Camposanto — the arm of the building that had always been its most sacred: raised two steps above the rest, conceived as a kind of presbyterial zone where the altars for liturgical functions stood. Yet the chapel itself is a latecomer. Contrary to what has been stated by many modern researchers, this space did not exist prior to 1475: all the altars needed for liturgical celebration had until then been erected along the wall of the eastern gallery.

Construction of the new chapel was begun according to the last will of Archbishop Filippo de' Medici (in office 1461–1474), was left unfinished in the fifteenth century, and assumed its definitive shape only under Archbishop Carlo Antonio Dal Pozzo<a href="/endnotes.html#fn-carlo-antonio-dal-pozzo" class="footnote"><sup>7</sup></a> (1582–1607). The chapel that bears the Dal Pozzo name is therefore the completion, more than a century later, of a Medici bequest — a fact the audioguide literature compresses into the formula that Dal Pozzo erected it in 1594 "su una preesistenza più antica," upon an older pre-existing structure. Projecting far beyond the roof of the galleries, the chapel with its high dome created a new focal point at the eastern end of the cemetery's longitudinal axis — the one vertical accent in a building otherwise defined by its unbroken horizontals.

The man who gave the chapel its definitive form deserves more than a date. Carlo Antonio Dal Pozzo (1547–1607) came from the noble family of Dal Pozzo Della Cisterna of Biella in Piedmont, and was the uncle of Cassiano dal Pozzo, the celebrated antiquarian and patron of Poussin. Appointed to the see of Pisa in the autumn of 1582, he proved one of the great builder-archbishops of the city's post-medieval history: among his many works, besides the chapel in the Camposanto (1594), he founded the Collegio Puteano (1605) to host students from Piedmont, and had cast the bell that bears his name — the "Dal Pozzo" — for the Leaning Tower (1606). His features are known to us through one of the most distinguished portrait busts of the early Baroque: the marble executed by Gianlorenzo Bernini in 1622–24, commissioned in the ambit of his nephew Cassiano and now in the National Galleries of Scotland, Edinburgh — so that the archbishop of Pisa, by a curious afterlife, became one of Bernini's early sitters.

The chapel was added to the structure of the Camposanto in 1594, along the east wall, and is distinguished by its characteristic dome. Within, above the altar, stands the painting of Saint Jerome by Aurelio Lomi, dated 1595 — the work of the leading Pisan painter of the generation, which gave the chapel its dedication: it is documented as the Cappella di San Girolamo. The choice of the penitent scholar-saint for a cemetery chapel needs no forcing: Jerome in meditation before the crucifix and the skull was the Counter-Reformation image of learned penance, and a fitting titular for the private chapel of a prelate of the Dal Pozzo's stamp.

That the chapel was conceived as a tomb is explicit in the sources. From 1600 the archbishop had prepared and arranged the Chapel of San Girolamo in the Camposanto of Pisa as his own funerary chapel. He was able to enjoy his foundations only briefly: in 1607 his health worsened suddenly while he was on villeggiatura at Seravezza, and he died on 13 July of that year; his remains were conducted to the Baptistery of San Giovanni, and the exequies were celebrated in the cathedral, where a catafalque was erected, decorated by the "pittore di Borgho" with four painted coats of arms for the funeral. One honest caveat is required here: the sources consulted describe the preparation of the chapel as his burial place and the itinerary of his obsequies, but do not state in so many words the final deposition of his body; I therefore leave that last step unasserted rather than supply it. The fundamental modern study of the chapel — where such questions are pursued on documentary grounds — is Arabella Cifani and Franco Monetti, "La cappella Dal Pozzo del Camposanto di Pisa," in *Saggi e memorie di Storia dell'arte*, 24 (2001).

The chapel's most recent chapter has restored it to active sacred use. In 2009 the Holy Relics of the Cathedral were moved to the chapel: these included relics of eleven of the twelve Apostles, two fragments of the True Cross, a thorn from Christ's Crown of Thorns, and a small piece of material from the dress of the Virgin Mary. The reliquaries of the Opera del Duomo are gathered within the chapel in two purpose-made cabinets. The translation has a certain historical fitness. The Camposanto of the second Trecento project had grounded its sanctity in the legendary earth of Golgotha spread beneath its lawn; seven centuries later, the relics of the Passion — Cross and Thorn — entered the building by the east door, so that the cemetery which had once claimed holiness through its soil now houses it, catalogued and enshrined, beneath Dal Pozzo's dome. The chapel communicates with the rest of the Camposanto through a wooden portal, and with the exterior through the entrance now fitted with the accessibility ramp — the archbishop's chapel serving, prosaically but symbolically, as one of the building's living doors.

Of the Camposanto's three chapels, the Dal Pozzo is the grandest and the most stratified in its patronage: willed by one archbishop in the 1470s, completed by another in the 1590s, painted by Lomi in 1595, appointed as a funerary chapel in 1600, portrayed — through its patron — by Bernini's chisel in the 1620s, and converted into the cathedral's treasury of relics in 2009. Read together with the Ammannati and Aulla chapels, it completes a small history of the building's changing self-understanding: the Trecento professor's tomb, the Renaissance altarpiece in exile, and the Counter-Reformation archbishop's domed mausoleum — three centuries of Pisan commemoration gathered under one leaden roof.

### The Camposanto as Burial Place and Early Museum

The Camposanto's function as the pantheon of Pisan civic memory is inseparable from its role as one of the earliest museums of ancient sculpture in Europe. Its galleries and lawn hold tomb-slabs of Pisan patricians underfoot and, along the walls, a celebrated collection of reused Roman sarcophagi, together with Etruscan urns, Greek stelae and a large body of Latin inscriptions. Many of these antiquities had accumulated around the Cathedral from the earlier Middle Ages and were reused as tombs for prominent citizens according to the fashion of the age; the reuse of a Roman sarcophagus for the mother of Countess Matilda of Canossa is among the well-known early instances. Some 84 Roman sarcophagi, most of them of the third century AD, still survive today. This assemblage made the Camposanto a resource for artists: it was precisely these classical models that nourished Nicola Pisano's revival of antique form, and the cemetery functioned for centuries as a school of the antique in the heart of Tuscany, an aspect that recent scholarship has connected to Pisa's persistent ambition to present itself as a "new Rome."

The Canossa burial deserves to be told in full, because it is the clearest single case of what reuse meant here. Beatrice of Lorraine, mother of Matilda of Canossa, died at Pisa on 18 April 1076 and was laid in a Roman sarcophagus of the second century carved with the myth of Phaedra and Hippolytus, set against the south flank of the Cathedral. Its epitaph, in leonine hexameters, turns the container's grandeur into a formula of humility:

> Quamvis peccatrix sum domna vocata Beatrix, / in tumulo missa iaceo quae comitissa

"Sinner though I am, I was called the lady Beatrice; I who was a countess lie here, laid in the tomb."

Tiziana Lazzari has argued that the choice of container was Matilda's own, made around 1100 during her first documented stay in Pisa, some twenty-four years after the death it commemorated, and that it presupposes an educated reading of the classical relief and a deliberate appeal to the prestige of the antique. The sarcophagus was displaced early in the fourteenth century when the cathedral steps were built — 1302 by Lazzari's reckoning, 1303 in other accounts, and the sources disagree over whether the move took it within the building or to the outer wall of the apse — and it reached the Camposanto only in 1810, the transfer recorded by an inscription of that year; Alessandro da Morrona published in 1812 a further text, dated 1116, in which Matilda endows the church for her mother's soul. The reuse is therefore not antiquarian recycling but a dynastic argument conducted in Roman marble, and it long predates the building that now houses it.

That same sarcophagus is the pivot of the Camposanto's claim to have been a school of the antique, and the claim can be tested rather than merely asserted. Vasari made the connection in the *Vite*, writing that Nicola, pondering the beauty of the ancient work and greatly pleased by it, put such study and diligence into imitating that manner and other good sculptures among the antique sarcophagi that he was soon held the best sculptor of his day. The classic demonstration is the Phaedra relief itself: the seated Phaedra with her attendants supplies the Virgin of the Adoration of the Magi on the Baptistery pulpit of 1260, and figures from the Calydonian hunt sarcophagus have likewise been traced in the Massacre of the Innocents.

Two cautions are in order. The individual derivations are art-historical inferences, secure in the aggregate but contested in detail, and the antique nude of Fortitude is better described as drawing on the general repertory available in and around the Piazza than as copied from any one identified marble. And the collection Nicola saw was not the collection we see: the marbles then stood dispersed around the Cathedral, on its façade and against its flanks, and were gathered into the galleries only much later. The lesson was still being learnt at the other end of the Renaissance — Veronica Sofia Tulli has proposed a direct iconographic link between three of the combatants in Michelangelo's *Battle of the Centaurs* and three fighting figures on the late Roman sarcophagus catalogued A 13 est. in the Camposanto, mediated by Bertoldo di Giovanni's bronze relief.

That siglum indicates the scholarly instrument by which the antiquities are cited. The standard catalogue is Paolo Enrico Arias, Emilio Cristiani and Emilio Gabba, *Camposanto monumentale di Pisa: le Antichità*, published at Pisa in 1977, whose sigla — A 13 est. and the like, keyed to gallery and position — remain the normal way of referring to individual pieces in the literature. What the catalogue describes is a repertory rather than a collection in the museological sense. Beside the mythological sarcophagi — Phaedra and Hippolytus, Selene and Endymion, the Rape of Proserpina, the Calydonian hunt — stand portrait sarcophagi with the deceased carried in a *clipeus* held aloft by the Seasons, together with Etruscan urns, Greek funerary stelae and a large body of Latin inscriptions. The point worth insisting on is that nothing here was ever collected. The marbles arrived in Pisa as merchandise, ballast and spoil, were adopted as prestige containers by families who wanted their dead housed in porphyry-grade antiquity, and only afterwards, and gradually, came to be read as works of art. The Camposanto's status as one of the earliest museums of ancient sculpture in Europe is thus a retrospective description of an accumulation that began for quite other reasons.

The conversion of that accumulation into a museum proper is the work of one man and one decade. Carlo Lasinio (Treviso, 15 February 1759 – Pisa, 27 March 1838), a reproductive engraver formed in the Uffizi milieu, was appointed *conservatore del Camposanto* on 10 June 1807 by Maria Luisa of Bourbon, at a stipend of 1,260 lire a year, and held the office until his death. He gathered into the galleries works from the churches and convents suppressed under the Napoleonic administration — sparing them dispersal and, incidentally, making the building the first public museum of the city — and he compiled the analytical inventories and schedules that constitute the beginning of modern museum practice at Pisa. The ambiguity of his position should be recorded: the same regime whose suppressions filled his galleries also stripped them, and in 1812 Lasinio had to superintend the despatch to Paris of panels by Cimabue, Giotto and Taddeo di Bartolo.

His engravings did more than anything else to make the monument famous. The *Pitture a fresco del Campo Santo di Pisa* appeared in December 1812 in forty plates, executed in a deliberately flat bite for legibility, with a second edition in 1828; his son Giovanni Paolo engraved the *Raccolta di sarcofagi, urne e altri monumenti di scultura del Campo Santo di Pisa* (Pisa: co' caratteri di Didot, 1814) in one hundred and fifty-eight plates, and a further forty-six plates of the frescoes in 1832. These volumes are now primary documents of the first importance for what was lost in 1944. Nor was his practice uncontested in his own lifetime: in 1836 Bruno Scorzi publicly accused him of damaging what he restored, and although the Accademia di Belle Arti vindicated him, the episode opens the long argument over intervention that the fresco cycle has generated ever since.

The building's other identity, as a pantheon of civic and European memory, is legible in the modern monuments that crowd the galleries, and the most conspicuous of them is foreign. Francesco Algarotti — Venetian, Newtonian, Fellow of the Royal Society, born in 1712 — died at Pisa on 3 May 1764, and Frederick II of Prussia had a monument raised over him at his own expense, to a design by Mauro Tesi, with the dedication *Algarotto Ovidii aemulo, Newtoni discipulo, Fridericus rex*: to Algarotti, rival of Ovid, disciple of Newton, from Frederick the King. Beside it stands the phrase Algarotti had chosen for himself out of Horace, *Algarottus, sed non omnis* — Algarotti, but not all of him. Giovanni Volpato's etching of 1769, made after a drawing by Carlo Bianconi, shows the tomb surrounded by admiring spectators: the Camposanto was already a fixed station of the Grand Tour, and its monuments were designed to be looked at by travellers.

That reception is now itself edited as a corpus, in the University of Zurich's "Written Camposanto: The Pisan Cemetery through the Eyes of Chroniclers, Artists and Travellers," which assembles chronicles, pilgrimage narratives, guidebooks, geographical and art-historical writings from the thirteenth to the eighteenth century in six languages. What such a body of testimony makes plain is that "burial place" and "museum" were never successive phases of the building's life but simultaneous readings of it: the sarcophagi were prestige containers before they were antiquities, the frescoes were devotional images before they were art history, and each generation of visitors found in the galleries the thing it had come to look for. The standard modern account of that whole process remains [Clara Baracchini](/scholars.html#baracchini-clara) and [Enrico Castelnuovo](/scholars.html#castelnuovo-enrico), eds., *Il Camposanto di Pisa* (Turin: Einaudi, 1996), whose second part is devoted precisely to the transformations and the fortunes of the monument.

### The Frescoes: A Campaign of a Century and a Half

The chief source of the Camposanto's modern fame is the vast cycle of frescoes that once covered the gallery walls — estimates of its surface range from roughly 2,000 to as much as 2,600 square metres, in either case an area exceeding that of the Sistine Chapel — created over roughly a century and a half, from about 1336 to about 1485. Because of its unequalled scale, the Camposanto gave painters a spatial framework that existed nowhere else, and because of its particular funerary function it permitted departures from the standardised iconographic programmes of church decoration.

The first campaign occupied the ritual core in the south-eastern corner. On the east wall Francesco Traini painted a monumental Crucifixion around 1330. On the south wall was painted the most famous ensemble: the Triumph of Death, the Last Judgement (with a strikingly expanded Hell), and the Thebaid — scenes from the lives of the Desert Fathers offered as models for the preservation of the soul. The programme, likely shaped by Dominican preaching, confronted the visitor with death's omnipotence and unpredictability, the particular judgement of the soul, the terrors of damnation, and the redemptive example of the hermits.

To the west along the south wall, Giotto's pupil Taddeo Gaddi painted the story of Job around 1341–1343, introducing the two-register, six-scene layout that would govern all subsequent phases. After a long interruption, a cycle of the life of San Ranieri, the Pisan patron, was commissioned in 1376 by Pietro Gambacorta: begun by Andrea di Firenze (Andrea Bonaiuti) and, left unfinished at his death, completed by Antonio Veneziano in 1384–1386. The final section of the south wall, devoted to the Sardinian martyrs Ephesus and Potitus — recalling Pisa's lost dominion over Sardinia — was painted by Spinello Aretino in 1391–1392.

In the north gallery, the Opera commissioned from Pietro di Puccio of Orvieto, as early as 1389–1390, a great cosmological diagram (the "macrocosm") and the first scenes of an Old Testament cycle. That cycle — the Camposanto's most extensive narrative — was continued about eight decades later, under Archbishop Filippo de' Medici, by Benozzo Gozzoli, who was commissioned in January 1469 to cover the long north wall. His immense series of Old Testament scenes — variously counted as some twenty-three to twenty-six large narratives, plus an Adoration of the Magi with an Annunciation over the Ammannati chapel door — occupied him for more than fifteen years: the earliest, the Wine Harvest and Drunkenness of Noah, is signed and dated 1469, and the Visit of the Queen of Sheba to Solomon was finished in May 1484. So esteemed was the work that in 1478 the citizens of Pisa granted Benozzo a tomb beneath his own fresco of Joseph interpreting Pharaoh's dream. The Gozzoli campaign, undertaken decades after the Florentine conquest of 1406, expressed a renewed appropriation of the Camposanto as a monument of Tuscan cultural identity.

### The Attribution Debate: From Vasari to Buffalmacco

The authorship of the great south-wall frescoes — the Triumph of Death and its companions — constitutes one of the most instructive controversies in the historiography of Italian painting, and it must be presented as a sequence of shifting positions rather than as a settled fact. No archival document names the painter, and the earliest literary sources (Ghiberti's *Commentarii*, the Anonimo Magliabechiano, a late fifteenth-century laudatory poem) mention the name of Buonamico Buffalmacco most often but without specification. In the second edition of the *Vite* (1568), Vasari assigned the parts individually: the east-wall Passion cycle to Buffalmacco, the Triumph of Death and Last Judgement to Andrea di Cione (Orcagna), the Hell to his brother Nardo di Cione, and the Thebaid to Pietro Lorenzetti. Vasari's authority carried these attributions through most later literature.

In the nineteenth century, connoisseurs rejected the attributions to Orcagna and Lorenzetti; [Igino Benvenuto Supino](/scholars.html#supino-igino-benvenuto), in *Il Camposanto di Pisa* (Florence: Fratelli Alinari, 1896), the first modern monograph on the cemetery, proposed instead — tentatively — the local painter Francesco Traini. This Traini attribution was powerfully corroborated by [Millard Meiss](/scholars.html#meiss-millard) in a landmark article of 1933, "The Problem of Francesco Traini" (*The Art Bulletin* 15, no. 2, pp. 97–173), and it became the dominant view for a generation, with the fresco dated around 1350. A rival current, led by [Roberto Longhi](/scholars.html#longhi-roberto), preferred instead an anonymous Bolognese master working around 1360; the debate stalled between these two positions.

The decisive reorientation came with [Luciano Bellosi](/scholars.html#bellosi-luciano)'s book *Buffalmacco e il Trionfo della Morte* (Turin: Einaudi, 1974). On the basis of stylistic comparison, a fresh reading of Ghiberti, and — importantly — a close analysis of the fashion and costume worn by the figures in the fresco, together with a Pisan document of 1336, Bellosi argued that the entire first campaign (with the exception of Traini's Crucifixion) was the work of Buonamico Buffalmacco, the prankster-painter celebrated in the tales of Boccaccio and [Sacchetti](/scholars.html#sacchetti-franco) but until then regarded as a merely literary figure without identified works, and that it was painted around 1336–1341, roughly a decade before the plague. This attribution, subsequently endorsed by the specialist [Antonino Caleca](/scholars.html#caleca-antonino) and adopted by the Opera della Primaziale Pisana, is today the standard view; the dating range 1336–1341 is now generally cited, and Traini's securely attributed contribution is confined to the earlier Crucifixion. It should be recorded honestly, however, that the Buffalmacco attribution remains majority rather than unanimous — some reference works still favour Traini — and that a number of scholars regard the pre-plague evidence as suggestive rather than conclusive.

### Inscriptions and the Epigraphic Collection

Beyond the pictorial cycles, the Camposanto is a great repository of epigraphy. Its walls carry Roman inscriptions alongside the funerary and commemorative texts of medieval and later Pisans; the reused sarcophagi bear their own ancient carved inscriptions; and the whole ensemble functioned, from the antiquarian revival onward, as a corpus for the study of classical and early Christian inscriptions. This antiquarian dimension reinforced the cemetery's civic-Roman self-presentation and made it, long before the modern museum, a site where inscriptions were collected, read and imitated.

## The Fire of 1944 and Its Aftermath

### 27 July 1944

The catastrophe was not a freak accident but the predictable outcome of six weeks in which the Piazza del Duomo lay inside a contested front line, and the military situation deserves to be stated before the damage is described. Pisa had already been gutted from the air: the raid of 31 August 1943, in which some hundred and fifty B-17 and B-24 bombers dropped upwards of four hundred tons of ordnance on the marshalling yard and the quarters around it, killed at least 952 people and emptied the city of most of its population. By the last week of July 1944 the Fifth Army had closed up to the southern bank of the Arno — American units reached the river mouth and the southern quarters of the city on 23 July — while German forces held the northern bank, on which the Piazza stands, as part of the delaying positions covering the withdrawal towards the Gothic Line.

The Arno, canalised between masonry embankments, made an ideal anti-tank obstacle, and the two armies faced one another across it until the beginning of September. The few thousand civilians remaining were concentrated in the quarter between the Cathedral and the Porta a Lucca, around the Spedale di Santa Chiara and the archbishop's palace, where Archbishop Gabriele Vettori (in office 1933–1953) organised what assistance could be improvised, and the Cathedral itself was opened to refugees. The monuments, in short, were not at a distance from the fighting; they were in it. The campanile was suspected of serving as a German observation post, and in August 1944 Sergeant Leon Weckstein of the U.S. 91st Infantry Division spent the better part of a day watching it through a telescope with a radio at hand and orders to bring the divisional artillery down on it if he saw movement in the loggias; he saw none, was withdrawn under fire, and never called the mission — an episode recorded in his memoir *Through My Eyes: 91st Infantry Division in the Italian Campaign*, and worth setting beside the fate of the Camposanto as a measure of how narrow the margin was. Nor did the shelling stop with the liberation of the city on 2 September: Deane Keller, the American Monuments officer who took charge of the Piazza, recorded German rounds still falling on it on 23 September 1944.

The single event that most decisively shaped the modern Camposanto was its near-destruction by fire on 27 July 1944. During intense fighting as Allied forces advanced from the south against German troops holding the line of the Arno, an artillery shell — described in the sources as an incendiary round of Allied artillery — struck the roof of the cemetery and set alight its wooden framework. Because the city was without a functioning water supply, and the cisterns were under military control, the fire could not be extinguished; it burned for some three days. The wooden roof collapsed, and the lead sheeting of the covering, melted by the heat, ran down the walls over the frescoes, which were already in a precarious state. The heat caused the plaster to blister and the pigments to alter, giving the walls a uniform reddish tone; sculptures and sarcophagi were shattered. The remaining exposure of the murals to seasonal rains completed the disaster, destroying almost entirely the largely *a secco* finish of Benozzo Gozzoli's north-wall cycle, whose final layers had been applied dry.

The most immediate written source for the event is not an art-historical one at all but the *giornale dei lavori*, the daily work journal of the Opera della Primaziale Pisana, kept through the war by an employee of the fabric, Bruno Farnesi, whose entries alternate between routine maintenance and the defence of the monuments under bombardment. His note for 27 July is the first chronicle of the fire, written within hours of it, and it fixes what later accounts blur:

> "Alle ore 19.30 circa, in seguito a granate dell'artiglieria anglo-americana il tetto del nostro Bel Camposanto venne incendiato; sono vani i tentativi di circoscrivere il fuoco a causa dei mezzi inadeguati e del continuare del cannoneggiamento – Il celebre Camposanto alle ore 24 era semidistrutto."

At about half past seven in the evening, that is, the roof caught; attempts to contain the fire were made and failed, for want of equipment and because the shelling did not stop; and by midnight — four and a half hours later — the building was half destroyed. The text was published, with a commentary on its language and on the archive that preserves it, by Chiara Franceschini and Fabrizio Franceschini as *La prima cronaca dell'incendio del Camposanto di Pisa (27 luglio 1944)* (Pisa: Pacini, 2018). Its value is not merely chronological. The possessive of "our lovely Camposanto," written by a workman of the Opera in an official register, records the loss as the institution that had maintained the building for seven centuries experienced it; and the admission that the fire could not be circumscribed disposes of the suggestion, occasionally met with, that nothing was attempted. The earliest visual record is of the following morning: the photographs taken on 28 July 1944 by Gustavo Cenni, which show the galleries still smoking.

The origin of the round requires a distinction that English-language accounts frequently lose. The Italian documentary tradition is consistent and specific: Farnesi's journal, the reports of the superintendency, Elena Franchi's archival study *Arte in assetto di guerra. Protezione e distruzione del patrimonio artistico a Pisa durante la seconda guerra mondiale* (Pisa: ETS, 2006), and the Zurich project's own account all attribute the fire to shells of the Anglo-American field artillery firing across the Arno into the German-held northern half of the city. English summaries, including the current Wikipedia entry, often render this as "a bomb fragment from an Allied raid," which is inaccurate in a way that matters: there was no air raid on 27 July 1944, and the Camposanto was not bombed.

It was struck by indirect ground fire during a bombardment of a defended sector — harassing and counter-battery fire in which the Piazza, standing a few hundred metres behind the German line, lay within the beaten zone. Whether the round was incendiary by design or simply ignited seasoned fourteenth-century timber is not established by any document known to the present writer; the sources divide between *granata incendiaria* and the neutral *un colpo di artiglieria*, and the point should be left open. No battery, battalion or fire mission has ever been identified. What can be said with confidence is that the destruction was not intentional, that it was nonetheless the consequence of a decision to shell an inhabited monumental quarter, and that the same command structure produced both the shell and, five weeks later, the Monuments officer who spent the following winter repairing what it had done — an irony that Franchi's title, "art in battle order," registers exactly.

The loss is the more painful because so much else had been correctly foreseen. From 1940 the Italian superintendencies had been evacuating movable works to rural deposits judged safer than the cities, the Certosa di Calci serving as the principal refuge for the provinces of Pisa, Livorno, Lucca and Apuania; sculpture that could not travel was shored, sandbagged and — after the autumn of 1942 had shown sandbags and timber to be useless against high explosive — encased in brick armatures, the treatment given to the great pulpits. Mural painting on standing walls could be neither moved nor meaningfully shielded.

The Camposanto's two thousand and more square metres were left, in the end, to the protection of the roof, which was precisely the element that destroyed them: the timber structure burned for three days and nights, the lead sheets of the covering melted and ran down over the painted surfaces, and the falling beams gouged and scarred what the metal had not already ruined. Heat blistered the *intonaco* and altered the pigments to the uniform reddish tone that photographs of 1944–45 record across every wall; sculptures and reused Roman sarcophagi cracked and shattered under the heat and the falling debris; among the murals lost outright was one traditionally assigned to Stefano Fiorentino.

Piero Sanpaolesi (1904–1980), appointed Soprintendente ai Monumenti e alle Gallerie di Pisa in 1943, thus inherited on the morning of 28 July a disaster in which every precaution had worked except the one that mattered, and it is from his emergency campaign of the following months that the whole subsequent history of the frescoes descends. The best documented modern reconstructions of these events are Daniela Lamberini, "L'attività di Piero Sanpaolesi alla Soprintendenza di Pisa nel 1944–'46," *Bollettino storico pisano* 75 (2006), pp. 129–174; Cathleen Hoeniger, "The Camposanto of Pisa in the Wake of World War Two: Loss and Discovery," in Holly Flora and Sarah Wilkins, eds., *Art and Experience in Trecento Italy* (Turnhout: Brepols, 2018), pp. 313–328; and the same author's *The Fate of Early Italian Art during World War II: Protection, Rescue, Restoration* (Turnhout: Brepols, 2024), which devotes a full chapter to the Camposanto.

### Emergency Detachment and the Discovery of the Sinopie

The response was among the founding episodes of modern conservation. Emergency protection and detachment of the painted surfaces was begun at once, under the direction of the superintendent responsible for the monument and with a large team of restorers; when American troops entered Pisa in early September, the Monuments unit of the U.S. Army (the MFAA) rapidly constructed a provisional roof and helped assemble Italian specialists to gather the hundreds of fresco fragments that littered the gallery floors. The painted surfaces were removed by the *strappo* technique — the tearing-away of the thin film of pigment by means of strong adhesives applied over cloth — and this operation, beginning with the more truly *buon fresco* Buffalmacco scenes, revealed beneath them the *sinopie*: the preparatory underdrawings executed in red earth (terra di Sinope) on the first coat of plaster. These *sinopie*, themselves subsequently detached, constitute the largest existing corpus of fourteenth- and fifteenth-century monumental drawing, and they preserve invaluable evidence of workshop practice — showing, for instance, that the painter of the first campaign worked without cartoons, laying in a rapid drawing directly in red earth and using direct incision for architecture and haloes.

### The Museo delle Sinopie and the Long Restoration

The recovered *sinopie*, provisionally displayed within the Camposanto and behind its north flank from 1952, were eventually installed in the Museo delle Sinopie, which opened in 1979 in the great medieval brick building on the south side of the Piazza — the former Spedale Nuovo (later Spedale della Misericordia and of Santa Chiara), itself designed by Giovanni di Simone, the architect of the first Camposanto. The museum, whose installation employed a modular system of metal structures on several levels, presents the *sinopie* together with the important series of aquatint engravings made in the early nineteenth century by the conservator then in charge of the Camposanto, [Carlo Lasinio](/scholars.html#lasinio-carlo), whose prints record the appearance of the frescoes before their twentieth-century ruin and are now a primary document for their reconstruction.

The restoration of the detached frescoes themselves proved to be, in the words of one Italian account, an "infinite restoration." The choices of the immediate post-war decades created new problems: the frescoes were mounted on panels of asbestos-cement (eternit) with calcium caseinate as adhesive — a decision opposed at the time by the pre-eminent theorist of Italian restoration but favoured by the superintendent — and this support later caused mould, loss of adhesion and sulphation. From the 1970s a second campaign became necessary, transferring the paintings by *strappo* from the asbestos panels onto modern honeycomb supports (aerolam).

From the 1990s the long-cherished project of returning the paintings to their original walls became concrete: under the direction of the art historian who led the final phase, and with the conservators of the Opera della Primaziale Pisana, the murals were progressively reinstalled in the galleries. Individual scenes of the Buffalmacco cycle — the Thebaid, the Hell, the Last Judgement — were returned in turn, and the reinstallation of the Triumph of Death, reported as the conclusion of the campaign, was completed in 2018; the San Ranieri cycle's restoration was likewise completed in May 2018. By the later stages a large majority of the surviving painting — on the order of eighty per cent — had been returned to the walls. The whole episode remains a reference case in the conservation debate over the *strappo* of frescoes, the reversibility of supports, and the ethics and practicalities of re-adhering detached paintings to their original architecture.

## Iconographic and Cultural Significance

### The Triumph of Death, the Plague, and the Dating Debate

The Triumph of Death is not merely a masterpiece of Trecento painting but a document at the centre of one of art history's great methodological debates — the question of whether it is a response to the Black Death of 1348. The connection was made canonical by Millard Meiss in his influential book *Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death: The Arts, Religion, and Society in the Mid-Fourteenth Century* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1951). Meiss argued that Tuscan society read the calamities of the mid-fourteenth century as divine punishment, and that painting after 1348 accordingly retreated from the human-centred naturalism of Giotto and the Lorenzetti toward a more hieratic, frontal, spiritually severe manner reviving the abstraction of the previous century. The Camposanto Triumph of Death — which Meiss, following his 1933 article, dated to around 1350 and attributed to Traini — served, together with Orcagna's Strozzi Altarpiece in Santa Maria Novella, as one of the two keystone examples of this thesis, which became the paradigm for a generation of scholarship.

Bellosi's redating of the fresco to around 1336, on the evidence of costume and a Pisan document, struck directly at this construction. If the Triumph of Death predates the plague by a decade, it cannot be a reaction to the Black Death; it belongs rather to the wider medieval tradition of the *memento mori* and to Dominican penitential preaching, and reflects, if any specific context, the famines and anxieties of the 1330s and early 1340s rather than the pestilence of 1348. The pre-plague dating is now the general consensus — supported independently by other scholars and by the sequence of execution observed during the post-war detachment — though, as noted above, some continue to regard the evidence as slender and the attribution as not wholly secure. The debate is a salutary lesson in the fragility of the link between images and events: a single redating dissolved one of the most famous causal arguments in the history of art. The plague connection is therefore presented here as a historiographical episode — a thesis raised by Meiss and substantially undone by Bellosi — rather than as an established meaning of the work.

### Nineteenth-Century Reception and Liszt's *Totentanz*

The Camposanto frescoes, and the Triumph of Death above all, exercised a powerful hold on the nineteenth-century imagination, in an age fascinated by everything medieval and macabre. Lasinio's engravings disseminated the imagery across Europe and are often credited with influencing the Pre-Raphaelite and Nazarene circles. The best-known instance of this reception is musical: Franz Liszt is reported to have visited the Camposanto in 1838, in the company of the Countess Marie d'Agoult, and to have been profoundly struck by the Triumph of Death, an encounter that his principal biographer identified as the inspiration for his *Totentanz* for piano and orchestra — a set of variations on the plainchant *Dies irae*, sketched from the late 1830s, completed in a first version around 1849, revised in 1853 and 1859, and first performed in 1865. The claim requires a measure of caution, and is flagged accordingly: several commentators note that Hans Holbein the Younger's woodcut series of the Dance of Death is at least as plausible a source, and that the two accounts of the work's inspiration compete rather than coincide. What can be affirmed is the reception itself — that the fresco was seen, admired and remembered by one of the century's most influential musicians — even if the precise causal weight of the encounter cannot be established.

## The Complex as a Whole

The four monuments of the Piazza are best understood not as isolated masterpieces but as a single, coordinated ensemble, unified by material (white and grey marble), by the shared vocabulary of blind arcading and superimposed loggias — the "Pisan Romanesque" — and by their axial and proportional relationships. The Baptistery stands on the axis of the Cathedral façade and shares its width; the Camposanto's two portals answer the entrances of Cathedral and Baptistery; the whole reads as an orchestrated composition in space, which is precisely the quality that later observers, from D'Annunzio to UNESCO, singled out as miraculous. The continuity of the ensemble was guaranteed institutionally by the Opera — originally distinct fabric-institutions (the Opera of Santa Maria for the Cathedral and campanile, that of San Giovanni for the Baptistery) that were in time absorbed into the single Opera della Primaziale Pisana, the lay body that has administered, financed and maintained the monuments for centuries and that directed the great post-war restorations.

In December 1987, at the eleventh session of the World Heritage Committee, the Piazza del Duomo of Pisa was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the sixth Italian cultural site to be so recognised (reference 395; the Camposanto is separately referenced as 395bis, with a 2007 extension). The inscription rests on multiple cultural criteria.

Under criterion (i), the Piazza is judged artistically unique for its spatial design and for containing "four absolute architectural masterpieces": the Cathedral, the Baptistery, the campanile and the Camposanto, together with such treasures as the pulpits and the Camposanto frescoes.

Under criterion (ii), the monuments are held to have profoundly influenced the development of architecture and monumental art — first during Pisa's prosperity up to 1284, when the polychrome, loggia-rich "Pisan style" was established and spread through Tuscany and the Pisan maritime territory.

Under criterion (iv), the group is cited as an outstanding example of a complex of medieval Christian religious buildings constructed for distinct liturgical functions.

Under criterion (vi), the site is associated with the work of Galileo, who is said to have observed the swinging of a lamp in the Cathedral and to have conducted experiments connected with the belfry.

## Conclusion: The State of the Question

The Baptistery of San Giovanni and the Camposanto Monumentale together preserve a remarkably complete record of Pisan artistic ambition from the height of the maritime republic to the long aftermath of its decline. The Baptistery embodies the transition from Romanesque to Gothic across two centuries of interrupted building, and it houses, in Nicola Pisano's pulpit of 1260, one of the securely dated, signed masterpieces from which the history of modern Italian sculpture is conventionally begun. The Camposanto embodies a distinctively Pisan conception of the cemetery as civic pantheon, museum of the antique, and theatre of the soul's fate, and it preserves — in ruined but partially recovered form, and uniquely doubled by its *sinopie* — one of the greatest fresco enterprises of the later Middle Ages.

Yet much remains uncertain or contested, and honest scholarship insists on saying so. The foundation legend of the holy earth is demonstrably later than the documented foundation of 1277, but the myth itself was constitutive of the building's meaning and its iconography. The attribution of the great south-wall frescoes to Buffalmacco, and their dating to around 1336, represent the prevailing consensus but not unanimity, and the collapse of Meiss's plague thesis in the wake of Bellosi's redating stands as a caution against too-confident readings of images as responses to events. The building history of both monuments — the precise phases of the Baptistery's dome, the exact sequence of the Camposanto's galleries — continues to be refined by documentary research. And the conservation of the frescoes, now largely returned to the walls, remains an open and instructive case in the ethics of restoration. The Piazza del Duomo, in short, is not a finished object but a living field of inquiry, in which the "miracles" of D'Annunzio's phrase are matched by an equally compelling set of unresolved historical questions.

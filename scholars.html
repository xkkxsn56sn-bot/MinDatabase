A B D G L S T V

A

Ansaldi, Giulio Romano

d. 1981

Author of the foundational twentieth-century monograph on the Galliano frescoes, published in 1949 under the title Gli affreschi della Basilica di San Vincenzo a Galliano: I restauri e l'organismo architettonico della basilica, co-authored with the architect Ambrogio Annoni and issued in the context of the restoration campaigns that were then being conducted on the building. The volume addressed, for the first time in a systematic monographic format, both the pictorial cycle itself — its iconographic content, its attributional questions, and its relationship to the broader landscape of Ottonian and Lombard painting — and the architectural fabric of the basilica as documented through the physical evidence of the restoration. Ansaldi's contribution was not limited to stylistic analysis: crucially, he was the first scholar to read and correctly interpret the Ambrosian inscription in the nave that identifies the father of Samson by name — the word MANVE (Manoah, Samson's father as named in the Book of Judges) — painted twice among the figures of the nave cycle, thereby establishing for the first time the precise iconographic source of the upper register of the right nave wall as the narrative of Samson from the Book of Judges. This philological precision is characteristic of Ansaldi's method, which combined close reading of the painted inscriptions with careful cross-referencing of biblical and hagiographic sources. He also provided the first serious discussion of Ariberto's role as not merely the financial patron but the active intellectual commissioner and program-deviser of both the frescoes and the architectural works at the basilica — a thesis subsequently developed and refined by Lampugnani. While the broader art-historical framework within which Ansaldi situated the Galliano frescoes has been substantially revised by later scholarship — particularly de Francovich's more detailed stylistic analysis of the Ottonian connections and Salvini's chronological clarifications — his monograph remains the essential documentary and iconographic foundation for all subsequent study of the cycle, and its meticulous attention to the physical condition and restoration history of the paintings constitutes an irreplaceable primary record.

B

Bertelli, Carlo

b. Florence, 1935

Italian art historian and former Soprintendente of the Pinacoteca di Brera in Milan. One of the foremost specialists in early medieval and pre-Romanesque painting in northern Italy, Bertelli produced fundamental studies of the frescoes of Castelseprio (Santa Maria Foris Portas) and the broader tradition of Lombard monumental painting in the ninth and tenth centuries. His work on the Ambrosian pictorial tradition and its articulation within the cultural networks of Carolingian and Ottonian patronage constitutes an essential methodological framework for understanding artists such as the Maestro di Galliano. Bertelli also held important roles in major restoration campaigns and contributed to several canonical survey volumes on Italian medieval art.

Boskovits, Miklós

Budapest, 1935 – Florence, 2011

Hungarian-Italian art historian and one of the towering figures of twentieth-century research on early Italian panel painting. Educated in Budapest and subsequently based in Florence, where he worked at the Kunsthistorisches Institut, Boskovits devoted his career to the systematic reconstruction of anonymous artistic personalities and the elucidation of attribution problems in Duecento and Trecento painting. His 1988 identification of the Florentine painter Grifo di Tancredi, achieved by connecting a fragmentary workshop inscription to a group of works previously attributed to the anonymous Master of San Gaggio, stands as a model of connoisseurial method. He was equally authoritative on Cimabue — confirming key attributions including a small Maestà discovered in a French farmhouse — and contributed major entries to the corpus of works associated with Coppo di Marcovaldo, the Maestro della Maddalena, and the Maestro del Dossale di Yale. His multi-volume Italian Paintings before 1400 for the National Gallery of Art in Washington remains a fundamental reference tool.

D

De Francovich, Géza

Gorizia, 1902 – Rome, 1996

Géza de Francovich was one of the most wide-ranging and methodologically rigorous Italian art historians of the twentieth century, whose scholarly interests spanned from Romanesque sculpture to the art of the ancient Near East.

De Francovich was born on August 28, 1902, in Gorizia — then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire — into an aristocratic family of Hungarian origin. He attended the German-language section of the Staatsgymnasium in Gorizia before the First World War disrupted his youth. After the conflict, he moved to Florence, where he completed his university degree in 1925. His formation thus combined Central European cultural sensibility with a deep immersion in the Italian art historical tradition, an unusual and productive duality that would shape his entire career.

Following his graduation, de Francovich took up a post as inspector at the Soprintendenza of Perugia from 1926 to 1928. He was subsequently recruited by the philosopher Giovanni Gentile to serve as a contributor to the Enciclopedia Italiana, where he worked alongside some of the leading art historians of the time — Pietro Toesca, Lionello Venturi, and Mario Salmi — whose assistant he also became at the University of Rome. He obtained his libera docenza (the Italian qualification for independent university teaching) from 1937, and from 1956 to 1972 he held the chair of medieval art history at the Università degli Studi di Roma "La Sapienza".

De Francovich's research was characterized by a rigorous historical method that placed artistic objects firmly within their broader ideological and cultural contexts. His thematic range was exceptional: he moved from Lombard Carolingian and Ottonian art to Romanesque wood sculpture, from Byzantine ivory carving to Achaemenid and Sasanian art, always grounding his analysis in direct examination of the monuments themselves. His 1937 article "A Romanesque School of Wood Carvers in Central Italy," published in the Art Bulletin, established an early international reputation. His most celebrated work remains the monumental two-volume Benedetto Antelami, architetto e scultore e l'arte del suo tempo (1952), a landmark study of the great Romanesque sculptor, sponsored by Bernard Berenson.

De Francovich traveled extensively throughout his career, conducting fieldwork not only across Europe but throughout the former Byzantine Empire and the Middle East. In 1966, he coordinated a team of Italian scholars from La Sapienza in a series of study trips to the historic regions of Armenia, resulting in extensive photographic documentation of medieval churches and monasteries. For the Italian National Research Council (CNR), he directed the multi-volume projects Studi di architettura medievale armena (4 vols., 1971–73) and Studi sulla pittura medievale campana (3 vols., 1974–81). His intellectual journey concluded with Santuari e tombe rupestri dell'antica Frigia (1990), a study of rock-cut sanctuaries in ancient Phrygia (modern Turkey), published when he was nearly ninety years old. He died in Rome on November 24, 1996.

G

Garrison, Edward B.

United States, 1908 – 1983

American art historian and specialist in Italian Romanesque panel painting, author of the foundational four-volume catalogue Italian Romanesque Panel Painting: An Illustrated Index (Florence, 1949–1962), which systematically documented early Italian painted panels and established the scholarly framework within which anonymous masters such as Maestro Guglielmo could be assessed. Garrison's patient archival and connoisseurial work identified stylistic relationships between objects previously treated in isolation, and his 1953 identification of connections between the Sarzana Cross and the Passionary P+ manuscript initiated the scholarly programme of reconstructing Guglielmo's broader artistic activity. His indices remain an indispensable reference for any researcher working on Italian Romanesque and early Duecento painting.

Ghiberti, Lorenzo

Florence, 1378 – Florence, 1455

Florentine sculptor, goldsmith, and early art chronicler, celebrated above all for the two sets of bronze doors for the Baptistery of Florence — the second of which Michelangelo later called the "Gates of Paradise." Alongside his activity as one of the foremost artists of the early Renaissance, Ghiberti composed the Commentarii (c. 1447–1455), a three-part text that stands as the earliest sustained attempt at a history of Italian art from ancient times to Ghiberti's own day. The Commentarii preserve important biographical and critical notices on Trecento masters — including Giotto, Cimabue, and the Berlinghieri family — that corroborate, amplify, or occasionally contradict the later accounts compiled by Vasari, and they remain a primary source for understanding how the art of the Duecento and Trecento was perceived by an informed contemporary witness working one generation after the style's apogee.

L

Lampugnani, Elena

Elena Lampugnani is a contemporary Italian art historian specializing in early medieval Lombard painting and ecclesiastical patronage, whose work on the Galliano frescoes has made the single most important iconographic discovery of recent decades regarding this cycle. Her principal contribution to the study of the Maestro di Galliano is the essay La "commendatio animae" di Ariberto nelle pitture absidali di San Vincenzo a Galliano (1007), published in 2006 in the collective volume Pittura murale del Medioevo lombardo: Ricerche iconografiche (secoli XI–XIII), edited by Paolo Piva (Jaca Book, 2006), a volume that brought together the most recent Italian scholarship on the major mural painting cycles of the provinces of Como, Lecco, and Bergamo between the year 1000 and the thirteenth century. In this essay, Lampugnani proposed for the first time a comprehensive reading of the entire apse program at Galliano as a commendatio animae — that is, a liturgical prayer of commendation of the soul to God, spoken at the moment of death and translated here into a painted visual program — a theological framework that unifies all the apparently disparate iconographic elements of the apse into a single coherent argument about intercession, death, and resurrection. The commendatio animae interpretation, now widely accepted in the scholarly literature, explains the selection of figures — Elijah and Enoch (who avoided death), the prophets prostrating before the Theophany, the martyred Vincent (who gained eternal life through his passio), the intercessor Adeodato, and finally the praying donor Ariberto — as the successive stages of a liturgical and eschatological narrative culminating in Ariberto's own petition for divine mercy at the moment of his anticipated death. Lampugnani's essay thus belongs to the tradition of iconographic theology in medieval art studies — a method that decodes painted programs not merely as aesthetic ensembles but as precise translations of theological and liturgical texts into visual form — and it represents a significant methodological advance over both Ansaldi's documentary approach and de Francovich's primarily stylistic analysis. She also authored the essay Ariberto, custos a Galliano e arcivescovo a Milano: la personalità storica di un committente artistico attraverso i suoi "ritratti" (Monduzzi Editoriale), in which she analysed the four known portraits of Ariberto — beginning with the Galliano apse fresco and extending through his later representations in manuscript illuminations and goldsmiths' works — as a coherent self-representational strategy through which Ariberto constructed and projected his public identity as a patron of exceptional cultural ambition. Her work has been incorporated and cited by subsequent scholars — including the authors of the entries in the Treccani Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale and of the JSTOR article on the nave paintings — confirming her position as one of the leading current authorities on the patronage and iconographic programs of the early eleventh-century Ambrosian world.

Longhi, Roberto

Alba, 1890 – Florence, 1970

Perhaps the most influential Italian art historian of the twentieth century, Roberto Longhi transformed the study of Italian painting across virtually every period through a combination of incisive connoisseurship, vivid critical prose, and an extraordinary gift for visual analysis. After formative studies in Turin and Rome, he held the chair of art history at the University of Bologna and subsequently at Florence, where his teaching and publications shaped generations of scholars. His contributions to medieval art history include foundational studies of Cimabue, the Maestro Consolo, and the Roman school of the late Duecento, as well as path-breaking assessments of the role of anonymous masters in the formation of the Florentine and Sienese traditions. Longhi also coined or refined several of the critical terms — including the attribution of anonymous works to reconstructed artistic personalities — that became standard tools of the scholarly literature. He founded the journal Paragone in 1950, which under his editorship became the most prestigious Italian venue for art historical studies, and the Fondazione Roberto Longhi in Florence, established in his name, remains a leading centre for doctoral training in the history of Italian art.

S

Salvini, Roberto

Modena, 1912 – Florence, 1985

Roberto Salvini was one of the most important Italian art historians of the twentieth century, making fundamental contributions to the study of medieval art, Romanesque sculpture, and Flemish painting. A student of the medievalist Mario Salmi, Salvini received his education in Florence, Munich, and Berlin, acquiring a European methodological perspective that would characterize his entire body of work. In 1937, he joined the Fine Arts administration, serving as superintendent of galleries in various locations — Trento, Palermo, and Modena — during the years of World War II. His institutional career culminated in his directorship of the Uffizi Gallery in Florence, where he promoted significant museographic innovations.

In 1956, Salvini began his university career as a professor at the University of Trieste, before moving to the University of Florence, where he taught for many years. He was actively engaged in the methodology of teaching art history, a topic he had already addressed in his theoretical essay La pura visibilità (1947), which reflected the influence of the German formalist tradition on his education. In 1983, he was elected a National Member of the Accademia dei Lincei, the highest academic honour in Italy. He also served as President of the Art History Section of the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno in Florence and held the position of Secretary General.

His studies ranged from Italian medieval art to the Renaissance, from Flemish art to European Romanesque sculpture. Among his most significant works in medieval studies are Medieval Mosaics in Sicily (1949), Wiligelmo and the Origins of Romanesque Sculpture in Europe (1956) — a pioneering study on the origins of Romanesque sculpture — and The Cloister of Monreale (1962). He also made significant contributions to Byzantine studies, writing seminal volumes such as The Cathedral of Modena (1966) and the volume Medieval Sculpture for the international series History of Western Sculpture.

A recurring theme throughout his work was his interest in the relationship between Italian patrons and Flemish painting, explored both in Flemish Painting (1958) and in his last published work, Florentine Bankers and Flemish Painters (1985), released in the year of his death. Salvini left a profound mark on half a century of national and international art history, and in 2018 and 2022 his legacy was celebrated with conferences and publications dedicated to his scholarly contributions.

T

Toesca, Pietro

Turin, 1877 – Rome, 1962

The preeminent Italian art historian of the medieval period and the founding figure of the systematic study of Lombard painting. After formative studies in Turin under Adolfo Venturi, Toesca held the chair of history of medieval and modern art successively at Turin and at the Università degli Studi di Roma, where he taught from 1923 until his retirement. His La pittura e la miniatura nella Lombardia dai più antichi monumenti alla metà del Quattrocento (Milan, 1912) constitutes the first comprehensive scholarly treatment of Lombard painting from late antiquity through the early Renaissance, and it established the Maestro di Galliano as a major artistic personality and defined the terms of all subsequent discussion of the Galliano frescoes. His two-volume Storia dell'arte italiana — comprising Il Medioevo (Turin, 1927) and Il Trecento (Turin, 1951) — established the canon of Italian medieval art history for much of the twentieth century and remains an indispensable reference. Toesca is cited across virtually the entire database as the ultimate scholarly authority on the pre-Duecento period.

V

Van Marle, Raimond

The Hague, 1888 – Perugia, 1936

Dutch art historian who spent much of his career in Italy and produced the monumental eighteen-volume The Development of the Italian Schools of Painting (The Hague, 1923–1938), the most encyclopaedic survey of Italian painting from its origins to the sixteenth century ever attempted by a single scholar. Though inevitably uneven in its attributions and assessments — and substantially revised by subsequent scholarship — the work remains an invaluable documentary resource for tracing the state of knowledge at the time of writing and for its systematic cataloguing of works that have since been lost or dispersed. Van Marle's treatment of the Duecento and Trecento, including his extended notices on the Berlinghieri family, Cimabue, and the Roman school, provided the bibliographic infrastructure within which mid-twentieth-century revision by scholars such as Toesca, Longhi, and Boskovits could operate. He died in Perugia before the final volumes of his encyclopaedia were complete.

Vasari, Giorgio

Arezzo, 1511 – Florence, 1574

Florentine architect, painter, and the most influential art historian of the early modern period, whose Le vite de' più eccellenti pittori, scultori e architettori (Florence, 1550; revised and enlarged edition, 1568) established the canonical narrative of Italian art history from Cimabue to Michelangelo. Articulating a teleological model in which art was understood to pass through cycles of birth, growth, decay, and rebirth, Vasari positioned the medieval period as an era of decline from ancient Roman standards, characterised by what he called the maniera greca — the Byzantine-influenced style he described as "clumsy" and backward — from which Italian painting was rescued by Cimabue and his pupil Giotto. This framework, while enormously productive in organizing the historical material, has been recognized by modern scholarship as distorting in its devaluation of medieval artistic achievement on its own terms; yet the Vite remain an irreplaceable primary source, preserving biographical notices, workshop traditions, and critical perceptions not recoverable from any other source. Vasari is cited throughout this database as an early written witness for works and attributions that must be weighed critically against documentary and material evidence.

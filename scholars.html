Andaloro, Maria

b. 1943

Maria Andaloro is one of the world's leading authorities on medieval and Byzantine art, a student of Cesare Brandi, and a key figure in some of the most ambitious scholarly endeavors in 20th- and 21st-century Italian medieval studies, ranging from research on the Norman mosaics of Palermo to the vast corpus of medieval Roman painting.

Andaloro graduated in 1967 from the University of Rome with a degree in Byzantine Art History, under the guidance of Cesare Brandi—the great theorist of restoration and Crocean aesthetics, founder of the Central Institute of Restoration. That choice was, in a way, unconventional: Byzantine art history was then considered by many Italian academics to be a "dead" discipline, far removed from the classical and humanistic tradition upon which the dominant art historiography was founded. Andaloro absorbed from Brandi not only critical sensibility and theoretical refinement, but above all the ability to combine art history, image theory, and restoration into a comprehensive approach to the figurative heritage.

After years of research and teaching, Andaloro became a full professor of Byzantine Art History and Medieval Art History at the University of Tuscia in Viterbo, where she remained until her retirement in November 2013. From 2003 to 2009 she served as Dean of the Faculty of Cultural Heritage Conservation, and since 2015 she has been Professor Emerita at the same university. Over the course of her career, she coordinated six successive cycles of PRIN (Research Programs of National Interest) projects funded by the Ministry of Education, all dedicated to topics in Byzantine art and iconography. Since 2015, she has also directed the University of Tuscia's scientific mission in Cappadocia, a region of central Anatolia home to one of the world's most extraordinary collections of early Christian and medieval rock art, featuring churches carved into tuff rock that preserve pictorial cycles dating from the 6th to the 16th centuries.

Andaloro's most monumental work is her direction, together with her Swiss colleague Serena Romano (University of Lausanne), of the editorial project Medieval Painting in Rome. Corpus and Atlas (312–1431), published by Jaca Book in nine volumes: six dedicated to the Corpus (edited by Andaloro and Romano) and three to the Atlas (edited solely by Andaloro). This work, begun in 2003 and still in progress, constitutes the most systematic survey ever undertaken of mural and mosaic painting produced in Rome and Lazio from the Constantinian era through the late Middle Ages, and represents an indispensable reference tool for any scholar of late antique and medieval art. For this project, Andaloro and Romano received the Borghese Prize in 2013, one of the most prestigious Italian awards in the field of art history. Andaloro also served as editor of one of the volumes of Guglielmo Matthiae's Pittura romana del Medioevo, contributing to the scholarly update of that work in its second edition of the 1980s.

A second key focus of Andaloro's research concerns the mosaics of Norman Palermo—the Palatine Chapel, Monreale Cathedral, and the Martorana—which she interprets as the most fruitful meeting point between the Greek-Byzantine mosaic tradition and the Latin-Western figurative culture in the medieval Mediterranean. Her studies, published in Storia di Palermo edited by Rosario La Duca and in numerous scholarly essays, have redefined the chronology and patronage of those cycles, highlighting the cultural complexity of Roger's court and the diversity of artisans involved. At the same time, Andaloro has devoted extensive work to the phenomenon of icons in Rome—in particular to the panel paintings preserved in Roman basilicas—analyzing them as a living instrument of devotion and religious communication far beyond their purely aesthetic function.

A theoretical thread runs through Andaloro's entire scholarly output: her interest in the phenomenon of the icon as a conceptual category, not merely as a physical object. In her view, the icon is not a passive devotional image but an instrument that "attempts to make the invisible visible," according to the formulation she herself developed. This theoretical perspective, drawing on John of Damascus's theology of the image and Brandi's philosophy of restoration, enabled her to analyze both the great apse mosaic cycles of Rome and the humblest devotional panels within a unified interpretive framework, situating medieval figurative production at the crossroads of theology, liturgy, and the history of mentalities. The volume Art and Iconography in Rome: From Late Antiquity to the End of the Middle Ages (2001), edited with Serena Romano, remains the most accessible synthesis of this approach.

Agnello, Fabrizio

active 21st century

Fabrizio Agnello is an architect and associate professor in the Department of Architecture at the University of Palermo, where he teaches Drawing and Graphic Documentation and Architectural Drawing and Surveying Workshop. He is a scholar whose work spans the history of medieval architecture, descriptive geometry, and digital methodologies applied to cultural heritage.

Trained as an architect, Agnello holds a Ph.D. and is currently an associate professor of Drawing at the University of Palermo. He works within the Sicilian academic system, conducting research that intertwines the history of medieval architecture with surveying and graphic representation techniques, both traditional and digital.

Agnello's most significant contribution to medieval studies concerns the documentation and geometric analysis of the ceiling of the central nave of the Cappella Palatina in Palermo, the very monument at the center of Lev Kapitaikin's research. Agnello has developed highly detailed technical surveys of the ceiling's wooden muqarnas structures, producing geometric reconstructions and analyses of the paintings that constitute a fundamental tool for understanding this masterpiece of twelfth-century Norman and Islamic art. His approach combines metric and photographic surveying with the analysis of the proportional relationships of forms, offering an indispensable technical framework for anyone approaching the study of this monument.

In 2023, Agnello published the volume The Photographic Memory of Architecture: Perspective Restorations and Reconstructions with FrancoAngeli. The book explores how historical photographs can be used to reconstruct the three-dimensional form of buildings through perspective restoration, a method that allows, starting from a single image, the recovery of spatial information that would otherwise be lost.

This is a highly useful methodological tool for the history of medieval architecture, where nineteenth- or twentieth-century photographic documents often represent the only evidence of construction phases that were later altered or destroyed.

A significant part of his most recent research is dedicated to digital methods for the enhancement and dissemination of architectural heritage. Agnello thus fits into the contemporary trend of digital heritage, developing three-dimensional models and virtual representations that make the geometric complexities of Sicilian medieval architecture accessible to a wide audience. His Google Scholar profile lists approximately 291 citations, attesting to a well-established scientific impact in the field of surveying and virtual reconstructions of historical architecture.

Ansaldi, Giulio Romano

d. 1981

Author of the foundational twentieth-century monograph on the Galliano frescoes, published in 1949 under the title Gli affreschi della Basilica di San Vincenzo a Galliano: I restauri e l'organismo architettonico della basilica, co-authored with the architect Ambrogio Annoni and issued in the context of the restoration campaigns that were then being conducted on the building. The volume addressed, for the first time in a systematic monographic format, both the pictorial cycle itself — its iconographic content, its attributional questions, and its relationship to the broader landscape of Ottonian and Lombard painting — and the architectural fabric of the basilica as documented through the physical evidence of the restoration. Ansaldi's contribution was not limited to stylistic analysis: crucially, he was the first scholar to read and correctly interpret the Ambrosian inscription in the nave that identifies the father of Samson by name — the word MANVE (Manoah, Samson's father as named in the Book of Judges) — painted twice among the figures of the nave cycle, thereby establishing for the first time the precise iconographic source of the upper register of the right nave wall as the narrative of Samson from the Book of Judges. This philological precision is characteristic of Ansaldi's method, which combined close reading of the painted inscriptions with careful cross-referencing of biblical and hagiographic sources. He also provided the first serious discussion of Ariberto's role as not merely the financial patron but the active intellectual commissioner and program-deviser of both the frescoes and the architectural works at the basilica — a thesis subsequently developed and refined by Lampugnani. While the broader art-historical framework within which Ansaldi situated the Galliano frescoes has been substantially revised by later scholarship — particularly de Francovich's more detailed stylistic analysis of the Ottonian connections and Salvini's chronological clarifications — his monograph remains the essential documentary and iconographic foundation for all subsequent study of the cycle, and its meticulous attention to the physical condition and restoration history of the paintings constitutes an irreplaceable primary record.

Arbace, Lucia

active late 20th-21st century

Lucia Arbace (also known as Luciana Arbace) is one of Italy's leading art historians specializing in the cultural heritage of Abruzzo and Naples. Her academic profile on Academia.edu describes her as a specialist whose primary research interests include ceramics, Renaissance sculpture, landscape painting, and drawing, with particular attention to Italian historical painting.

Arbace built her career within the Ministry of Cultural Heritage and Activities (now the Ministry of Culture), holding senior management positions of major responsibility. She served as Superintendent for the Historical, Artistic, and Ethno-anthropological Heritage of Abruzzo, overseeing the protection of the region's historical and artistic patrimony. In this capacity, in 2011 she curated the exhibition People of Abruzzo - Social Realism in Painting, publicly stating the goal of spreading awareness of an art form "still unknown to many." She also held roles at the Capodimonte Museum and Royal Park in Naples, where she participated in scientific research and conferences.

The most significant chapter of her career is her directorship of the Polo Museale d'Abruzzo and the Museo Nazionale d'Abruzzo (MuNDA) in L'Aquila, a position she held for nearly eleven years. Her tenure coincided with one of the most difficult periods in the city's modern history: the reconstruction after the devastating earthquake of 6 April 2009.

Arbace guided the museum through this critical phase, coordinating restorations of works initially considered irrecoverable, including paintings by Saturnino Gatti, brought back to public display by 2015, the year of MuNDA's reopening.

In June 2020, Arbace stepped down as director of MuNDA after reaching mandatory retirement age, bidding farewell with the words: "These have been intense but very demanding years. I carry L'Aquila in my heart." Under her leadership, the museum combined high-level restoration with digital tools, including virtual tours that proved essential during COVID-19 closures. A ministerial reform then provided for two separate appointments: one director for MuNDA, moving toward institutional autonomy, and one for the regional museum network.

As an author, Arbace has produced an extensive bibliography spanning medieval painting in Abruzzo and seventeenth-century Naples. Notable publications include The Naples Art Institute and Stefano Brun (1929-1958) and The Walls of L'Aquila, presented at MuNDA, a study that reconstructs centuries of urban history through the city's walls and their defensive, social, and sacred functions. She has also curated exhibitions on seventeenth-century painting in Abruzzo between Rome and Naples, bringing works from major collections to public attention. Her profiles on platforms such as ScholarGPS and Academia.edu attest to her recognition in the Italian art-historical research community.

Ascani, Valerio

Lucca, 1962

Valerio Ascani, born in Lucca in 1962, is an Italian university researcher currently serving at the Department of Civilization and Forms of Knowledge at the University of Pisa, where he teaches Medieval Art History and coordinates the master's degree program in History of the Visual Arts.

His scholarly interests span a thematically coherent and rich range, centered on Italian medieval art from the High to the Late Middle Ages. His research focuses primarily on three areas: monastic architecture and Romanesque sculpture, with particular attention to the workshops of Lombard-Ticino sculptors active in Tuscany during the thirteenth century; liturgical manuscripts and medieval iconography; and medieval religious architecture in central and northern Italy.

One of Ascani's most original contributions concerns the Bigarelli workshop, the family of Ticino sculptors originally from Arogno, active in Pisa, Lucca, and Pistoia during the first half of the thirteenth century, on which he published a seminal study as early as 1991 titled La bottega dei Bigarelli. Ticino Sculptors in Tuscany. He has also conducted in-depth studies of the thirteenth-century artists of Arogno and their contributions to the portals of the cathedrals of Lucca and Trento, analyzing the iconographic choices, technical experimentation, and communicative strategies of these itinerant workshops of Lombard craftsmen.

A second area of great relevance for those studying medieval illuminated manuscripts is represented by his studies on medieval liturgical codices and their decoration. He has written on the relationship between liturgy and iconography in the medieval Gospel book, on the symbolic horizon of the liturgical codex in Christian civilization, and on late-antique mimesis in the figurative arts of early medieval Italy, with an essay dedicated to the theme of the Good Shepherd and to manuscript 490 of the Capitular Library of Lucca.

In the field of architecture, Ascani collaborated with the medievalist Mauro Ronzani on a monograph on the Bell Tower of Pisa Cathedral, the famous Leaning Tower, published in 2011 by Edizioni ETS in Pisa as part of the Mirabilia Pisana series, offering a historical and interpretive reinterpretation of the structure that integrates documentary sources with architectural analysis. He has also studied the Volto Santo of Lucca, the famous medieval wooden crucifix, participating in September 2020 in a study meeting organized by the Museum Complex of the Cathedral of Lucca.

Valerio Ascani is an important point of reference for anyone engaged in the study of Tuscan medieval art from the twelfth to the fourteenth century. His combined focus on sculpture, architecture, iconography, and liturgical manuscripts makes him a particularly valuable resource for research spanning multiple areas of medieval artistic production, from cathedral construction sites to the decoration of manuscripts, from the sculptor's workshop to the illuminator's scriptorium.

Baroni, Costantino Nicola

Milan, 12 August 1905 - Milan, 3 March 1956

Costantino Nicola Baroni was one of the most distinguished Italian art historians, archivists, and museum directors of the twentieth century, whose brief but extraordinarily productive life left an indelible mark on the cultural heritage of Milan. Born in Milan on 12 August 1905 to parents of Vicentine origin, he died in the same city on 3 March 1956, just weeks before the triumphant inauguration of the museum he had spent a decade rebuilding.

Baroni's family background was steeped in the artisan traditions of the Veneto: among his ancestors was Giovanni Baroni, a celebrated ceramist from Bassano active in the early nineteenth century, and a Vicentine tapestry weaver. This hereditary proximity to the decorative arts profoundly shaped the direction of his earliest scholarly inquiries. Around the age of twenty, serious illness forced him to abandon his preferred course of study - architecture at the Politecnico di Milano - and enroll instead in the Faculty of Law, which permitted him to study without the burden of demanding attendance obligations.

He graduated in Law in 1932, but his true intellectual vocation had never wavered. Even before completing his degree, he was a regular and devoted visitor to the artistic collections of the Castello Sforzesco, and upon graduation he began volunteering there immediately. In 1934, two years after his law degree, he obtained the diploma in palaeography, diplomatic studies, and archival doctrine - a qualification that would prove indispensable to his subsequent career of systematic documentary research into Lombard monuments and medieval sources.

Baroni's earliest published scholarship concerned Italian ceramics, a field he entered with both personal and intellectual motivations. As he wrote in his own curriculum: "For ceramics in particular, when I began to study them in earnest, very little was being done in Italy, except at the centre of studies created at Faenza. Foreign historians - Bode, Wallis, Serre, Rackham - knew far more than we did." At just twenty-five, he submitted a study on eighteenth-century Venetian ceramics to the prize competition of the Regia Deputazione di Storia Patria per le Venezie, which was commended and subsequently published in the Archivio Veneto in 1932.

In a 1933 essay in Dedalo, he disputed the conventional attribution of a significant group of embossed ceramics to Venice, reassigning them to Bassano production and backdating them by nearly a century - a revision that won broad scholarly approval and encouraged him to deepen his ceramic studies further. He also discovered the existence of a previously unknown tradition of medieval Milanese ceramics, clarifying the relationships between the various eighteenth-century city manufactories. These early contributions established him as a methodologically rigorous scholar willing to challenge received opinion through documentary verification.

His growing reputation led to a formal appointment by the Comune di Milano in April 1935, when he was officially engaged as cataloguer of the artworks at the Castello Sforzesco. Under the mentorship of Professor Giorgio Nicodemi, Superintendent of the Castle's archives and museums, Baroni undertook the new arrangement and presentation of the palaeontological and archaeological collections, the reorganization of the medieval and modern ceramics, the Art Library, and the Raccolta Vinciana - the specialist collection devoted to Leonardo da Vinci studies. He was elected secretary of the Raccolta Vinciana in 1936, reflecting his growing expertise in Leonardesque documentation.

In 1937 he declined a hard-won post as assistant at the Civici Musei di Venezia, preferring instead to remain in Milan, where the following year he was appointed Conservator of Fine Arts for the Comune di Milano through public competition. This decision to remain in his native city allowed him to deepen and expand his scholarly production and to develop lasting collegial relationships with the greatest art historians of his generation: Mario Salmi, Adolfo Venturi, Pietro Toesca, Roberto Longhi, Giuseppe Fiocco, and others.

The heart of Baroni's art-historical contribution lies in his systematic reassessment of Lombard architecture and sculpture from the Trecento to the Seicento. A decade of methodical archival exploration yielded a series of publications that dismantled many long-standing attributions and chronological assumptions. His communication to the First Lombard Historical Congress at Como in 1937, Il metodo storico e i problemi di valutazione critica dell'architettura lombarda da Bramante al Richini, laid out the programmatic lines of his entire research project: the conviction that traditional art-historical accounts of Lombard Renaissance architecture rested on uncertain documentation and attributions perpetuated by convention rather than evidence.

This argument was expanded into a full monograph, L'architettura lombarda da Bramante a Richini. Questioni di metodo (Milan, 1941), published alongside the first volume - devoted to religious buildings - of his major documentary compilation Documenti per la storia dell'architettura a Milano nel Rinascimento e nel Barocco, issued by Sansoni in Florence. Sadly, only this first of three projected parts was ever published. In 1944 he produced his most ambitious scholarly work, Scultura gotica lombarda, a comprehensive study of Gothic sculpture in Lombardy that brought new attributions and original critical solutions to long-debated problems. He later contributed essays on the same subject to volumes V and VI of the monumental Storia di Milano published by the Treccani Institute (1955).

Baroni's expertise extended to Leonardo studies, a natural complement to his architectural research and his stewardship of the Raccolta Vinciana. His work in this field was recognized when, for the major Leonardo exhibition held in Milan in 1939, he was appointed commissioner for the sections devoted to biographical documents and architecture. His contributions to Vincian scholarship were sufficiently significant to warrant a dedicated bibliography in his obituary published in the Raccolta Vinciana (1960).

When Italy entered the Second World War and the director Nicodemi was absent in Austria, it fell to Baroni - as the responsible public official - to organize and complete the enormous task of dismantling the Museo d'Arte del Castello Sforzesco and the Civica Galleria d'Arte Moderna, and transporting part of the collections to anti-aircraft shelters. This activity was documented in his essay Le operazioni di sgombero e di ricovero delle civiche raccolte d'arte (1941). He simultaneously collaborated with the Soprintendenza ai Monumenti di Milano on provisional measures to safeguard the city's artistic patrimony.

In 1945, as the Liberation of Italy approached, Baroni was one of three members of the Comitato di Liberazione Nazionale per le Arti (alongside Pacchioni and Morassi) who made contact with the Cardinal Archbishop and the Allied Authorities to signal artworks looted to Germany and others subject to war damage. From May 1945, first by the CLNAI and then by the municipal authorities, he was entrusted with the direction of the Belle Arti of the Comune di Milano, charged with organizing the recovery of displaced works of art and pressing for the restoration of monumentally damaged sites.

The most visible phase of Baroni's career was his direction of the Civic Museums of Milan in the postwar decade. He faced the immense challenge of reconstituting the Museo d'Arte Antica at the Castello Sforzesco and the Galleria d'Arte Moderna, both devastated by the Allied bombing of August 1943. His vision for the museums was guided by the most advanced museographical doctrines of the time, and he engaged the celebrated architectural group BBPR to design the new installations, resulting in displays of the highest aesthetic dignity.

His acquisitions policy was equally remarkable. Under his directorship the Castello Sforzesco acquired the Pieta Rondanini - Michelangelo's last great sculpture - securing it permanently for Milan and the nation. He also enriched the collections with twenty-seven Romanesque and Gothic sculptures from the Villa Tittoni park at Desio, the monumental fresco cycle with the Story of Griselda from the castle of Roccabianca, two rare Tusco-Lazial crucifixes of the thirteenth century and an Umbrian one of the fourteenth, paintings by Bergognone, Magnasco, and Del Cairo, groups of Coptic textiles and medieval fabrics, and the first important nucleus of a contemporary art collection featuring works by Modigliani, Morandi, Sironi, Campigli, Manzu, Martini, and Marino Marini.

In 1941 Baroni obtained the libera docenza in medieval and modern art history and in the history of architectural styles, thereafter teaching at the Universita Cattolica and serving as a voluntary assistant at the chair of architectural history at the Politecnico di Milano. His teaching, which lasted from 1943 to 1951, was animated by the ambition to restore art historical instruction from a pallid institutional exercise to a rigorous and critically engaged discipline.

In parallel, from 1945 until his death, he served as art critic for the daily newspaper Il Popolo, where he wrote with directness and unflinching judgement on all aspects of the modern and ancient art worlds. He directed editorial series for the Casa Editrice Martello in Milan: I grandi maestri del disegno, Il fiore della miniatura italiana, and I sommi dell'arte italiana - the last of which is of particular relevance to the study of Italian medieval illuminated manuscripts. He also contributed to the Storia di Milano published by the Treccani Institute.

In 1948, at the Venice Biennale, Baroni was awarded the prize for art criticism, the most important Italian recognition in that field. In January 1955 he won the competitive examination for Vice-Director of the Musei d'Arte, a nomination that formally ratified the position of director he had effectively held since 1945.

His last great achievement - one that had absorbed a decade of his most devoted energy - was the complete reconstruction and reinstallation of the Museo d'Arte Antica at the Castello Sforzesco. He died on the night of 3-4 March 1956, just five weeks before the solemn inauguration of that museum in the presence of the President of the Republic. His final essay, Come risorge il Museo del Castello Sforzesco, was found on his desk, pen beside the last page: he died, it was written at the time, with the vision of the Castello Sforzesco before him and the thought of his museum in his mind. He rests in the Monumental Cemetery of Milan, and the Comune has named one of the principal streets of the Gratosoglio quarter in his honour.

Bellosi, Luciano

Scandicci, 7 July 1936 – Florence, 26 April 2011

Luciano Bellosi was one of the most distinguished Italian art historians of the twentieth century, renowned for his scholarship on medieval and early Renaissance painting, especially the Tuscan tradition from the Duecento to the Quattrocento. His work combined connoisseurial sharpness with rigorous historical method, placing him in the lineage of the major Italian historians of style and attribution.

Born in Scandicci near Florence, Bellosi studied at the University of Florence and graduated in 1963 under Roberto Longhi with a thesis on Lorenzo Monaco. Longhi's teaching shaped Bellosi's method: primacy of direct visual analysis, close formal comparison, and reconstruction of artistic personalities through works themselves before documentary confirmation. After military service (1963-1964), he began the institutional and academic path that would define his career.

From 1969 to 1979 Bellosi worked at the Soprintendenza alle Gallerie di Firenze, gaining sustained firsthand contact with major public collections. In 1979 he moved to the University of Siena, where he taught medieval art history, directed the Department of Archaeology and Art History (1983-1986), and trained generations of scholars until his early retirement in 2002. He often described his method as detective-like: reading paintings with the eye first, then testing hypotheses against texts and archives.

His first major book, Buffalmacco e il Trionfo della Morte (Einaudi, 1974), was a landmark reconstruction and won the Premio Viareggio Opera Prima di Saggistica the same year. He later published La pecora di Giotto (Einaudi, 1985), with influential arguments on Giotto's workshop and attribution, and Cimabue (Federico Motta, 1998), still a standard reference for the painter's oeuvre and historical position. His collected essays, "I vivi parean vivi": scritti di storia dell'arte italiana del Duecento e del Trecento (2006), brought together decades of core research on thirteenth- and fourteenth-century painting.

Bellosi's scholarship also extended beyond Tuscany. He published an influential 1975 Prospettiva study on the Limbourg Brothers and the Tres Riches Heures, and in later years wrote important work on the young Giovanni Bellini in relation to Andrea Mantegna. He contributed regularly to journals including Paragone, Prospettiva, Bollettino d'Arte, The Burlington Magazine, and Revue de l'Art.

He was also a major exhibition curator, responsible for projects such as Lorenzo Ghiberti: Materia e ragionamenti (Florence, 1978), Pittura di luce (Florence, 1990), Una scuola per Piero (Florence, 1992), Francesco di Giorgio e il Rinascimento a Siena (Siena, 1993), and in 2003 both Masaccio e le origini del Rinascimento (San Giovanni Valdarno) and Duccio. Alle origini della pittura senese (Siena), the latter a major international event. Active in scholarly institutions and conferences, including the 1985 international conference on Simone Martini, Bellosi was a member of the Accademia degli Intronati. After his death in 2011, his library of about 8,000 volumes and his archive were donated to the University of Siena, where they remain an enduring resource for research.

Benazzi, Giordana

Ferrara - Perugia, 4 February 2026

Giordana Benazzi was one of Italy's most dedicated art historians in the fields of medieval and Renaissance art, and a central figure in the protection and interpretation of Umbria's cultural heritage. After her death in Perugia on 4 February 2026, she was widely remembered for combining rigorous scholarship with a long and concrete commitment to conservation practice in the region.

Born in Ferrara, she completed her university formation in Florence, earning a Laurea in Filosofia at the Universita degli Studi di Firenze between 1967 and 1971 under Eugenio Garin. She later specialized in art history with Mina Gregori. This dual formation, philosophical and art-historical, shaped the interdisciplinary method that marked her scholarship on medieval and Renaissance works.

Benazzi later settled in Perugia, where she served for approximately thirty years as a funzionario at the Soprintendenza per i Beni Architettonici, il Paesaggio, il Patrimonio Storico, Artistico e Etnoantropologico dell'Umbria. In that role she supervised major restoration campaigns involving the Duomo and Rocca of Spoleto, pictorial cycles and artworks in Gubbio, and the Palazzo Trinci complex in Foligno. Her work after the 1976, 1979, and 1997 earthquakes was repeatedly described as an exemplary case of technical competence and dedication under difficult conditions.

Her publications and curatorial activity made her a major reference point for Umbrian medieval and Renaissance studies. She curated the exhibition on Nicolo Alunno in Foligno (2004) and "Gubbio al tempo di Giotto" in Gubbio, and co-edited with Francesco Federico Mancini the major volume Il Palazzo Trinci di Foligno (Perugia, Quattroeme, 2001, 729 pp.). Her collaboration with Vittorio Sgarbi on Marchigian artistic culture, including "Da Giotto a Gentile / Pittura e scultura a Fabriano tra Due e Trecento" (Fabriano, 2014), further confirmed her specialization in medieval sculpture, especially scultura lignea. She also contributed to projects linked to the Museo Diocesano in Foligno and to the exhibition Segni di Croce.

Among her most noted contributions was the study of a gonfalon in Gubbio that she identified as a probable early work by Raphael; after its presentation in Gubbio, the work entered major national and international exhibitions. Even after retirement, she remained active in cultural life: in 2018 she was among the founders of the Associazione Umbra Federico II di Svevia Hohenstaufen in Perugia, and she supported projects such as "Castelli eugubini sulla Via di Francesco" (Gubbio, 2024; reprised in Valfabbrica, 2025), alongside documentary initiatives on Umbrian history and art from Antiquity to the Renaissance. Her funeral was held on 6 February 2026 in the Cattedrale di San Lorenzo (Duomo di Perugia), a monument emblematic of the heritage she had spent her life preserving.

Berenson, Bernard

Butrimonys (Lithuania), 26 June 1865 - Fiesole, 6 October 1959

Bernard Berenson was one of the most influential art historians and connoisseurs of the twentieth century and, during his lifetime, the most authoritative international voice on Italian Renaissance painting. Born Bernhard Valvrojenski into a Jewish family in Lithuania, he later became a central figure in the way European and American scholars, collectors, and museums identified, valued, and interpreted early Italian art.

His family emigrated to Boston in 1875 after severe financial losses, and the surname was changed to Berenson. From early youth he demonstrated extraordinary linguistic ability and broad humanistic interests. He studied at Harvard, graduating in 1887 with training in literature and history, and initially moved toward literary criticism. Financial support from Boston patrons, especially Isabella Stewart Gardner and Thomas Sergeant Perry, enabled extended travel in Europe, where his intellectual focus shifted decisively from literature to painting.

Berenson developed a method of attribution that combined Giovanni Morelli's comparative analysis of recurring anatomical details with a psychological reading of artistic personality associated with John Addington Symonds. This synthesis made his connoisseurship unusually systematic while preserving interpretive nuance, and helped define modern attribution practice in Italian art history.

His major early books established his authority rapidly: The Venetian Painters of the Renaissance (1894), The Florentine Painters of the Renaissance (1896), and Lorenzo Lotto: An Essay in Constructive Art Criticism (1895). He became especially known for rigorous reattribution lists that reduced inflated traditional catalogues and imposed stricter standards of authorship. His monumental The Drawings of the Florentine Painters (1903), later expanded in 1938, remains one of the landmark works of twentieth-century art-historical scholarship.

Berenson's influence extended beyond academia into the international art market. As adviser to Lord Duveen and to major American collectors, including Isabella Stewart Gardner and Henry Walters, he shaped acquisitions that would become foundational holdings in American museums. At the same time, his financial arrangements with the trade, particularly undisclosed commissions linked to authentication, generated lasting controversy over the relationship between scholarship and the art market. His authority was also publicly tested in the famous La belle ferronniere litigation associated with Duveen and Andree Hahn.

In 1900 he married Mary Whitall Smith; in the following years they established themselves at Villa I Tatti near Fiesole, transforming it into a major center of study, collecting, and intellectual sociability. During the Second World War, Berenson remained at I Tatti under increasingly dangerous conditions due to anti-Jewish persecution. His wartime observations were later published in Rumour and Reflection, 1941-1944 (1952).

At his death in 1959, Berenson bequeathed Villa I Tatti, together with its collections, gardens, and extraordinary library, to Harvard University. It became the Harvard Center for Italian Renaissance Studies, one of the most important institutions in the world for Renaissance scholarship. Through his writings, attributions, and institutional legacy, Berenson decisively shaped modern understanding of Italian Renaissance art.

Bertelli, Carlo

Rome, 1930

Italian art historian and former public official, widely recognised as a leading specialist in late antique and medieval art, with significant contributions to conservation, photography as historical evidence, and institutional reform in Italy's cultural heritage management. Bertelli studied literature at the University of Rome (La Sapienza), graduating in 1954, and pursued advanced specialisation at the Warburg Institute in London, where he trained under Pietro Toesca, Otto Pächt, Richard Krautheimer, and Mario Salmi. This interdisciplinary formation equipped him to bridge iconographic analysis with material history, particularly in early Christian and medieval painting. He married Ilaria Toesca, herself an art historian, and was the brother of the historian Sergio Bertelli.

From 1956 to 1977 Bertelli edited entries for Treccani's Enciclopedia dell'arte antica, while serving as inspector at the Istituto Centrale per il Restauro from 1958, focusing on unpublished medieval icons and Roman early medieval painting, including the Madonna di Santa Maria in Trastevere (1961). As director of the Gabinetto Fotografico Nazionale (1963–1973), he elevated photography to a rigorous historical tool, a project crystallised in L'immagine fotografica: 1845–1945 (1979). He subsequently directed the Calcografia Nazionale (1973–1975), merging it into the Istituto Nazionale per la Grafica.

Appointed Superintendent for Artistic and Historical Heritage in Milan and Western Lombardy (1978–1984), Bertelli spearheaded the modernisation of the Pinacoteca di Brera and the critical restoration framework for Leonardo's Last Supper. His tenure emphasised systematic recovery of Lombard medieval sites, aligning administrative duties with the scholarly rigour inherited from Cesare Brandi. These efforts earned him honours including the Sanseverino Prize for his Piero della Francesca monograph and gold medals from Italian cultural ministries.

Transitioning to academia after 1984, Bertelli taught at the University of Lausanne until 1995 and subsequently at the Accademia di Architettura in Mendrisio, Switzerland, as professor emeritus of art history at the Università della Svizzera italiana. His principal monographs include Il ciborio della Basilica di Sant'Ambrogio (1981), Piero della Francesca (1991), Lombardia medievale (2003), and Wolvinio e gli angeli (2006). He also curated the major exhibition Il futuro dei Longobardi (2000). His work integrates paleochristian iconography, Renaissance attribution, and archival photography, and constitutes an essential methodological framework for understanding artists such as the Maestro di Galliano and the broader tradition of Lombard monumental painting.

Bologna, Ferdinando

L'Aquila, 27 September 1925 – Ocre, 3 April 2019

Ferdinando Bologna was one of the greatest Italian art historians of the twentieth century, a student of Pietro Toesca and a collaborator of Roberto Longhi, whose career spanned over sixty years across the fields of medieval and modern art and the theory of art historiography, always characterized by philological rigor and a broad historical perspective.

Born in L'Aquila on September 27, 1925, Bologna earned his degree in Rome under the guidance of Pietro Toesca, the great systematizer of medieval Italian painting, from whom he absorbed the attribution method based on rigorous stylistic analysis and archival documentation. At just twenty-five years old, in 1950—the very year of its founding—he joined the editorial staff of the journal Paragone, established by Roberto Longhi as an instrument of cultural struggle and the renewal of Italian art historiography. His association with Longhi was decisive: Bologna absorbed his ability to combine philology and critical sensibility, but over time developed an independent vocation for the historical-social analysis of art, distancing himself from his mentor's pure aesthetics in favor of an interpretation more materialistically rooted in the conditions of production and patronage.

After a brief stint as a young official at the Superintendency of Naples—a city that would remain at the center of his interests throughout his life—Bologna rapidly advanced through the university ranks, becoming a full professor of Medieval and Modern Art History at the University of Naples in 1965. He later taught at the universities of Salerno, Messina, and Siena, where he was among the founders of the School of Specialization in Art History established by Giovanni Previtali. In 1984, he assumed the chair of Medieval and Modern Art History at the University of Rome Tor Vergata, of which he was one of the founders, holding that position until his retirement and remaining professor emeritus until his death. The city of Naples conferred upon him honorary citizenship in 1997, an extremely rare honor that attests to the deep bond between the scholar and the southern region he had loved most.

Bologna's most enduring contribution to medieval studies is undoubtedly La pittura italiana delle origini (1962, reprinted in 1978), a seminal work in which he reconstructs, with a broad perspective, the roots of the Italian pictorial tradition, from early medieval cycles to the emergence of the Romanesque and Proto-Gothic styles, situating Italian painting within the broader context of the late medieval Mediterranean. This is complemented by crucial studies on the Angevin court of Naples—the volume I pittori alla corte angioina di Napoli (1969) remains an indispensable reference for anyone studying 14th-century art in southern Italy—and on the relationship between Naples and the major Mediterranean routes of painting, analyzed in the volume Napoli e le rotte mediterranee della pittura (1977). Also significant were his contributions on Giotto (Novità su Giotto, 1969) and on Simone Martini, of whom he presented a historiographically more accurate portrait than the prevailing interpretations.

A common thread runs through Bologna's entire scholarly output: the historiographical vindication of the art of southern Italy, traditionally neglected by criticism centered on Florence and Rome. His study of the ceiling of the Sala Magna at the Steri in Palermo (The Ceiling of the Sala Magna at the Steri in Palermo and Sicilian Feudal Culture in the Late Middle Ages, 1975, now in its fourth edition) is a paradigmatic example of this approach: Bologna decodes the complex figurative program of the 14th-century ceiling as a mirror of Sicilian feudal culture at the moment of its decline, interweaving art history, political history, and the history of mentalities with a methodology that was pioneering at the time. Similarly, his studies on Francesco Solimena (1958), on Roviale Spagnolo and 16th-Century Neapolitan Painting (1959), and on Gaspare Traversi in the European Enlightenment (1980) demonstrate a constant focus on modern Neapolitan art, in the conviction that it deserved the same critical standing reserved for the northern schools.

Bologna was also a theorist of historiographical method, author of some of the most stimulating contributions on the problem of art history as a discipline. In the volume From the Minor Arts to Industrial Design: History of an Ideology (1972), he investigated the genesis of the concept of "decorative arts" and its ideological function within the capitalist system, anticipating issues that would become central to the international debate of the 1980s. With Methods of Studying Italian Art and the Methodological Problem Today (1979, for Einaudi's Storia dell'arte) and La coscienza storica dell'arte d'Italia (1982), he took a clear stand against what he called Warburgian-derived iconological "mentalism"—the, in his view, casual use of iconology as an interpretive key independent of the material history of the works. He edited the ambitious UTET series Storia dell'arte in Italia (eight volumes, 1980–1988), which remains a standard reference work in Italian university libraries.

In the 1990s, Bologna explored the theme of naturalism in painting with the volume L'incredulità del Caravaggio e l'esperienza delle "cose naturali" (1992), in which he interpreted Caravaggio's realism not as an individual aesthetic choice but as a response to a collective cultural need of post-Tridentine Catholicism, rooted in the tangible concreteness of Franciscan preaching and in the Lombard tradition of observing nature. He also curated the exhibition Battistello Caracciolo and Early Naturalism in Naples (1991), a further contribution to the appreciation of the Neapolitan Caravaggio period. Ferdinando Bologna passed away on April 3, 2019 in Ocre, Abruzzo, at the age of ninety-four, leaving behind a scientific library of extraordinary scope and a school of students who have continued his southern-focused approach to the history of Italian art.

Boskovits, Miklós

Budapest, 1935 – 2011

Hungarian-born Italian art historian renowned for his rigorous scholarship on Tuscan painting from the Duecento through the early Trecento, establishing himself as a pivotal figure in the attribution of anonymous masters and the refinement of chronologies of Sienese and Florentine primitives. Born in Budapest on October 11, 1935, Boskovits fled Hungary amid the 1956 revolution and settled in Italy, where he pursued classical studies and art history at the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore in Milan. Working within the intellectual legacy of Pietro Toesca and under the influence of Cesare Brandi, he developed a method that blended connoisseurship, archival rigour, and close stylistic analysis. He later held a professorship at the same university, where he taught courses on Giotto and the Florentine primitives.

Boskovits revolutionised attributions in medieval Tuscan painting and worked in close collaboration with Luciano Bellosi as one of the foremost experts on the period. His seminal article Su Niccolò di Buonaccorso, Benedetto di Bindo and Sienese Painting in the Early Fifteenth Century (Paragone, 1980) dissected Trecento Sienese developments, while broader studies on Bartolomeo Bulgarini and Niccolò di Buonaccorso clarified workshop practices and stylistic evolutions in the aftermath of the Black Death. His oeuvre emphasised the interplay of Florentine innovation — Giotto's naturalism — with Sienese Gothic elegance, reshaping understandings of figures such as the Master of the Bambino Vispo.

As professor emeritus at the Università Cattolica, Boskovits contributed to catalogues for major exhibitions and institutions, influencing the restoration and museum display of Duecento–Trecento panels. His methodology bridged Brandi's restoration theory with the iconographic precision associated with Carlo Bertelli, as seen in collaborative contexts on Lombard and Sienese heritage. His multi-volume Italian Paintings before 1400 for the National Gallery of Art in Washington remains a fundamental reference tool. He died in 2011, leaving a corpus that continues to guide scholars navigating the anonymous masters of medieval Tuscany.

Brandi, Cesare

Siena, 1906 – Vignano, 1988

Born on April 8, 1906, in Siena, Brandi graduated in literature from the University of Florence in 1928, following studies that also included law at the University of Siena. He began his career in the Administration of Antiquities and Fine Arts, starting in Siena in 1930 where he catalogued paintings for the Academy of Fine Arts, and later moving to Bologna in 1933 as an inspector, during which he organised early restoration workshops and exhibitions such as the Exhibition of Riminese Painting of the Fourteenth Century in 1935. These formative roles immersed him in practical conservation challenges, shaping his later theoretical innovations amid Italy's rich medieval and Renaissance heritage.

In 1939, Brandi became the inaugural director of the Istituto Centrale per il Restauro (Central Institute for Restoration) in Rome — a position he held until 1959 — transforming it into a global benchmark for conservation science. Proposed for the role by fellow critic Giulio Carlo Argan, he designed the institute to integrate phenomenological philosophy with hands-on techniques, addressing postwar damages to frescoes in places like Mantua and Viterbo. His tenure extended to UNESCO collaborations from 1948, international consultancies, and the role of Superintendent of Antiquities and Fine Arts from 1953, earning honours such as the Feltrinelli Prize for art criticism in 1958, shared with Argan.

Brandi's enduring legacy stems from his theory of restoration, first outlined in the 1963 book Teoria del Restauro, which posits that restoration must respect an artwork's historical authenticity, aesthetic unity, and material integrity — often summarised as the "duality of matter and form." Drawing from philosophers including Heidegger, Husserl, Hegel, and Croce, he argued for interventions that reveal rather than replace the object's temporal patina, influencing Italy's 1972 restoration charter and worldwide conservation practice. Major publications such as Le due vie (1966) and Teoria generale della critica (1974) further explored aesthetics and criticism, sparking debates with thinkers including Roland Barthes.

As a professor of medieval and modern art history — first as a free lecturer at the University of Rome from 1934, then holding chairs at Palermo (1961) and Rome's La Sapienza — Brandi mentored generations of scholars while producing essays on artists such as Filippo de Pisis and catalogues of Tuscan collections. He founded journals including L'Immagine (1947–1950) and the institute's Bollettino, blending criticism with travelogues and poetry that reflected his phenomenological approach to art's encounter with time. He died on January 19, 1988, in Vignano, leaving an adopted son, Vittorio Brandi Rubiu, who also pursued art history.

Carli, Enzo

Pisa, August 20, 1910 - Siena, September 26, 1999

Enzo Carli was one of the leading Italian art historians of the twentieth century, an authority on the study of medieval Sienese and Pisan art, as well as a museum director, curator, and extraordinarily prolific writer.

He graduated from the University of Pisa, where he was first a student of Mario Salmi, one of the founders of modern Italian medieval art history, and later of Matteo Marangoni. His thesis was dedicated to the Sienese sculptor Tino di Camaino (1285-1337), a choice that perfectly foreshadowed the two geographical and stylistic poles around which his entire research would revolve: Pisa and Siena, and the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Alongside his university studies, he cultivated music, studying piano and composition, and poetry with equal passion, two dimensions that accompanied his intellectual life until the end.

From 1933, he taught art history in Florentine high schools, gaining experience through direct contact with Tuscany's artistic heritage. In 1937, he was appointed Superintendent in L'Aquila, and in 1939 he was transferred to Siena, the city that became his permanent home and the center of his work for over sixty years. In Siena, he directed the Pinacoteca Nazionale until 1952 and the Museo dell'Opera Metropolitana del Duomo until 1973, two institutions that housed the masterpieces of medieval Sienese painting, from Duccio to Simone Martini, and from Pietro to Ambrogio Lorenzetti. That long tenure at the helm of Siena's museums allowed him to develop a direct, daily, and inexhaustible knowledge of the original works, which translated into a level of meticulousness in attribution and description that was rare indeed.

In 1942, he obtained a teaching qualification in Medieval and Modern Art History, which he exercised at the Faculty of Letters of the University of Pisa. In 1962, he won a university competition and was appointed to the Faculty of Letters of the University of Genoa; subsequently, he taught Art History at the University of Siena, thus combining his museum work with his academic career.

His approach to the study of artistic phenomena was always rooted in a historicism of Crocean descent: rigorous formal analysis, historical contextualization, and attention to the expressive quality of the work, without veering into formalism or sociological interpretations.

His bibliographic output was vast, over 500 titles, including monographs, specialized essays, catalogs, and popular texts. His fundamental contributions primarily concern Sienese art: I capolavori dell'arte senese (Milan, 1946), La pittura senese (Milan, 1955), and numerous monographs on individual masters, including Duccio, Simone Martini, the Lorenzetti, Giovanni di Paolo, and Sassetta. On the Pisan front, he dealt with equal depth with Romanesque sculpture, the Camposanto Monumentale, and Duecento painting. In collaboration with Gian Alberto Dell'Acqua, he edited Storia dell'arte ad uso dei licei, a textbook published in multiple volumes and editions between 1952 and 1986, which educated generations of Italian students.

In 1980, he received the President of the Republic Award as an emeritus member of the Accademia dei Lincei. In 1995, the Accademia dei Lincei awarded him the Feltrinelli Prize for Art and Poetry Criticism, one of the most prestigious honors in Italian culture, crowning a career of exceptional consistency and breadth. He died in Siena on September 26, 1999, and is buried in the Monumental Cemetery of Pisa. In his memory, his children established the Enzo Carli Fund at the University of Siena Library, which houses his books, papers, and scholarly correspondence.

Cavalcaselle, Giovanni Battista

Legnago, 22 January 1819 - Rome, 31 October 1897

Giovanni Battista Cavalcaselle was an Italian writer, critic, and art historian, born at Legnago on 22 January 1819 and dead in Rome on 31 October 1897, who is widely regarded as one of the founders of modern Italian art history and art criticism. He is especially famous for his long collaboration with Joseph Archer Crowe, with whom he produced major histories of Italian and Flemish painting that remained influential well into the twentieth century.

Cavalcaselle studied at the Accademia di Belle Arti in Venice, but from an early stage his real formation came from direct travel and close observation of artworks across Italy rather than from academic routine alone. Treccani describes him as moving from town to town on foot, visiting churches, convents, palaces, and collections, and recording newly seen works in sketches and notes that became the foundation of his later method.

In 1846-47 he traveled through Germany, especially Munich, Dresden, Leipzig, and Berlin, expanding his knowledge of northern collections and meeting the English art enthusiast Joseph Archer Crowe. When the revolutions of 1848 broke out, he returned to Italy, took part in the anti-Austrian uprising, later fought for the Roman Republic, and was eventually condemned to death in absentia by the Austrians, forcing him into exile. By 1850 he was living in London in difficult circumstances, supported only modestly by relatives and by the network of friends he had formed abroad.

In London, Cavalcaselle renewed his connection with Crowe, and together they published Early Flemish Painters in 1856, followed by the major History of Painting in Italy beginning in 1864. Their partnership later produced A History of Painting in North Italy, as well as substantial studies on Titian and Raphael, and these works helped establish a more empirical and comparative way of writing art history in English. Even though many of their individual attributions have since been revised, modern scholars still cite them because of the extraordinary breadth of firsthand looking on which their books were based.

Cavalcaselle's reputation rested above all on his powers of attribution and connoisseurship, and Italian scholarship has often treated him as a founder of modern art criticism for that reason. His sketchbooks and notes, preserved in Venice, are considered vital evidence of both his visual method and his vast range of knowledge. He also contributed to the development of restoration thinking, proposing more updated criteria at a time when the conceptual basis of restoration was still unsettled in Italy.

After Italian unification, Cavalcaselle entered public service and from 1875 to 1893 worked in Rome as a central inspector in the state administration responsible for excavations, museums, antiquities, and fine arts. In that role he traveled widely through the newly unified kingdom, advising on museum organization, acquisitions, and urgent restorations, and in some cases personally supervising interventions. For a scholar of medieval and early Renaissance art, his importance lies not only in the books he wrote, but in the union he forged between travel, archival diligence, visual analysis, attribution, and cultural administration.

Casazza, Ornella

Rezzoaglio (Genoa), 1943 – Florence, March 2020

Ornella Casazza was one of the most celebrated and respected Italian restorers of the twentieth century, widely recognized as a leading authority in the conservation of pictorial heritage. Although born in Rezzoaglio in Liguria in 1943, she spent almost her entire professional life in Florence, where she developed the career that would place her at the center of postwar restoration culture in Italy and abroad.

Her formation unfolded in a decisive historical context. Living in Florence at the time of the catastrophic flood of 4 November 1966, she experienced directly the emergency that transformed the discipline of conservation and brought her into contact with Umberto Baldini and the restoration environment that later shaped the Opificio delle Pietre Dure's post-flood methodologies. After a long workshop apprenticeship, she entered the Opificio officially in 1974, following a national competitive examination.

The range and quality of her interventions are exceptional. Her work on Cimabue's Crucifix in Santa Croce became a key site for the refinement of lacuna reintegration methods, while the restoration of Botticelli's Primavera was presented as a methodological landmark in the 1982 exhibition Method and Science. The most internationally celebrated project of her career, however, was the direction of the restoration of the Brancacci Chapel frescoes by Masaccio, Masolino, and Filippino Lippi in Santa Maria del Carmine, a campaign considered exemplary for both technical innovation and historical results. She also directed the restoration of Masaccio's San Giovenale Triptych, a fundamental work for early fifteenth-century Florentine painting.

Casazza's contribution was as theoretical as it was practical. Through sustained work on flood-damaged paintings and on major medieval and Renaissance panels, she helped define and stabilize the methods of chromatic selection and chromatic abstraction, approaches that made visual reintegration possible without falsifying the original. In this sense, her work belongs to the broader methodological transformation associated with the Florentine school of restoration in the second half of the twentieth century. Antonio Paolucci would later remember her as a restorer of exceptional standing with international recognition.

After advancing through further competitive appointments, Casazza also held major institutional roles, including leadership of advanced-technology research for conservation in Florence and direction of the Museum of Silver and Porcelain at Palazzo Pitti. She curated projects that connected restoration, decorative arts, and modern production, and remained active as a teacher and author in Italy and abroad, with publications on conservation, iconography, and painting techniques that remain widely cited. She died in Florence in March 2020. The 2021 volume L'eredità culturale di Ornella Casazza, edited by Lura Felici, stands as the principal collective tribute to her life and legacy.

Colgrave, Bertram

Derry, 1889 - Cambridge, 13 January 1968

Bertram Colgrave was one of the twentieth century's foremost authorities on the Latin hagiographic literature of early Anglo-Saxon England, best known for his critical editions of the lives of saints Cuthbert, Wilfrid, Guthlac, and Gregory the Great, and above all for his landmark edition of Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People.

Colgrave was born in 1889 in Derry, Ireland, and pursued a distinguished academic formation at two of England's leading universities. After completing his first degree, he went on to study Anglo-Saxon and Middle English at Clare College, Cambridge, where he developed the deep philological training in Old and Middle English that would underpin his entire scholarly career. He taught briefly at Merchiston Castle School near Edinburgh from 1916 to 1918, before moving into the full-time academic life that defined him.

Colgrave spent the central decades of his career at Durham University, and his close association with Durham was not merely institutional but deeply personal. Durham Cathedral is the resting place of Saint Cuthbert, and Colgrave wrote in the preface to his edition of the Two Lives of Saint Cuthbert that editing those texts was "almost a pious duty for one who lives under the shadow of Durham Cathedral". He became one of the university's leading scholars of early medieval hagiography, wrote the official guide to the city and diocese of Durham, and on his retirement in 1954 was celebrated as one of the institution's most distinguished figures.

His most enduring contribution to medieval studies was the preparation of scrupulously edited, bilingual Latin-English critical editions of foundational texts of early Northumbrian Christianity. His edition of the Two Lives of Saint Cuthbert, presenting both the anonymous Life composed by a monk of Lindisfarne between 698 and 705 and Bede's polished prose Life of 721, remains a standard scholarly reference. Taken together, the two texts illuminate the golden age of Northumbrian monasticism and the ecclesiastical tensions between Celtic and Roman practice, resolved in the generation after Whitby.

He also prepared editions of the Latin Life of Wilfrid by Eddius Stephanus, the Life of Guthlac by Felix, and the earliest Life of Gregory the Great by the anonymous monk of Whitby, works that form the core corpus of early Anglo-Saxon hagiography. His greatest posthumous achievement was the monumental critical edition of Bede's Ecclesiastical History, co-edited with R. A. B. Mynors and published by Oxford University Press in 1969, the year after his death. In the Oxford Medieval Texts series, with facing Latin text and Colgrave's English translation, it has remained continuously in print and is cited in virtually all anglophone scholarship on early medieval England.

Beyond editorial work, Colgrave served from 1950 to 1963 as the founding editor-in-chief of Early English Manuscripts in Facsimile, a landmark series that made major Anglo-Saxon manuscripts accessible to scholars internationally. This role placed him at the intersection of palaeography, codicology, and literary history, the same intersection in which illuminated manuscript studies are grounded. After retiring from Durham in 1954, he held visiting professorships at the University of North Carolina, the University of Texas, the University of Kansas, the University of Colorado, and Mount Holyoke College, carrying the study of Bede and early English hagiography to American audiences.

Colgrave died on 13 January 1968 in Cambridge. His editions remain indispensable tools for scholars working on early medieval England, Northumbrian Christianity, and the Latin literary culture of the seventh and eighth centuries, a body of work whose care, erudition, and longevity place him among the major medievalists of the twentieth century.

Crowe, Joseph Archer

London, 25 October 1825 – Gamburg an der Tauber, 6 September 1896

Joseph Archer Crowe was an English journalist, diplomat, and art historian whose work with Giovanni Battista Cavalcaselle helped give art history in English a more rigorous, evidence-based method. Born in London on 25 October 1825 and dead at Gamburg an der Tauber, in present-day Germany, on 6 September 1896, he belonged to that rare nineteenth-century generation whose careers moved easily between politics, letters, and scholarship.

Crowe was born into a cultivated and cosmopolitan family as the son of the journalist Eyre Evans Crowe, and he spent much of his boyhood in France, especially in Paris, where his father worked as a correspondent and where the family home became a meeting place for liberal and artistic circles. His education was directed largely by his father, and this early exposure to languages, journalism, and continental culture gave him an unusually international outlook for an English writer of his time. He was also the brother of the painter Eyre Crowe, so the visual arts were part of his family world from the beginning.

After returning to England in the 1840s, Crowe entered journalism, working for the Morning Chronicle and the Daily News before serving as a correspondent for the Illustrated London News during the Crimean War. He later reported for The Times during the Indian Mutiny and the Austro-Italian War, and he was present at the Battle of Solferino, an experience that placed him close to one of the defining military events of nineteenth-century Europe. This journalistic training sharpened the habits of close observation and factual scrutiny that later marked his writing on painting and attribution.

In 1860 Crowe entered the British diplomatic service as consul-general for Saxony at Leipzig, beginning a second career that ran alongside his scholarly work for decades. He later served at Dusseldorf, then as commercial attache in Berlin and Vienna, and eventually held responsibility for commercial affairs across Europe; he also represented Britain at the Danube Conference in 1883, the Samoa Conference in 1889, and the Telegraph Congress in Paris in 1890. His public service was recognized with appointment as Companion of the Bath in 1885 and Knight Commander of the Order of St. Michael and St. George in 1890, and his private life included a marriage in 1861 to Asta von Barby, with whom he had a large family.

Crowe's lasting fame rests above all on his partnership with Giovanni Battista Cavalcaselle, the Italian critic and connoisseur with whom he collaborated on a series of major works that transformed the study of European painting. Their first major book, The Early Flemish Painters of 1856, was followed by A New History of Painting in Italy from the Second to the Sixteenth Century and then by A History of Painting in North Italy, as well as substantial monographs on Titian and Raphael. In these works, Crowe supplied much of the prose structure and literary form, while the collaboration as a whole depended on sustained travel, firsthand viewing, stylistic comparison, and the attempt to reconstruct the historical development of individual artists.

What made Crowe important was not simply the number of books he wrote, but the method those books embodied: a disciplined reliance on direct visual examination, chronology, and connoisseurship rather than on inherited tradition alone. Even though many individual attributions made by Crowe and Cavalcaselle have been revised by later scholars, their work still stands near the beginning of modern art-historical writing in English because it treated painting as something to be studied critically, comparatively, and historically. Crowe published his memoirs, Reminiscences of Thirty-Five Years of My Life, in 1895, one year before his death, leaving behind the image of a Victorian scholar whose authority was built as much on travel and observation as on reading and theory.

Cuppini, Luciano

active second half of the 20th century

Luciano Cuppini was an Italian art historian active in Verona during the second half of the twentieth century, serving as a key link between the great tradition of Longhian criticism and local research on Venetian medieval and Renaissance art. Although he never held a university chair, his influence was exerted with extraordinary effectiveness through his teaching and scholarly contributions, leaving a lasting mark on Veronese art historiography.

Cuppini trained as a direct student of Roberto Longhi, the greatest Italian art historian of the twentieth century, known for his method based on close examination of works and a stylistic analysis of extraordinary acuity. This apprenticeship left an indelible mark on Cuppini's method: his ability to interpret pictorial and sculptural forms, to identify individual artists and workshops, and to link archival documents to anonymous artistic figures directly reflects the Longhi school. The correspondence preserved at the Longhi Foundation in Florence documents a letter-writing relationship between the two dating back at least to 1950, attesting to an intellectual association that continued even after their formative years.

Upon returning to Verona, Cuppini began a career as a teacher of art history at the Liceo Classico Scipione Maffei, one of the city's most prestigious schools, historically linked to Verona's antiquarian and literary culture. His teaching was instrumental in shaping a generation of art historians: among his students was Paola Marini, who would later direct the Civic Art Museums of Verona from 1997 to 2015 and subsequently the Gallerie dell'Accademia in Venice until 2018. Cuppini was thus not only a researcher but also an educator capable of transmitting the rigor of the attribution method to future professionals in Italian cultural heritage.

On the scholarly level, Cuppini focused on two major thematic areas. The first concerns medieval painting and sculpture in Verona: together with Maria Teresa Cuppini, presumably a close collaborator or family member, he developed in the 1950s and 1960s the critical identification of the sculptural corpus of Giovanni di Rigino, the leading fourteenth-century Veronese sculptor and creator of signed works, thereby contributing to defining the artistic character of Verona under the Scaliger dynasty. His studies on the Arca di Cangrande I della Scala proposed specific attributions for the equestrian statues and funerary reliefs that subsequent critics would debate and, in part, revise.

The second area concerns thirteenth-century Italian painting in the broadest sense. In 1952, he published the essay Ranieri di Ugolino (Commentari, vol. 3, pp. 7-13), in which he identified the Pisan painter Ranieri di Ugolino with the so-called Master of San Martino, the creator of the large panel preserved at the National Museum of San Matteo in Pisa. This proposal, shared by Garrison and supported by Battisti in 1963, did not gain definitive acceptance from subsequent critics, but it attests to the breadth of Cuppini's interests, which ranged from local Veronese painting to thirteenth-century Pisan panel paintings.

A little-known but significant aspect of Cuppini's life is his collecting. In 1993, he donated to the Castelvecchio Museum in Verona a substantial private collection of ancient drawings, which remains a valuable documentary resource for the study of Venetian graphic art. This donation, likely made shortly before his death, was an act of returning to the Veronese community the heritage he had accumulated over decades of study and research.

Luciano Cuppini's legacy can be measured on two distinct yet complementary levels. As a researcher, his contributions to our understanding of Scaliger sculpture and thirteenth-century Pisan painting remain key references in the scholarly literature, cited in major studies on medieval art in Verona and Tuscany. As a teacher and mentor, his influence lives on in the careers of his students and in the direction of art studies in Verona during the second half of the twentieth century, at a time when the city was establishing its own museums and cultural institutions. He epitomizes the figure of the high-profile local scholar who, despite not holding a university chair, worked with a methodological rigor fully comparable to that of his academic colleagues.

De Angelis, Luigi

Citta della Pieve, 1759 - Siena, 13 August 1832

Luigi De Angelis was one of the most significant figures in Sienese culture at the turn of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: theologian, librarian, scholar, and art historian, he played a decisive role in preserving Siena's artistic and literary heritage during the Napoleonic period.

Born in 1759 in Citta della Pieve (Perugia), where his father held a papal office, he moved with his family in 1764 to Piancastagnaio, in the Sienese area. Orphaned young, he found support with the Preti Ricci family of Abbadia San Salvatore. On 17 February 1775, still a teenager, he entered the convent of San Francesco in Siena as a novice, beginning a solid Franciscan ecclesiastical career.

After advanced theological studies, he earned a degree in Dogmatic Theology at the College of Saint Bonaventure in Rome in 1786. From 1791 he taught Philosophy and Moral Theology in the seminaries of Foligno and Florence, and in 1792 returned to Siena as custodian of the Franciscan custody.

His ascent in the Franciscan hierarchy was rapid: guardian of the monastery in 1798, examiner of clergy for the Diocese of Siena in 1801, and definitor for the Province of Tuscia in 1802. His scholarly prestige and ties with ruling elites led to his appointment, on 14 April 1803, to the chair of Dogmatic Theology at the University of Siena by Maria Luisa of Bourbon, regent queen of the Kingdom of Etruria. In 1805 he was also charged with reviewing librettos for operas and theatrical performances in Siena.

When the French administration abolished the University of Siena in 1808, De Angelis lost his chair but reoriented his career with remarkable diplomatic skill. On 11 March 1810 he was appointed librarian of the Public Library of Siena, reopened on 4 April 1810 as an institution of the Civic Community. In that office he transformed the library into a major center for collecting manuscripts, incunabula, and artworks from Napoleonic suppressions, expanding holdings from roughly ten thousand to more than forty-five thousand volumes.

His impact on art preservation was equally decisive. Paintings gathered from suppressed religious institutions, together with earlier collections assembled by Giuseppe Ciaccheri, formed the founding nucleus of what became the National Art Gallery of Siena. De Angelis can therefore be regarded as the effective founder of Siena's first public art museum.

With the reopening of the University in 1814, he resumed teaching theology while also serving as curator of the Cabinet of Fine Arts. In Sienese art historiography, he continued the municipal scholarly tradition associated with figures such as Giulio Mancini and Guglielmo Della Valle, promoting the reevaluation of Sienese painting against the dominant Florentine model. He devoted special attention to Fra Giacomo da Torrita (Jacopo Torriti), authoring Notizie istorico-critiche and praising him as a master of the renewal of Italian mosaic art and a precursor to Simone Martini.

He was also the first scholar to recognize the historical and artistic value of the Biccherna Panels, at a time when they were still largely neglected by critics.

Among his principal publications are Discorso storico su l'Universita di Siena (Siena, 1810), Ragguaglio del nuovo Istituto delle Belle Arti stabilito in Siena con la descrizione della sala nella quale sono distribuiti cronologicamente i quadri dell'antica Scuola Senese (Siena, 1816), and Capitoli dei Disciplinati della venerabile Compagnia della Madonna sotto le volte dell'Imperiale e Reale Spedale di Santa Maria della Scala di Siena (Siena, 1818), which includes on pages 153-282 an important catalogue of Italian-language manuscripts in the Siena Municipal Library that remains a key reference for researchers. He also left an unpublished manuscript, Di Giulio Mancini elogio, later published and studied as evidence of his commitment to revaluing Sienese art history.

In 1825 he was appointed provost of the collegiate church of Santa Maria in Provenzano in Siena. The following year he purchased, with his own funds, the ruined chapel of San Francesco all'Alberino, restored it, and dedicated to it the monograph Dell'albero di San Francesco vicino alle mura di Siena (Siena, 1827). He died in Siena on 13 August 1832 and was buried in that same chapel. His biography has been comprehensively reconstructed by Alessandro Leoncini in Luigi De Angelis. A Life Between University, Library, and Art Gallery (University of Siena, 2014), presented at the Biblioteca degli Intronati in March 2015.

De Francovich, Géza

Gorizia, 1902 – Rome, 1996

Géza de Francovich was one of the most wide-ranging and methodologically rigorous Italian art historians of the twentieth century, whose scholarly interests spanned from Romanesque sculpture to the art of the ancient Near East.

De Francovich was born on August 28, 1902, in Gorizia — then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire — into an aristocratic family of Hungarian origin. He attended the German-language section of the Staatsgymnasium in Gorizia before the First World War disrupted his youth. After the conflict, he moved to Florence, where he completed his university degree in 1925. His formation thus combined Central European cultural sensibility with a deep immersion in the Italian art historical tradition, an unusual and productive duality that would shape his entire career.

Following his graduation, de Francovich took up a post as inspector at the Soprintendenza of Perugia from 1926 to 1928. He was subsequently recruited by the philosopher Giovanni Gentile to serve as a contributor to the Enciclopedia Italiana, where he worked alongside some of the leading art historians of the time — Pietro Toesca, Lionello Venturi, and Mario Salmi — whose assistant he also became at the University of Rome. He obtained his libera docenza (the Italian qualification for independent university teaching) from 1937, and from 1956 to 1972 he held the chair of medieval art history at the Università degli Studi di Roma "La Sapienza".

De Francovich's research was characterized by a rigorous historical method that placed artistic objects firmly within their broader ideological and cultural contexts. His thematic range was exceptional: he moved from Lombard Carolingian and Ottonian art to Romanesque wood sculpture, from Byzantine ivory carving to Achaemenid and Sasanian art, always grounding his analysis in direct examination of the monuments themselves. His 1937 article "A Romanesque School of Wood Carvers in Central Italy," published in the Art Bulletin, established an early international reputation. His most celebrated work remains the monumental two-volume Benedetto Antelami, architetto e scultore e l'arte del suo tempo (1952), a landmark study of the great Romanesque sculptor, sponsored by Bernard Berenson.

De Francovich traveled extensively throughout his career, conducting fieldwork not only across Europe but throughout the former Byzantine Empire and the Middle East. In 1966, he coordinated a team of Italian scholars from La Sapienza in a series of study trips to the historic regions of Armenia, resulting in extensive photographic documentation of medieval churches and monasteries. For the Italian National Research Council (CNR), he directed the multi-volume projects Studi di architettura medievale armena (4 vols., 1971–73) and Studi sulla pittura medievale campana (3 vols., 1974–81). His intellectual journey concluded with Santuari e tombe rupestri dell'antica Frigia (1990), a study of rock-cut sanctuaries in ancient Phrygia (modern Turkey), published when he was nearly ninety years old. He died in Rome on November 24, 1996.

De Marchi, Andrea

Biella, 1962

Andrea De Marchi is one of Italy's leading scholars of medieval art history and currently serves as Full Professor of Medieval Art History at the University of Florence. Trained in Siena under Luciano Bellosi, he developed within one of the most rigorous traditions of Italian medieval art criticism, combining close stylistic reading with documentary and liturgical contextualization.

After serving as an inspector at the Soprintendenza of Pisa (1994-1995), he was researcher at the University of Lecce (1995-2000) and then professor at the University of Udine (2000-2006), before moving to Florence. He is affiliated with the SAGAS Department (History, Archaeology, Geography, Art, and Performing Arts), based at Via Gino Capponi 9, Florence.

De Marchi's research focuses primarily on painting, drawing, and miniature art from the Gothic to the Renaissance, with particular attention to the decoration of mendicant churches, especially Franciscan contexts, in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. A further central axis of his work is the evolution of the altarpiece from Gothic to Renaissance forms, examined through workshop practice, patronage, and the liturgical functions of images.

He has also made significant contributions to philological and attributional questions, especially in the reconstruction of fragmented contexts and dismembered works. His method repeatedly links objects to their original spatial and devotional settings, clarifying how artistic programs were read and used inside ecclesiastical spaces.

His first major monograph, Gentile da Fabriano. Viaggio nella pittura italiana alla fine del gotico (1992; reissued 2006), remains a fundamental reference for late Gothic painting. He later published major studies on fifteenth-century painting in the Marche, including Pittori a Camerino nel Quattrocento (2002) and, with Matteo Mazzalupi, Pittori ad Ancona nel Quattrocento (2008). He was also co-author, with Francesco Caglioti, of the exhibition project on Verrocchio at Palazzo Strozzi (Florence, 2019).

Since 2015, De Marchi has chaired the Association "Corpus of Florentine Painting," the long-term project dedicated to digitizing and critically updating the historic corpus of medieval and Renaissance Florentine painting begun in the early twentieth century. He also supervises doctoral research on late Gothic and early Renaissance Sienese painting, reflecting an active and ongoing role in advanced scholarly training.

Particularly relevant for manuscript and materials-based studies is his 2016 essay Aureola e grandine. La materia che cattura la luce e la trasfigura: esperienze nella pittura tardogotica, in which he investigates the relationship between matter, light, and spiritual transfiguration in late Gothic painting.

Demus, Otto

1902 – 1990

Otto Demus was an Austrian art historian and Byzantinist widely regarded as one of the towering figures of twentieth-century medieval art scholarship. Hans Belting famously described him as "the eye" of art history in his areas of study, in recognition of Demus's exceptional visual acuity and connoisseurship.

Demus studied art history at the University of Vienna between 1921 and 1928 under Josef Strzygowski and received his doctorate summa cum laude. He is generally considered part of the second Vienna School of Art History, rooted in the theoretical legacy of Alois Riegl and committed to understanding the artwork as a functional perceptual whole rather than as a merely documentary object. In keeping with this approach, Demus consistently analyzed how figures, architecture, and light operated together in decorative programs to produce a unified visual and spiritual experience.

After the annexation of Austria in 1938, Demus refused to cooperate with the Nazi regime and emigrated to England in 1939. During the war years in London, he worked on what became his most theoretically foundational book, Byzantine Mosaic Decoration (1947), a project first conceived during his earlier fieldwork in Greece, Sicily, and Venice in the 1920s and 1930s.

Among Demus's most original and lasting contributions is the concept of the space icon (Raum-Ikone). In his analysis of middle Byzantine mosaic programs, he argued that Byzantine artists did not rely on Western linear perspective, which draws the eye into illusionistic depth, but instead employed a "negative perspective" in which figures seem to project outward toward the beholder. In this reading, curved apses, vaults, and domes become active collaborators with mosaic imagery, producing a strong sense of presence in the real space of the worshipper and fundamentally reframing the image-viewer relationship in Byzantine art.

Two monument groups occupied decades of his research: the Basilica of San Marco in Venice and the Norman churches of Sicily. His four-volume The Mosaics of San Marco in Venice remains the authoritative study of the basilica's decoration, and he was the first scholar to assess fully the central role of mosaic as San Marco's most indigenous and aesthetically accomplished medium. In The Mosaics of Norman Sicily (1949), he examined the transfer of Byzantine craftsmen and models into the Norman kingdom and the resulting hybrid artistic language.

In 1966 Demus delivered the Metropolitan Museum of Art lectures in New York that became Byzantine Art and the West (1970), widely regarded as a major work of twentieth-century Byzantine scholarship. The book traces, from the seventh to the thirteenth century, the sustained dialogue between Byzantine and Western artistic models.

After returning to Vienna, Demus was appointed Professor of Art History at the University of Vienna in 1963. Together with manuscript specialist Otto Pacht, the pair known as die zwei Ottos transformed the Vienna Institute into what contemporaries described as a Mekka der Mittelalterkunstgeschichte, an international center for medieval art historical study spanning both monumental and manuscript traditions.

Diehl, Charles (Michel-Charles)

Strasbourg, 4 July 1859 - Paris, 1 November 1944

Charles Diehl, whose full name was Michel-Charles Diehl, was the most important French Byzantinist of his generation and one of the key figures in the history of Byzantine art and civilization studies. Born in Strasbourg on July 4, 1859, his academic and scholarly career spanned more than half a century of tireless research.

Diehl completed his secondary education in Strasbourg, Nancy, and Paris with brilliant results, and in 1878 he was admitted to the prestigious Ecole Normale Superieure in Paris. His doctoral thesis, published in 1888 under the title Etudes sur l'administration byzantine dans l'exarchat de Ravenne, marked his official entry into Byzantine studies and remains to this day a benchmark for the history of the Exarchate of Ravenna, a subject of great importance for those interested in the medieval art of northern Italy.

In 1899, when the first chair of Byzantine History in France was established at the Sorbonne, Diehl was appointed to fill it, holding the position until 1934. From this position of exceptional influence, he trained generations of French, Italian, Greek, and Romanian scholars, thereby founding a true school of European Byzantine studies. In 1910, he was elected a member of the Academie des inscriptions et belles-lettres, of which he served as president in 1920-1921.

Diehl's scholarly output was extraordinarily vast and touched on every aspect of Byzantine civilization. His Manuel d'art byzantin (1910, second edition 1915) was the first systematic handbook on Byzantine art and remained an indispensable reference work for decades. The essay Byzance. Grandeur et decadence (1919), translated into Italian as La civilta bizantina in 1962 and into English by Naomi Walford in 1957, cemented his international fame.

Among the popular works that made him well known even outside the academic world, Figures byzantines (1906-1908, in two series) and Theodora, imperatrice de Byzance (1904) stand out; these works of elegant historical prose evoked the personalities of the Eastern Empire with a literary vividness rare among professional historians. Diehl also wrote an important volume on the history of Venice, Une republique patricienne (1915), which demonstrates his ability to look beyond strictly Eastern boundaries.

Diehl was not an armchair scholar: he conducted field research campaigns in Greece, North Africa, and Ravenna. Between 1907 and 1909, he directed the restoration of the Byzantine church of Hagia Sophia in Thessaloniki, and the Greek city honored his work by naming a street after him near that very church. His study L'Afrique byzantine (1896) opened new perspectives on the legacy of Byzantium throughout the Mediterranean.

Charles Diehl died in Paris on November 1, 1944. In his will, he bequeathed 240,000 francs to the Academie des inscriptions et belles-lettres for the establishment of a prize for the best works on Byzantine history and culture, a gesture that encapsulates his lifelong dedication to the discipline he had helped to found. His work, combining the rigor of archival research with accessible prose and a comprehensive vision of Byzantine civilization, remains a model of how medieval art history can be both science and literature.

Della Valle, Guglielmo

Moncalvo, 1746 - Turin, 1805

Guglielmo della Valle was an Italian art writer and Conventual Franciscan friar, now regarded as a key figure in eighteenth-century Italian art historiography. His importance for medieval art studies lies in his pioneering archival research on early Sienese and Tuscan painting, carried out well before the discipline assumed its modern academic form.

Likely born in 1745-1746 in Moncalvo, in the Monferrato region, to Guglielmo Antonio Della Valle and Anna Maria De Ferrari, he entered the Franciscan convent in Pinerolo in 1762 at a young age. As a Conventual friar he traveled extensively across Italy, studying firsthand the works of the major schools of Florence, Siena, and Pisa. In Rome, he became assistant to the Order and general secretary in 1783, and in 1784 he was appointed prior of the convent of the Twelve Apostles.

His principal achievement is the three-volume Lettere sanesi sopra le belle arti (Rome, 1782-1786), a monumental publication of enduring value for its documentary and archival content. The first volume addresses general methodological and historical issues; the second, composed of twenty-three monographs, concentrates largely on Sienese painting; and the third, with thirty-three letters, examines specific painters and sculptors, mostly Sienese, including a foundational letter to Abbot Lanzi on the Tuscan schools.

Della Valle advanced an original and controversial historiographical thesis, arguing that the Sienese school was older and more autonomous than the Florentine one, and that Florentine painting had effectively declined after Cimabue while Sienese traditions endured longer. Although later scholarship rejected this larger framework for lack of sufficient documentary support, several of his insights proved remarkably prescient: he identified the influence of Giunta Pisano on Cimabue, that of Nicola and Giovanni Pisano on Giotto, and emphasized the Roman component in Giotto's formation through figures such as Pietro Cavallini and Jacopo Torriti.

In 1791 he edited an important version of Vasari's lives, revising and updating descriptions through his own research. This edition remained a significant bibliographic reference for art criticism well into the late nineteenth century and confirms his role as a transitional figure between antiquarian erudition and modern historical method.

Delpriori, Alessandro

Fabriano (Marche), active 21st century

Alessandro Delpriori is one of Italy's most active art historians in the field of medieval and late Gothic painting in central Italy, with particular attention to the schools of Umbria and the Marche between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. Originally from Fabriano, a city central to the history of Italian late Gothic painting, he has developed a scholarly profile in which local artistic traditions are read within broader interregional networks of production, patronage, and stylistic exchange.

He completed his secondary studies at the "V. Volterra" scientific high school in Fabriano (diploma, 1996), then earned a degree in History and Conservation of Cultural Heritage at the University of Macerata (2004). He subsequently obtained a PhD in Art History at the University of Florence between 2008 and 2010. This training consolidated his specialization in medieval and late Gothic visual culture, with a focus on attributional issues, workshop dynamics, and the reconstruction of regional artistic identities in central Italy.

Delpriori is currently Professor and Researcher in Art History at the University of Camerino, where since 2015 he has taught in the Bachelor's program in Technology and Diagnostics for Conservation and Restoration. He has also taught at the University of Macerata and at the University of Florence. Alongside academic teaching, he has held significant institutional roles: from 2008 to 2011 he served as director and curator of the collections of the Cariverona Foundation and the Domus Foundation for Modern and Contemporary Art in Verona; from 2011 to 2014 he was curator of the Frascione Collection in Florence, overseeing research, cataloging, and restoration projects.

He collaborates with the Cini Foundation in Venice on the updating of catalogues of works and is a member of the Board of Directors of the Museum System of the Province of Macerata. His principal research interests include medieval painting and sculpture in central Italy, with specific studies on Gentile da Fabriano, fourteenth-century wooden sculpture in Umbria and the Marche, the Master of the Fabriano Crucifix, Luca di Paolo, Lorenzo d'Alessandro, and the early activity of Raphael in the Marche context. His scholarly output includes monographs, essays, and papers, including approximately thirty-three contributions published on his Academia.edu profile.

Among his best-known publications are volumes such as The School of Spoleto, Masterpieces of the 14th Century, Luca di Paolo and the Renaissance in the Marche, Lorenzo d'Alessandro, and Capriccio and Nature. He has curated major exhibitions, including Masterpieces of the 14th Century. Giotto's Workshop, Spoleto, and the Apennines, and has participated in public scholarly forums such as the Passaggi Festival (2023). Between 2014 and 2019 he also served as Mayor of Matelica, combining academic research with civic and administrative responsibility. His profile is therefore notable for the integration of specialist art-historical scholarship and sustained commitment to local cultural governance.

Douglas, Robert Langton

Davenham, 1864 – Fiesole, 1951

Robert Langton Douglas was an English art critic, dealer, lecturer, writer, and museum director, widely regarded as one of the most authoritative early twentieth-century voices on medieval and early Renaissance Italian painting, with a particular focus on Siena and the Sienese school. Born on March 1, 1864, in Davenham, England, he died on August 14, 1951, in Fiesole, Tuscany, after a life spent between Britain and Italy.

His career was unusually varied. After early ecclesiastical posts, he moved in 1900 to Australia as Professor of Modern History at the University of Adelaide, before returning to Italy in 1901. It was in this period that he deepened his knowledge of Tuscan painting and developed the research trajectory that made him a major specialist in Sienese art. In 1903, he was nominated three times for the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Douglas's most enduring scholarly contribution was his critical edition of Crowe and Cavalcaselle's History of Painting in Italy (1903 and subsequent volumes), which he expanded with updated research on Umbrian, Florentine, and Sienese painting from late antiquity to the sixteenth century. His principal publications include Fra Angelico (2nd ed., 1902), A History of Siena (1902), La Maioliche di Siena (1904), and the Catalogo Illustrato delle Opere d'Arte di Siena (1904). He also organized major exhibitions, notably the 1904 Exhibition of Sienese Art at the Burlington Fine Arts Club in London.

Across his studies of Duccio and related Duecento and Trecento masters, Douglas helped establish "primitive Italian" painting as a serious field in the Anglo-Saxon world, while also extending his interests to the High Renaissance through his monograph Leonardo da Vinci: His Life and His Pictures. His edition of Crowe and Cavalcaselle remains a fundamental bibliographic tool, and his work continues to be cited in scholarship on medieval and Renaissance Italian painting.

Donati, Piero

La Spezia, 1948

Italian art historian best known for his studies on religious art, medieval devotions, and the iconographic culture of Lunigiana and northern Tuscany. Born in La Spezia in 1948, he graduated in 1971 and continued his formation at the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore in Milan before entering the cultural-heritage administration of Liguria. He worked for many years as an official of the Soprintendenza per i Beni Artistici e Storici della Liguria, where his responsibilities brought together scholarly research, heritage protection, and the close study of the artistic patrimony of historic Lunigiana. Alongside his work on medieval and devotional subjects, he also devoted sustained attention to seventeenth-century Genoese painting, publishing on figures such as Domenico Fiasella, Cornelio de Wael, and Orazio De Ferrari. His curatorial activity included major exhibitions, among them the 1990 exhibition dedicated to Fiasella and exhibitions on the arts in Levanto between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries, which helped situate local artistic production within broader regional networks.

In his writings for journals including Bollettino d'Arte, Arte Lombarda, Arte Cristiana, and the Quaderni del Centro Studi Lunensi, Donati has consistently combined philological precision with historical-iconographic analysis. His more recent research has focused on subjects such as the Volto Santo of Bocca di Magra, Trecento images of the Maestà, and the circulation of sacred images between Tuscany, Liguria, and Lucca, offering reinterpretations of widely diffused devotional objects and clarifying the channels through which forms, cults, and workshop practices moved across the northern Tyrrhenian region. Through essays, catalogue entries, and exhibition scholarship, he has helped refine chronologies, reconstruct workshop trajectories, and illuminate the devotional dynamics that shaped both late medieval and post-medieval art in this territory. For these reasons, Donati remains a leading point of reference for scholars working on the religious imagery and artistic exchanges of Lunigiana, Liguria, and northern Tuscany.

Edgell, George Henry

1887 – 1954

George Henry Edgell was an American art historian and director of the Fogg Art Museum at Harvard University (1927–1946) whose systematic connoisseurial investigations of medieval Italian panel painting established rigorous scholarly standards in American academic circles. Through his meticulous comparative analysis of formal features and technical procedures in Giunta Pisano's works, Edgell demonstrated to American audiences the extraordinary formal sophistication of Duecento crucifix painting.

His institutional position at Harvard enabled him to integrate the study of medieval panel painting, particularly Giunta's innovations in representing human suffering and theological complexity, into the American university curriculum, establishing enduring traditions of medieval art historical scholarship in North America.

Federici, Vincenzo

Monterotondo, 12 August 1871 - Rome, 20 November 1953

Vincenzo Federici was the leading Italian paleographer and diplomatist of the first half of the twentieth century, a full professor at Sapienza University of Rome for over thirty years, director of the Institute of Paleography, and a prominent figure in Italian cultural life. He is credited with the scholarly organization of some of the most important collections of medieval Italian documents, and his work, ranging from Carolingian minuscule to papal chancelleries, from Benedictine monasteries to medieval epigraphy, has left a lasting mark on the discipline.

He was born in Monterotondo, in the Lazio region north of Rome, on August 12, 1871, to Gregorio Federici and Francesca Moreschi. He completed his high school studies in Rome and enrolled in the Faculty of Letters at Sapienza University, where he graduated in 1895 with a thesis on the thirteenth-century Florentine comic poet Rustico di Filippo, under the guidance of the great Romance philologist Ernesto Monaci. His intellectual partnership with Monaci, founder of the Gabinetto di Paleografia and the Archivio Paleografico Italiano, was decisive in steering the young Federici toward paleography and diplomatics, disciplines to which he would devote his entire life.

As early as 1899, Federici obtained his qualification to teach paleography and diplomatics, and in 1901 he began teaching at the University of Rome as an adjunct professor. In 1905 he was promoted to associate professor, and on July 1, 1910, he became full professor of paleography and diplomatics, a position he held until 1942, when, due to delays caused by the war in announcing the competition for his successor, Aldo Cerlini, he continued for one final year as an adjunct. In this capacity, he assumed responsibility for the Cabinet of Paleography, founded by Monaci in January 1887, which Federici developed in an extraordinary manner: the collection of facsimiles and reproductions of manuscripts reached such a level that it aroused admiration even at the Parisian Ecole des chartes. From 1935 to 1942 he served as director of the Institute of Paleography at the new Sapienza campus, and from 1918 to 1952 he oversaw the Archivio Paleografico Italiano, the publication of facsimiles originally envisioned by Monaci.

Among his most significant works stands out the monumental study I Monasteri di Subiaco (1904), a two-volume work that systematically investigated the libraries and archives of the Benedictine monasteries of Santa Scolastica and Sacro Speco. The second volume, dedicated to the library and the archive, catalogs the medieval parchments and provides a documentary repertoire of extraordinary breadth. In those pages, Federici also classified the Subiaco manuscripts into three paleographic categories: those in Roman minuscule, those in Romanesque minuscule, and those in Gothic script, making a significant contribution to the understanding of the scribal traditions of central Italy. It was precisely in these same pages that Federici transcribed the name of the fourteenth-century painter as "Consulus," reading it as the Latin form of "Console" in the third declension, a paleographic oversight corrected only in 1960 by G. Salvi, who demonstrated that "Consulus" belonged instead to the second declension and that the painter who signed "Conxolus" at the Sacro Speco and the "Benedictus Consuli" in the documents were the same person.

His most famous and widely consulted work is La scrittura delle cancellerie italiane dal secolo XII al XVII (1934), a collection of facsimiles intended for the paleography schools of the Italian State Archives, published under the patronage of Cesare Maria de Vecchi. The volume provides a systematic overview of the graphic evolution of documentary scripts in Italian chancelleries over the course of five centuries, and for decades remained the standard textbook for the teaching of documentary paleography in Italy.

The editorial masterpiece of his mature years was the critical edition of the Chronicon Vulturnense by the monk Giovanni, the medieval chronicle of the monastery of San Vincenzo al Volturno compiled in the eleventh century, published in four volumes between 1925 and 1940 in the Fonti per la storia d'Italia series of the Italian Historical Institute, and reprinted in 1995. The edition, which combines a paleographic study of the manuscript with a historical and diplomatical analysis of the sources, is considered a model of medieval documentary philology.

Federici's body of work is vast and multifaceted. He published the Regesto di Sant'Apollinare Nuovo (1907) and the Regesto della Chiesa di Ravenna (1911-1931), two fundamental registers for medieval ecclesiastical history. Together with Pietro Egidi and Francesco Tomassetti, he edited the Statuti della Provincia Romana (1910), which included the statutes of Subiaco, Viterbo, Anagni, and other municipalities in Lazio. He also worked on medieval epigraphy, chronology, and the origins of the Saracen colony of Lucera (1911-1914). His contribution to the question of the origin of Carolingian script (1908) placed him at the center of the international scholarly debate that engaged the greatest European paleographers of the time.

In the final years of his life, Federici was called upon to hold positions of high cultural responsibility: from 1943 he served as president of the Deputazione Romana di Storia Patria, and in 1947 he was elected a national member of the Accademia dei Lincei. Among his most distinguished students was Giorgio Cencetti, who would become one of the leading Italian diplomatists of the twentieth century and his ideal successor in teaching. Vincenzo Federici passed away in Rome on November 20, 1953, at the age of 82.

Fontana, Giovanni

Padua, c. 1395 - after 1454

Giovanni Fontana was an Italian scholar, physician, engineer, and humanist active in the early fifteenth century, especially in Padua and the Venetian world. He belongs to that generation of pre-Renaissance polymaths who combined scholastic learning with experimental curiosity, mechanical design, and a sustained interest in cryptography and hidden writing systems.

Born around 1395 in Padua, from a Venetian family (his father was Michele da Venezia), Fontana studied at the University of Padua and is documented there between 1417 and 1421; in 1421 he also served as Rettore delle Arti. His education stood at the intersection of late medieval Aristotelian culture and the emerging humanism of the Veneto. He later practiced medicine in multiple cities, while repeatedly noting how difficult it was to access books outside major intellectual centers.

His writings draw on Greek, Latin, and Arabic traditions and range across mechanics, optics, hydraulics, natural philosophy, and mnemonic theory. He is among the earliest Latin authors to refer to firearms (bombarde), indicating a concrete interest in contemporary military technology. Some scholars, including Lynn Thorndike, have argued that Fontana should be identified as the real author of the Liber de omnibus rebus naturalibus, traditionally linked to Pompilio Azalio, further strengthening his profile as a synthetic and inventive thinker.

Fontana is best known for the Bellicorum instrumentorum liber cum figuris et fictitiis litteris conscriptus, composed around 1420 in Venice. This extraordinary illustrated manuscript, now preserved in Munich (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cod. Icon. 242), contains roughly 140 pages of machine drawings accompanied by commentary in a cipher he devised. The designs include siege devices, hydraulic pumps and fountains, lifting mechanisms, drag-boats, locks, ratchets, gear systems related to horology, and a range of automata and pyrotechnic contrivances.

Among the most celebrated features of the codex is the earliest known representation of a magic-lantern-like projection device, along with theatrical and devotional special-effects machinery. This blend of practical engineering, military applications, visual illusion, and staged spectacle shows that Fontana did not draw strict boundaries between technology, natural magic, and performative invention.

His use of ciphered text reflects more than secrecy. It points to a broader reflection on signs, communication, and the encoding of knowledge through text and diagram. In this respect, Fontana's work anticipates later humanist and early modern debates on symbolic systems and technical representation. He also contributed to the study of the camera obscura and image formation, positioning him within the long prehistory of projection and optical experimentation.

Although long overshadowed by later figures such as Leonardo da Vinci, Fontana is now increasingly recognized as a crucial precursor. He predates Leonardo by decades and demonstrates that sophisticated design drawing, mechanical imagination, and experimental problem-solving were already strongly developed in early fifteenth-century Italy. For historians of technology and visual culture, he stands as a key bridge between late medieval scholastic science and the drawing-based engineering culture of the early Renaissance.

Garrison, Edward B.

Chicago, 1900 – London, 1981

American art historian who made foundational contributions to the study of medieval Italian painting, particularly through his meticulous documentation of Romanesque panel paintings and twelfth-century manuscripts from central Italy. Born in Chicago in 1900, Garrison initially pursued a business career before pivoting in 1945 to art history, earning an MA from New York University's Institute of Fine Arts. He then immersed himself in Italy, collaborating with Richard Offner on the Corpus of Florentine Painting (Volume V, 1943–1945), which honed his expertise in Duecento and Trecento primitives. This transition reflected a broader postwar scholarly migration to Italy's artistic heartland, where Garrison's independent research thrived without institutional ties.

His breakthrough came with Italian Romanesque Panel Painting: An Illustrated Index (1949), a pioneering catalogue that systematically inventoried surviving panels from the eleventh and twelfth centuries, providing scholars with their first comprehensive visual reference for styles bridging Byzantine and proto-Renaissance forms. Between 1953 and 1962 he issued four volumes of Studies in the History of Medieval Italian Painting, featuring photographs of Tuscan and Papal States miniatures, wall paintings, and initials — unprecedented in scope and enabling precise stylistic comparisons across regions including Umbria and Latium. Later compilations, such as Early Italian Painting: Selected Studies (1984, posthumous, 2 vols.), consolidated his essays on panels, frescoes, and manuscripts, emphasising iconographic and formal evolutions. His 1953 identification of connections between the Sarzana Cross and the Passionary P+ manuscript initiated the scholarly programme of reconstructing the broader artistic activity of Maestro Guglielmo.

Travelling extensively in Italy, Garrison amassed a vast photographic archive of medieval artworks, donated in 1962 to the Courtauld Institute of Art in London, where he served as honorary curator of the Garrison Collection. This resource — now undergoing digitisation as part of the Conway Library project — captured details often overlooked in earlier studies, supporting attributions of anonymous masters and illuminating workshop practices in Romanesque illumination. His method integrated visual analysis with historical context, influencing successors such as Miklós Boskovits in their pursuit of chronological clarity for Tuscany's early painting schools. An honorary member of the Courtauld from 1979, Garrison died in London on October 16, 1981; his indices remain an indispensable reference for any researcher working on Italian Romanesque and early Duecento painting.

Ghiberti, Lorenzo

Florence, c. 1378 – Florence, 1455

Florentine goldsmith, sculptor, and writer whose bronze doors for the Florence Baptistery marked a pivotal transition from Gothic to Renaissance art, blending classical naturalism with innovative perspective and narrative depth. Born around 1378 as Lorenzo di Cione di Ser Bonaccorso Ghiberti, he trained as a goldsmith in his father's workshop and under Bartoluccio di Michele, mastering techniques in modelling and chasing that later defined his sculptural reliefs. In 1401, at the age of twenty-three, he won the prestigious competition sponsored by the Arte di Calimala for the Baptistery's north doors, outshining rivals including Filippo Brunelleschi with his Sacrifice of Isaac panel — its balanced composition, nude figure inspired by classical antiquity, and subtle depth showcasing emerging humanist ideals within late Gothic conventions. This victory secured the commission for twenty-eight New Testament panels completed between 1403 and 1424.

His masterpiece, the east doors (1425–1452) — dubbed the "Gates of Paradise" by Michelangelo — features ten large panels from Genesis executed in schiacciato (flattened) relief with multi-plane compositions simulating atmospheric perspective and continuous narrative, drawing on Donatello's influence and antique sarcophagi. Surrounding frames with prophets and sibyls integrated architecture and sculpture, while free-standing figures such as the St. John the Baptist (1414) for Orsanmichele demonstrate the breadth of his workshop's output, which trained talents including Donatello, Masolino, and Paolo Uccello. These gilded bronze works embodied Florence's mercantile patronage, harmonising biblical storytelling with mathematical precision in foreshortening and the handling of light.

In the Commentarii (c. 1450) — the first autobiography by an artist — Ghiberti reflected on art's evolution from Cimabue to his own era, praising ancient Roman sculpture and advocating disegno as foundational. The Commentarii preserve important biographical and critical notices on Trecento masters, including Giotto, Cimabue, and the Berlinghieri family, that corroborate, amplify, or occasionally contradict the later accounts compiled by Vasari, and they remain a primary source for understanding how the art of the Duecento and Trecento was perceived by an informed witness working one generation after the style's apogee. Running a prolific bottega until his death on December 1, 1455, Ghiberti also served on the committee for Brunelleschi's dome; his sons Vittorio and Lorenzo the Younger continued the family legacy. His fusion of goldsmith finesse with monumental bronze bridged the International Gothic and the full Renaissance, establishing narrative sculpture as a rhetorical art form.

Giorgi, Silvia

active 21st century

Silvia Giorgi is an Italian art historian specializing in medieval panel painting, with particular scholarly focus on the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Her research concentrates on the study of Italian panel painting across multiple geographical centers — Bologna, Siena, Spoleto — and on the broader artistic transitions of the Romanesque-Gothic period. She is recognized as a specialist in attributional analysis and stylistic chronology during this formative era of Italian art.

Giorgi's scholarly work has been cited in major publications on thirteenth-century Sienese painting, particularly in studies devoted to the work of Guido da Siena. She contributed to the exhibition catalog Duccio: Alle origini della pittura senese, a comprehensive volume that examines the development of Sienese painting from its earliest manifestations. Through this and related publications, she has established herself as a reference point in the literature on Duecento Italian art and the emergence of regional stylistic identities in central Italy.

Giorgi's most significant contribution to medieval art history is her entry on Rainaldetto di Ranuccio, written for the General Catalog of the National Art Gallery of Bologna. In this work, she advances a major chronological hypothesis through rigorous comparative analysis. Giorgi argues that the Fabriano Cross predates the documented Bolognese cross — which is dated to April 1265 — by approximately five years, placing it around 1260. This dating represents a revival and refinement of a hypothesis originally formulated by the American scholar Edward B. Garrison in 1949.

Her methodology exemplifies high-level Italian medieval art-historical scholarship, grounded in precise formal analysis and close visual comparison. The dating of the Fabriano Cross rests on detailed stylistic examination of Christ's abdomen and a cross-reading of the inscriptions appearing on both crosses, demonstrating the kind of disciplined connoisseurial work that has long characterized Italian studies in panel painting. Through arguments of this type — combining technical observation with documentary evidence and comparative methodology — Giorgi contributes to the ongoing refinement of chronologies for early Italian panel painting and helps illuminate the networks of artistic exchange between regional centers during the critical period of the Duecento.

Grabar, André

Kiev, 1896 – Paris, 1990

Pioneering French art historian of Russian origin, renowned for his foundational studies on Byzantine and early Christian art. Born on July 26, 1896, in Kiev — then part of the Russian Empire — he fled the Bolshevik Revolution in 1917 and eventually settled in Paris, where he immersed himself in the city's scholarly circles. He studied at the University of Paris (Sorbonne), earning his doctorate in 1931 with a dissertation on early Christian iconography that established him as a leading authority in the field. He taught at the École des Hautes Études and later at the Collège de France from 1946 until his retirement, shaping generations of scholars through a rigorous methodology that blended formal analysis, liturgical context, and cultural history. His tenure at the Bibliothèque Byzantine in Paris further amplified his influence, as he curated resources that became essential for Byzantine studies worldwide.

His seminal works — including L'empereur dans l'art byzantin (1936) and The Beginnings of Christian Art, 200–395 (1967) — redefined the field by arguing that early Christian imagery drew from imperial Roman symbolism to assert theological authority, famously interpreting the haloed emperor as a precursor to the Christ Pantocrator icon. He also explored monastic art and relic cults, linking them to broader socio-political shifts in the Christian East. Later studies on L'art paléochrétien and Byzantium emphasised art's role in imperial ideology and popular devotion, challenging purely aesthetic readings. His interdisciplinary approach — integrating archaeology, texts, and theology — influenced subsequent scholars and remains vital for understanding the transitions from late antiquity to the Romanesque era. He died on October 2, 1990, in Paris.

Grube, Ernst J.

Kufstein, 9 May 1932 - London, 12 June 2011

Ernst J. Grube was one of the foremost historians of Islamic art in the twentieth century and is often regarded as one of the last major representatives of rigorous German-trained art-historical scholarship. He is especially known as the first curator appointed to a dedicated Islamic art department, at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Raised and educated in Berlin, he attended the Schiller-Gymnasium and received his doctorate from the Free University of Berlin in 1955. In the same year he entered the Art Library of the Berlin State Museums as an assistant and was later invited into the Islamic Department by Ernst Kuhnel, one of the founders of modern Islamic art history. In 1958 he received a research grant at the Metropolitan Museum, joining its Department of Near Eastern Art in 1959.

In 1962, Grube became Curator of the newly established Department of Islamic Art at the Met, and he taught Islamic art concurrently as an Adjunct Professor at Columbia University. His work at the Met helped shape one of the world's most important Islamic art collections, and his studies of Islamic pottery produced there remain standard references.

After leaving the Met in 1969 and teaching at Hunter College, he moved to Italy in 1972, where he built the next phase of his career. He taught at the University of Padua and then, from 1973, at the Oriental Institute in Naples, before being appointed in 1977 to the Chair of Islamic Art History at Ca' Foscari University of Venice, retiring in 1988. During these years he also founded the journal Islamic Art and served as co-editor with his wife, Eleanor Sims.

His scholarly output was substantial: twelve authored or co-authored books, 117 articles, and 32 reviews. Major works include Muslim Miniature Painting from the XIII to the XIX Century, The Classical Style in Islamic Painting, Cobalt and Lustre: The First Centuries of Islamic Pottery, and The World of Islam (translated into multiple languages). Portable arts, especially ceramics, miniature painting, and Fatimid rock crystal, remained central to his research throughout his career.

For medieval art and manuscript studies, Grube's work on Islamic miniature painting remains a key comparative framework. He highlighted how manuscript traditions developing from the thirteenth century onward, including the Baghdad school, formed sophisticated narrative and figural systems in dialogue with Byzantine and Italian visual cultures through trade, diplomacy, and the Sicilian context.

Gudiol i Ricart, Josep

Vic, 1904 – Barcelona, 1985

Catalan art historian who specialised in Catalan Romanesque and Gothic painting and the broader field of Spanish medieval art, with a scholarly focus directly relevant to the twelfth through fourteenth centuries. Born Josep Maria Gudiol i Ricart in 1904 in Vic, Catalonia, he came from a family of art scholars: his uncle Josep Gudiol i Cunill was among the founders of Catalan art historiography. His work was distinguished by a systematic approach to cataloguing and stylistic analysis. During the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), he served as the Generalitat's commissioner for the preservation of artistic heritage, actively dedicating himself to the rescue of works of art — including the Romanesque frescoes of the Monastery of Santa Maria de Sixena in Aragon — documenting and removing wall panels to protect them from destruction. After the war he emigrated to the United States, where he taught as a professor, helping to disseminate knowledge of Catalan and Spanish art beyond European borders.

Gudiol Ricart directed the Institut Amatller d'Art Hispànic in Barcelona, a key centre for the study of Hispanic art, where he promoted restorations and research on medieval frescoes and sculptures. His most monumental contribution is the series Ars Hispaniae, a forty-volume encyclopaedia on the history of Spanish art, for which he edited the sections dedicated to the Middle Ages. Key publications within this programme include Las Pinturas Murales Románicas de Cataluña (1948, with José Pijoán), which analyses Catalan Romanesque frescoes with a focus on techniques, iconography, and attributions, and Arquitectura y Escultura Románicas (1948, with Juan Antonio Gaya Nuño), essential for understanding the evolution of sculpture in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. These works employ connoisseurship methods to distinguish local schools, offering a detailed picture of Romanesque painting traditions that preceded Catalan Gothic.

Gudiol died in 1985 in Barcelona, leaving a lasting legacy in the historiography of medieval Iberian art often compared to that of Chandler Rathfon Post for its coverage of Spanish painting. His daughter, Montserrat Gudiol i Corominas (1933–2015), was a painter trained in his studio for the restoration of medieval paintings, continuing the family's commitment to art; her research on Romanesque manuscripts and frescoes remains a reference for scholars of medieval iconography.

Hermanin de Reichenfeld, Federico

Bari, 16 September 1868 – Rome, 29 June 1953

Federico Hermanin de Reichenfeld was born in Bari on September 16, 1868, to Federico Leopoldo, a general staff officer and honorary aide-de-camp to Umberto I, and Camilla Marstaller, both of Austro-German origin and Protestant faith. Although the family moved frequently due to his father's duties, their connection to the Germanic community in Italy remained strong, further strengthened by his marriage to Margherita Hausmann, daughter of the Danish consul in Bari. After settling in Rome, the city became the absolute center of his intellectual and professional life, decisively shaping his identity as a scholar.

In Rome, he earned his degree in 1895 with a thesis in Romance philology on the reception of the Tristan poem in Italy, under the guidance of E. Monaci, holder of the chair of Neo-Latin languages and literature. It was only beginning in 1896 that he turned toward art history, drawing closer to Adolfo Venturi, who that year had just secured the establishment of the first Italian chair of art history at the University of Rome, along with an affiliated advanced training school for future museum curators. His solid philological and positivist training proved decisive: Hermanin realized that the rigor of analyzing written sources was perfectly applicable to the critical study of works of art, a method that would characterize his entire scholarly output.

The most celebrated moment of Hermanin's scholarly career was the discovery, in October 1900, of the great thirteenth-century fresco of the Last Judgment in the Basilica of Santa Cecilia in Trastevere, which he immediately attributed to the Roman painter Pietro Cavallini. This was a discovery of exceptional significance, destined to reignite critical attention on Roman medieval art and to fuel the historiographical debate for decades regarding the relationship between Cavallini and Giotto in the birth of modern Italian painting. Hermanin returned to the subject several times: in 1900 with a report on the discovery in the Archivio della Società romana di storia patria, in 1901 with a critical analysis in the journal L'Arte, and finally in 1902 with the seminal monograph Gli affreschi di Pietro Cavallini a Santa Cecilia in Trastevere, published in the Gallerie nazionali italiane.

In 1924, with the article Il maestro di Giotto, Hermanin explicitly stated his position: Cavallini preceded and influenced Giotto, being a key figure for understanding the Roman roots of the great pictorial turning point of the thirteenth century. This thesis, later discussed and reworked by successive generations of art historians, remains one of the most original and fruitful contributions to the entire field of Italian medieval historiography.

Having joined the Fine Arts administration in 1898, Hermanin worked for years at the National Print Cabinet in Palazzo Corsini, curating the catalog of engravings featuring Roman views and promoting a rich program of public exhibitions on graphic art. In 1908, he was appointed director of the National Gallery of Ancient Art at Palazzo Corsini, where he distinguished himself through an acquisition policy focused on the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, going against the grain of his mentor Venturi, who favored the Renaissance. It was he who secured the famous Narcissus by Caravaggio as a gift for the Gallery in 1914, organizing a small exhibition of Caravaggio-inspired paintings on that occasion.

On December 30, 1913, he was appointed Superintendent of the Galleries and Museums of Lazio and Abruzzo, a position he held until his retirement in 1938, although he continued to receive official assignments until 1945. In this capacity, he actively participated in the recovery of artworks damaged by the 1915 Marsica earthquake, saving a priceless heritage from dispersion and transferring many works to Rome.

The most ambitious museographic project of Hermanin's entire career was the creation of the Museum of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance at Palazzo Venezia, which became the focus of his professional life for over twenty years. As early as 1906, he had advocated for the establishment of a National Historical Museum at Castel Sant'Angelo, envisioning a space that would bring together paintings, sculptures, and the so-called "minor" arts — ceramics, ivories, textiles, small bronzes — to evoke the harmonious unity of the arts across every era, in contrast to the cold, positivist museum organized by schools and periods. Work began in 1916, and Hermanin gradually arranged over thirty rooms, completing the project in 1936, although the museum was never regularly opened to the public due to interference from the fascist regime, which designated Palazzo Venezia as the government's official residence.

In 1904, on a ministerial commission, Hermanin studied the paintings of the monasteries of Subiaco, producing a fundamental contribution in the collective volume The Monasteries of Subiaco (Rome, 1904), which remained for a long time the standard work for that geographical and artistic area. His magnum opus, Art in Rome from the 8th to the 14th Century, was conceived in the 1930s on commission from the National Institute of Roman Studies, but could only be published in 1945, symbolically crowning a lifetime of research. This work, still consulted today as a milestone in historiography, provides a systematic reconstruction of Roman artistic production from the early Middle Ages to the end of the fourteenth century, with a perspective that highlights Rome as the driving force behind Italian medieval painting.

Hermanin was also an active contributor to Venturi's journal L'Arte from 1898 to 1908, editing bibliographic reviews of foreign journals and publishing original research, including studies on Vatican miniatures. In 1923, he founded the journal Roma, later transferred to the National Institute of Roman Studies, and published a monograph on G. B. Piranesi. His popular works include La Farnesina (1927), Life in Old Italian Prints (1928), The Myth of Giorgione (1933), and The Borgia Apartment in the Vatican (1934), while his two-volume work on Italian artists in Germany was of greater scholarly rigor. He died in Rome on June 29, 1953, leaving a lasting mark on both the historiography of medieval Italian art and the history of Roman museum institutions.

J

Johns, Jeremy

active late 20th-21st century

Jeremy Johns is Professor of the Art and Archaeology of the Islamic Mediterranean at the University of Oxford, based at the Khalili Research Centre for the Art and Material Culture of the Middle East (3 St John Street, Oxford). He is also a Fellow of Wolfson College. Since 2004 he has served as the first Director of the Khalili Research Centre, and in 2006 he was appointed to his full professorship.

Johns began doctoral research at Balliol College, Oxford, on the Muslims of Norman Sicily, with support from a Leverhulme Trust Study Abroad Scholarship. He completed his doctorate in 1983, after election to a Stipendiary Junior Research Fellowship at Wolfson College in 1981. In 1984 he was appointed New Blood Lecturer in Early Islamic Archaeology at the University of Newcastle upon Tyne, and in 1990 returned to Oxford as University Lecturer in Islamic Archaeology. This trajectory shaped his profile as a scholar bridging Islamic studies and medieval Latin history.

Johns's work is centrally concerned with relations between Muslim and Christian societies in the medieval Mediterranean as expressed through material and visual culture. His method emphasizes objects, buildings, documents, and images rather than purely theological or literary polemic, making coexistence and conflict historically legible through concrete evidence.

His research has two principal axes. The first addresses archaeological transformation from Late Antiquity to early Islam in the Levant. The second, for which he is especially renowned, concerns the archaeology, history, and art history of Sicily under Islamic and Norman rule, from the Muslim conquest of the ninth century to the destruction of Sicily's Islamic communities under Frederick II in the thirteenth.

Among his major ongoing initiatives is the project Documenting Multiculturalism, which studies the administrative and legal records of Norman and Hohenstaufen Sicily to reconstruct relations among Arabic-speaking Muslims and Jews, Greek-speaking Christians, and Latin Christians. This documentary work is fundamental for understanding how multilingual governance operated in the courts of Roger II and William II, where Arabic functioned institutionally alongside Greek and Latin.

His landmark monograph Arabic Administration in Norman Sicily: The Royal Diwan (Cambridge University Press, 2002) remains the foundational study of the Arabic bureaucratic structures maintained by the Norman kings after conquest. He has also published influential studies on the Cappella Palatina in Palermo, including work on its mosaics and courtly material culture, clarifying how visual programs and documentary practice articulate a single political ideology of rulership.

Johns occupies a distinctive position in medieval scholarship because he works across Arabic documentary sources, Byzantine and Islamic art history, Latin narrative texts, and archaeological evidence with equal authority. Against older historiographical separations, he reads Norman Sicily as an integrated Mediterranean formation: not merely a Latin kingdom with exotic ornament, nor an Islamic society under simple occupation, but a complex and genuinely hybrid political civilization that can only be understood through all of its linguistic and artistic traditions together.

K

Kaftal, George

1897 - 1987

George Kaftal was one of the most original and prolific iconographers of the twentieth century, the author of a monumental four-volume series on the iconography of saints in Italian painting that remains, more than seventy years after its first publication, an indispensable reference for the study of medieval and Renaissance art. His work transformed sacred iconography from an auxiliary field into an autonomous scholarly discipline by providing, for the first time, a systematic and comparative instrument of analysis.

Born in 1897, Kaftal conducted most of his studies and research career in England, where he lived for many years in Oxford. His formation unfolded in an intellectual milieu deeply shaped by the Viennese art-historical tradition (the Wiener Schule) and by the methodological innovations of Aby Warburg and the Warburg circle, whose iconological studies were reshaping approaches to medieval and Renaissance painting in the early twentieth century. Writing almost entirely in English, unusual in a field then dominated by German and Italian scholarship, he positioned his work within an international scholarly horizon.

Kaftal's scholarly debut was St. Francis in Italian Painting (London, 1950), where he mapped representations of the saint of Assisi across regional Italian schools with a new and rigorous method. He proceeded through iconographic cataloging based on each saint's visual attributes, classified variants of individual hagiographic episodes, and traced their geographical and chronological distribution. This approach, closer in structure to textual philology than to traditional impressionistic criticism, anticipated the major project that would occupy the rest of his career.

Kaftal's life's work is the four-volume series Saints in Italian Art: Iconography of the Saints in Italian Painting from Its Beginnings to the Early 16th Century, first published by Sansoni in Florence and later by Le Lettere across more than three decades. The first volume, Iconography of the Saints in Tuscan Painting (Florence, Sansoni, 1952), is the most extensive: 1,274 pages and 1,185 illustrations across fifty plates, cataloging approximately 700 saints and blessed figures in Tuscan painting from its origins to the early sixteenth century.

The second volume, Iconography of the Saints in Central and South Italian Schools of Painting (Florence, Sansoni, 1965), expanded the survey to central and southern Italy, including the Roman, Umbrian, Laziale, and southern schools; in this volume Kaftal accepted G. Salvi's thesis on the identity of the painter Conxolus at the Sacro Speco in Subiaco. The third and fourth volumes, dedicated respectively to northeastern Italy (1978) and northwestern Italy (1985), were completed in collaboration with Fabio Bisogni of the University of Siena. The fourth volume was published posthumously in 1986, one year before Kaftal's death.

The architecture of the series is that of an encyclopedic dictionary of iconographic attributes. Each entry begins with a saint's canonical signs, such as the palm of martyrdom, book, lamb, cross, and locally specific emblems, then catalogues each identified representation with details on location, school, and dating. This framework made it possible to trace both the geographical spread and chronological evolution of iconographic types, while also recovering minor or local saints who appear only in specific regional traditions.

Kaftal's influence on the historiography of Italian medieval and Renaissance art has been enduring and pervasive. His volumes are systematically cited in museum catalogues, including those of the National Gallery in London, the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore, and the Uffizi, as well as in monographs on Italian artists, auction catalogues, and specialist studies in sacred iconography. Their continued authority, unmatched in scope decades after publication, testifies to the extraordinary documentary depth of Kaftal's achievement.

Kapitaikin, Lev Arie

St. Petersburg, active 21st century

Lev Arie Kapitaikin is a contemporary scholar of Islamic and medieval art history, currently affiliated with the Department of Art History at Tel Aviv University as a lecturer and researcher. He is of great interest to those specializing in medieval Mediterranean art, as his work focuses specifically on the points of contact between Islamic, Christian, and Byzantine cultures in the Mediterranean during the twelfth century.

Born in St. Petersburg, Russia, Kapitaikin completed his university studies in Israel: he earned a bachelor's degree in Arabic Literature and Classical Archaeology from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and a master's degree in Art History from Tel Aviv University. He then completed his Ph.D. at the University of Oxford in 2011, under the supervision of Jeremy Johns, with a dissertation on the ceiling paintings of the Cappella Palatina in Palermo.

The focus of Kapitaikin's research is twelfth-century Norman Sicily, and in particular the extraordinary cultural and artistic complexity that characterizes the monuments commissioned by Roger II. His doctoral dissertation on the ceiling paintings of the Cappella Palatina in Palermo, commissioned by Roger II around 1143, constitutes a seminal study. Through meticulous stylistic and iconographic analysis, Kapitaikin argues that the ceiling painters came primarily from Fatimid Egypt, and that princely Islamic iconography was deliberately interwoven with Christian themes drawn from Romanesque, middle Byzantine, and Coptic models. Far from being an Eastern element foreign to the Christian chapel, the decorative program reflects a conscious strategy of manipulating and recontextualizing Islamic images within a triumphant Christian framework.

In 2020, Kapitaikin published the volume Designing Norman Sicily: Material Culture and Society (Boydell Studies in Medieval Art and Architecture, Woodbridge), which expands his reflection on the material culture of Norman Sicily from an interdisciplinary perspective. In previous articles, he had already explored the links between Norman Sicily and al-Andalus in a 2013 essay titled "The Daughter of al-Andalus": Interrelations between Norman Sicily and the Muslim West, and contributed to the volume A Companion to Islamic Art and Architecture (Wiley, 2017) with a chapter titled Sicily and the Staging of Multiculturalism.

A lesser-known but fascinating aspect of his research concerns depictions of dance and music on the ceilings of the Cappella Palatina. In a 2019 article published in the journal Early Music, Kapitaikin analyzed the dancing figures painted in Palermo, relating them to Islamic iconography of dance in Fatimid Egypt, Seljuk Iran, and medieval Spain, and interpreting their Christian recontextualization through the biblical theme of David's dancers.

Kapitaikin's work fits squarely within the contemporary debate on the intercultural nature of medieval Mediterranean art, offering sophisticated analytical tools for understanding how patrons, artists, and iconographies circulated across the religious and political boundaries of the twelfth century. For scholars interested in illuminated manuscripts and medieval art from the Mediterranean basin, his research on the Cappella Palatina serves as an exemplary case of how art history can decode visual objects as documents of the encounter between civilizations.

Kitzinger, Ernst

27 December 1912 - 22 January 2003

Ernst Kitzinger was one of the most influential art historians of the twentieth century, specializing in late antique, early Christian, Byzantine, and early medieval art. His work made him a central point of reference for the study of the transition from late antique to medieval visual culture.

Kitzinger was born in Munich, entered the University of Munich in 1931, and studied art history principally under Wilhelm Pinder, one of the leading German art historians of the period. The rise of National Socialism forced him, like many Jewish intellectuals, to emigrate. After a period in England, where he worked at the British Museum, he traveled in 1941 to Washington, D.C., and became a Junior Fellow at Dumbarton Oaks, the Harvard-affiliated research library that became his long-term intellectual base.

At Dumbarton Oaks, Kitzinger rose rapidly, becoming Director of Studies in 1955 and Professor of Byzantine Art and Archaeology in 1956. He is widely credited with establishing Dumbarton Oaks as the world's foremost institution for Byzantine studies, transforming it from a private library and garden into an internationally recognized center for Byzantine and medieval scholarship.

In 1967, after a period at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, he moved permanently to Harvard as Arthur Kingsley Porter University Professor, a chair he held until retirement in 1979. At Harvard he supervised eighteen doctoral dissertations, and his students included major later figures in medieval art history such as Hans Belting, Irving Lavin, and Lawrence Nees.

His scholarly range extended from the decline of classical antiquity through the rise of Byzantium to Giotto. His most theoretically ambitious book, Byzantine Art in the Making (1977), based on his Slade Lectures at Cambridge (1974-75), articulated his lifelong conviction that stylistic analysis carries an interpretive authority equal to iconographic or textual history.

He also edited and contributed to important essay collections, including The Art of Byzantium and the Medieval West (1976) and the two-volume Studies in Late Antique, Byzantine and Medieval Western Art (2002-2004), works that further consolidated his international influence across Byzantine and medieval art historiography.

Lampugnani, Elena

Elena Lampugnani is a contemporary Italian art historian specializing in early medieval Lombard painting and ecclesiastical patronage, whose work on the Galliano frescoes has made the single most important iconographic discovery of recent decades regarding this cycle. Her principal contribution to the study of the Maestro di Galliano is the essay La "commendatio animae" di Ariberto nelle pitture absidali di San Vincenzo a Galliano (1007), published in 2006 in the collective volume Pittura murale del Medioevo lombardo: Ricerche iconografiche (secoli XI–XIII), edited by Paolo Piva (Jaca Book, 2006), a volume that brought together the most recent Italian scholarship on the major mural painting cycles of the provinces of Como, Lecco, and Bergamo between the year 1000 and the thirteenth century. In this essay, Lampugnani proposed for the first time a comprehensive reading of the entire apse program at Galliano as a commendatio animae — that is, a liturgical prayer of commendation of the soul to God, spoken at the moment of death and translated here into a painted visual program — a theological framework that unifies all the apparently disparate iconographic elements of the apse into a single coherent argument about intercession, death, and resurrection. The commendatio animae interpretation, now widely accepted in the scholarly literature, explains the selection of figures — Elijah and Enoch (who avoided death), the prophets prostrating before the Theophany, the martyred Vincent (who gained eternal life through his passio), the intercessor Adeodato, and finally the praying donor Ariberto — as the successive stages of a liturgical and eschatological narrative culminating in Ariberto's own petition for divine mercy at the moment of his anticipated death. Lampugnani's essay thus belongs to the tradition of iconographic theology in medieval art studies — a method that decodes painted programs not merely as aesthetic ensembles but as precise translations of theological and liturgical texts into visual form — and it represents a significant methodological advance over both Ansaldi's documentary approach and de Francovich's primarily stylistic analysis. She also authored the essay Ariberto, custos a Galliano e arcivescovo a Milano: la personalità storica di un committente artistico attraverso i suoi "ritratti" (Monduzzi Editoriale), in which she analysed the four known portraits of Ariberto — beginning with the Galliano apse fresco and extending through his later representations in manuscript illuminations and goldsmiths' works — as a coherent self-representational strategy through which Ariberto constructed and projected his public identity as a patron of exceptional cultural ambition. Her work has been incorporated and cited by subsequent scholars — including the authors of the entries in the Treccani Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale and of the JSTOR article on the nave paintings — confirming her position as one of the leading current authorities on the patronage and iconographic programs of the early eleventh-century Ambrosian world.

Lo Vullo, Gaetano

active first half of the twentieth century; documented into the 1950s

Gaetano Lo Vullo was one of the most important and influential painting restorers in twentieth-century Italy, and is closely associated with the golden age of postwar Florentine restoration. Born into a noble Sicilian family from Licata, he developed a rigorously practical vocation that distinguished him within a field often divided between scholarly theory and workshop practice. Trained in Florentine restoration ateliers, he absorbed the artisanal discipline that made Florence a major international center of painting conservation, and built a methodology grounded in philological respect for the original work combined with technically necessary intervention.

Lo Vullo worked within the circle directed by Superintendent Ugo Procacci, a pivotal figure in postwar Italian restoration culture. Alongside Augusto Vermehren and Vittorio Granchi, he formed part of the historic core of restorers active at the Uffizi, where he participated in major campaigns for the preservation of some of Italy's most significant pictorial heritage. In this environment, where technical operations were increasingly linked to historical research on artistic materials and processes, Lo Vullo became one of the most representative practitioners of a restoration model that united craft knowledge and emerging scientific standards.

Among the best-documented interventions of his career is the restoration in the 1950s of Beato Angelico's panel of the Last Judgment in the Museum of San Marco, Florence, prepared in the context of the 1955 commemorations for the fifth centenary of the artist's death. Lo Vullo cleaned the pictorial surface, removing yellowed varnishes and altered retouchings, but deliberately preserved the underlying patina because the available means at the time made deeper removal too risky. This decision has often been cited as exemplary of his methodological balance: intervention where necessary, restraint where material integrity required caution.

Contemporaries and later historians valued Lo Vullo for what was described as a pragmatic eclecticism between conservation and integration. Rather than applying a rigidly conservative or interventionist doctrine, he developed case-by-case solutions calibrated to each object's condition and historical complexity. This approach made him an important point of reference for subsequent generations, and later theoretical developments in Florence — including those associated with the International University of Art (founded 1969-70) and figures such as Umberto Baldini — have often been traced back to the practical culture of the Uffizi restoration team of which he was a leading member.

Lo Vullo was also fully integrated into the wider intellectual life of postwar Florence. A personal friend of Ugo Procacci and a supporter of Carlo Ludovico Ragghianti since the Liberation, he moved within anti-fascist and cultural networks that connected restoration, criticism, and exhibition culture. He served for many years as Secretary General of La Strozzina at Palazzo Strozzi and, in that role, was frequently delegated by the Superintendency to supervise the conservation of works lent to major exhibitions. In 2008, the publication of an English-language monograph by Rossella Beatrice Batassa confirmed the international scholarly recognition of his legacy as a central figure in twentieth-century Italian restoration practice.

Lollini, Fabrizio

Bologna, 3 February 1964

Fabrizio Lollini was born in Bologna on 3 February 1964, and his entire scholarly career has unfolded within the same city — a remarkable continuity that reflects both deep institutional loyalty and the extraordinary richness of Bologna as a centre of medieval artistic production. He completed his Laurea in Lettere classiche at the Università di Bologna, a foundation in classical philology and literature that informs the rigour and textual sensitivity of all his subsequent art-historical work. He then obtained a Diploma di Specializzazione in Storia dell'Arte medievale e moderna at the same institution, before going on to a doctorate. His training was thus doubly rooted: in the classical tradition and in the specialist art-historical methodology of the Bolognese school.

Lollini is currently Professore Associato in the sector ARTE-01/A (Storia dell'Arte Medievale) in the Dipartimento delle Arti of the Università di Bologna, one of Italy's oldest and most prestigious universities. He teaches three courses: Storia dell'arte medievale, Storia della miniatura, and Alimentazione e arti visive — the last of which reflects an original and growing area of his interests, namely the representation of food and nourishment in the visual culture of the Middle Ages. He has organised numerous international conferences and delivered lectures at foreign universities, and sits on the editorial boards of several scientific journals including La Rivista di Engramma. He has also been a member of the scientific committees of many exhibitions in Italy and abroad.

The Storia della miniatura course Lollini teaches reflects one of his deepest scholarly commitments: the study of illuminated manuscripts as primary historical documents. His investigation of the so-called Codice Parigi-Imola — a Dantesque manuscript produced at the Visconti court, split between Paris and Imola — has attracted considerable attention and was the subject of a dedicated public lecture that he delivered online in February 2021. In 2007 he contributed to the scholarly study of illuminated manuscripts preserved in Imola, including a 1506 manuscript biography of Cardinal Albornoz transcribed and illuminated by Girolamo Pagliarolo, demonstrating his command of the late medieval and humanist manuscript traditions of Emilia-Romagna. His work on Bolognese illumination connects directly to the long tradition of manuscript production for which Bologna was one of the great centres of medieval Europe, supplying the university and the mendicant orders with legal, theological, and liturgical codices from the twelfth century onward.

Beyond manuscripts, Lollini has written extensively on medieval sculpture in Bologna. His essay on Niccolò dell'Arca's Compianto in Santa Maria della Vita — one of the most celebrated and emotionally overwhelming groups of terracotta sculpture in Italian art — examines the devotional, institutional, and figurative sources that converged in that extraordinary work. He traces the complex visual culture of the sculptor, who came from Apulia but worked exclusively in Bologna, demonstrating how Niccolò drew on a European range of sources while remaining firmly embedded in the Bolognese artistic context. This interest in the intersection between imported stylistic currents and local tradition is a constant thread throughout Lollini's scholarship.

Lollini has been curator and scientific director of numerous exhibitions, making him one of the most publicly active medieval art historians of his generation in Italy. In July 2024 he gave a lecture entitled "Sentire" l'arte medievale: gli oggetti, il pubblico e i suoi sensi in Imola, a title that encapsulates an important dimension of his recent research: the sensory and affective dimensions of the medieval encounter with artistic objects. He has also lectured at the Museo della Città di Livorno and at the Fondazione Collegio San Carlo di Modena, bringing medieval art history to audiences well beyond the university. His collaboration with the publisher specialising in manuscript facsimiles further reflects his commitment to making medieval visual culture physically accessible to readers and institutions.

Lollini's declared research interests, as listed on his Academia.edu profile, include cinema and the visual arts, Late Byzantine history, and food studies alongside his core medieval art-historical work. This breadth is unusual among Italian medievalists and signals a scholar who thinks across disciplinary boundaries — connecting the medieval image to questions of sensory perception, material culture, and the long history of visual representation. His course on Alimentazione e arti visive is, to the best of current knowledge, one of the few such courses offered at an Italian university, reflecting his pioneering engagement with the anthropology of the image.

Longhi, Roberto

Alba, 28 December 1890 – Florence, 3 June 1970

Roberto Longhi is universally regarded as the greatest Italian art historian of the twentieth century: a figure who revolutionized critical methodology, discovered or reevaluated entire areas of Italian painting that had remained in the shadows, and trained generations of scholars—from Federico Zeri to Ferdinando Bologna—who reshaped the discipline.

Born in Alba, in the Langhe region, on December 28, 1890, to a middle-class family of Modenese origin, Longhi soon moved to Rome, where in 1913 he completed his advanced studies at the school of Adolfo Venturi, the great encyclopedist of Italian painting, of whom he became the most brilliant student and later the most formidable critic. Venturi opened the doors of the journal L'Arte to him, in which Longhi published his first essays, wittily dismantling established attributions and inaugurating his method of formal analysis—based on the examination of brushstroke, color, and compositional structure, rather than on documentary sources. The characteristics of his literary style emerged even in his very earliest works: a dense prose, rich in synesthetic metaphors and lexical inventions, which transformed the art-historical essay into an autonomous literary genre.

From 1913, he taught art history at the Tasso and Visconti high schools in Rome, where his students included Lucia Lopresti, a writer who would publish under the pseudonym Anna Banti and who would become his wife in 1924, as well as his intellectual collaborator for life. During his years in Rome, Longhi published essays destined to become milestones: Orione (1919), Piero della Francesca (1913, expanded in 1927), and the polemical Gentile da Fabriano e il Pisanello (1926), in which he attacked Croce's aesthetics and asserted the philosophical autonomy of figurative language. Between 1920 and 1922, he undertook a long journey through Europe with the collector Alessandro Contini Bonacossi, visiting museums and collections in France, Spain, Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary—experiences that extraordinarily broadened his visual and comparative horizons.

In 1934, he was appointed to the chair of Medieval and Modern Art History at the University of Bologna, where he began teaching in 1935, establishing his first academic school. That very year he published Officina Ferrarese, his study of painting in Ferrara during the fifteenth century—from Cosmè Tura to Ercole de' Roberti—which remains an unsurpassed model of reconstructing a regional pictorial tradition through stylistic analysis. In 1937, through Minister Giuseppe Bottai (a former student of his at the Visconti School), he obtained a position at the General Directorate of Fine Arts in Rome, where he worked for two years on organizing the Italian art exhibition at the EUR, which was ultimately not held due to Italy's entry into the war. In 1943 he founded the journal Proporzioni, and refused to serve under the Italian Social Republic, resulting in his suspension from teaching until the end of the war—one of the few explicit political gestures in a life deliberately focused on art criticism.

In 1950 he was appointed to a professorship at the University of Florence, where he remained until reaching the mandatory retirement age, forming his second school and teaching in the small villa at Villa Il Tasso, which had become his studio-museum. In 1950 he founded the journal Paragone, which he edited until his death and which became the leading organ of postwar Italian art criticism. Through Paragone, Longhi waged his cultural battles: he reevaluated forgotten artists, debunked erroneous attributions, championed contemporary painting (particularly Giorgio Morandi, of whom he was a close friend and the first systematic critic), and published the writings of his best students, including Federico Zeri, Mina Gregori, Luciano Bellosi, and Ferdinando Bologna himself. The magazine outlived its founder: Anna Banti continued it after Longhi's death until the 1980s, maintaining its polemical spirit and critical quality.

Longhi's two major contributions to the history of world art concern Caravaggio and Piero della Francesca. Regarding the latter, Longhi was the first 20th-century critic to fully grasp Piero's role in the Italian 15th-century art scene: his 1927 monograph, immediately translated into French and English, restored the artist from Sansepolcro to the ranks of the greatest painters, influencing all subsequent criticism and paving the way for a reevaluation by Bernard Berenson and Anglo-Saxon critics. As for Caravaggio, Longhi was the undisputed leading figure in the 20th-century rediscovery: following the Quesiti caravaggeschi (1928–1934) and the Ultimi studi caravaggeschi (1943), in 1951 he curated the monumental exhibition Caravaggio e i Caravaggeschi at the Palazzo Reale in Milan, which marked a turning point in the public and critical perception of the Lombard painter, definitively establishing him as the father of European naturalism. His 1968 monograph on Caravaggio remains his most mature contribution to the subject.

Longhi's method is based on the supremacy of the "visual fact": Longhi refused to rely on documents or literary sources for attribution when the analysis of pictorial form—of color, light, space, and "tone"—could offer more precise answers. This formalism, rooted in the traditions of Warburg and Berenson, was, however, always tempered in Longhi by a keen historical sensibility, which placed every artist within the network of relationships between workshops, patrons, and local traditions. Equally celebrated is his prose: rich in neologisms, daring constructions, and visual metaphors capable of translating the quality of light or the texture of a color into words, his writings have influenced generations of critics and writers, from Pier Paolo Pasolini to Andrea Zanzotto.

Roberto Longhi died in Florence on June 3, 1970, bequeathing his extraordinary art collection, library, photo library, and archive to the Roberto Longhi Foundation for Art History Studies, now based at Villa Il Tasso in Florence. Each year, the Foundation awards scholarships to young art history researchers, continuing the educational work of its founder. The photographic archive, now part of the Zeri Foundation's Photo Library, is an indispensable resource for research on Italian painting from the 13th to the 18th century.

Lunghi, Elvio

Assisi, 1957

Elvio Lunghi was born in Assisi in 1957, a birthplace whose significance cannot be overstated: his entire scholarly life would orbit around the artistic heritage of that city and the Franciscan world it generated. He completed his Laurea in Lettere at the Università degli Studi di Perugia in 1981, and in 1987 defended his doctorate as an external candidate at the Università La Sapienza di Roma. His academic engagement began even before graduation: from 1983 to 1988 he served as professore incaricato at the Università per Stranieri di Perugia, a position that would prove the foundation of a career spent almost entirely at that institution. From 1988 to 1991 he taught as a tenured secondary school professor — an experience that cultivated his lifelong commitment to public communication of art history — before returning to the Università per Stranieri as professore comandato in 1991.

In 2004 Lunghi was appointed Professore Associato in Medieval Art History (sector L-ART/01) at the Università per Stranieri di Perugia, the post he has held ever since. His three declared research areas are: painting and sculpture in central Italy between the Middle Ages and the Renaissance; artistic patronage of the mendicant religious orders; and the popular promotion and dissemination of artistic heritage. These three axes are not parallel lines but deeply interwoven — the mendicant orders were the principal engine of visual culture in medieval Umbria, and understanding their patronage is inseparable from understanding the painters and sculptors Lunghi has spent his career studying.

No work has absorbed more of Lunghi's intellectual energy than the great artistic programme of the Basilica of San Francesco in Assisi. His monograph La Basilica di S. Francesco in Assisi (Scala Editrice, Florence, 1996) remains one of the most comprehensive single-volume accounts of the building's decoration. His 2012 volume Giotto e i pittori giotteschi ad Assisi (Editrice la Rocca, Marsciano, pp. 494) is the most systematic account yet written of the entire Giottesque workshop presence in the city. His article L'influenza di Pietro di Giovanni Olivi e di Ubertino da Casale nel programma iconografico della chiesa inferiore di S. Francesco ad Assisi, published in Collectanea Francescana in 1997, and the study La perduta decorazione trecentesca nell'abside della chiesa inferiore del S. Francesco, published in the same journal in 1996, together represent the most sustained scholarly engagement with the theological motivations behind the Lower Church's pictorial programme. His synthesis Immagini degli Spirituali. Il significato delle immagini nelle chiese francescane di Assisi (Il Formichiere, Foligno, 2019, pp. 313) crowns four decades of reflection on the relationship between Franciscan spirituality — particularly the Spiritual Franciscan movement — and the images produced for the order's churches.

Lunghi's contributions to the study of illuminated manuscripts in Umbria are among the most enduring in the field. In 2004 he wrote numerous entries for the Dizionario biografico dei miniatori italiani secoli IX–XVI (Edizioni Sylvestre Bonnard, Milan), covering artists including Bartolomeo Caporali, Giapeco Caporali, the Maestro dei Corali di Gubbio, the Maestro dei Corali di S. Lorenzo, the Maestro del Messale di Deruta, Marino da Perugia, Venturella di Pietro, and Vanni di Baldolo. Each of these entries represents original archival and stylistic research into artists who had received little or no dedicated scholarly attention. His essay Intorno ai codici liturgici del XIII e XIV secolo conservati nelle raccolte umbre, written with P.M. Della Porta and published in the catalogue Canto e colore: I corali di San Domenico di Perugia nella Biblioteca comunale Augusta (Perugia, 2006), accompanied catalogue entries for some twenty-three individual manuscripts preserved in the Augusta library. The 2018 exhibition Gubbio al tempo di Giotto, which Lunghi co-curated with Giordana Benazzi and Enrica Neri Lusanna, extended this manuscript work with entries on the Maestri dei corali di Gubbio, an anonymous Bolognese miniatore active in Gubbio, Venturella di Pietro, and Vanni di Baldolo.

Lunghi has also made a decisive mark on scholarship concerning wooden devotional sculpture in Umbria. His monograph La Passione degli Umbri. Crocifissi di legno in Valle Umbra tra Medioevo e Rinascimento (Orfini Numeister, Foligno, 2000, pp. 190) was the first systematic survey of the surviving corpus of wooden crucifixes in the Valle Umbra, tracing the artistic, liturgical, and devotional contexts that produced them. His essay La scultura lignea in Umbria nel XIII secolo, published in the CISAM volume L'Umbria nel XIII secolo in 2011, extended this analysis to the earliest documented phase of production in the region. Through the exhibition catalogues Da Giotto a Gentile (Fabriano, 2014) and Dal visibile all'indicibile (Foligno, 2012), he continued to catalogue individual crucifixes and place them in the broader context of mystical and penitential culture in central Italy.

As a connoisseur, Lunghi has proposed and refined a number of attributions that have entered standard art-historical usage. His early study Il Crocifisso di Giunta Pisano e l'icona del "Maestro di San Francesco" alla Porziuncola (1995) placed the two most important early devotional images at the Porziuncola in their full historical context, distinguishing the documented work of Giunta Pisano from the anonymous panel known as the Maestro di San Francesco. More controversially but very influentially, his essay Le arti del Duecento a Gubbio (2018) proposed an identification between the anonymous Maestro dei Crocifissi Francescani and the documented Bolognese painter Guido di Pietro da Gubbio, father of the miniatore Oderisi immortalised by Dante. In the catalogues of the exhibitions Da Giotto a Gentile (2014) and Gubbio al tempo di Giotto (2018), he systematically attributed multiple works to the Maestro dei Crocifissi Francescani across institutions in Assisi, Camerino, and Bologna. He also confirmed the presence of the Monteripido Crucifix within the corpus of the German sculptor Giovanni Teutonico, active in Umbria in the late fifteenth century.

While Lunghi's reputation rests primarily on medieval art, his scholarship extends naturally into the Quattrocento and early Cinquecento. He edited the major exhibition catalogue Nicolaus Pictor. Nicolò di Liberatore detto l'Alunno (Foligno, 2004, pp. 544) with Giordana Benazzi, the fullest account of the Foligno painter's life and workshop. He wrote Itinerari del Perugino in Umbria (Silvana Editoriale, 2004) and the monograph Il Martirio di san Sebastiano di Pietro Perugino a Panicale (2005), and in 2015 co-authored Cesare Sermei pittore devoto nell'Umbria del Seicento with P. Mercurelli Salari (Edizioni Orfini Numeister, pp. 330). A notable early contribution to Raphael studies was his 2003 monograph Raffaello a Cerqueto. Un affresco giovanile e l'esordio in Umbria and his article Una proposta per il giovane Raffaello (2002), which advanced a new attribution for the young Raphael's debut in Umbrian fresco painting.

Lunghi's scholarship has reached international audiences through publications in English and German. His essay Francis of Assisi in Prayer before the Crucifix in the Accounts of the First Biographers was published in the prestigious National Gallery of Art volume Italian Panel Painting of the Duecento and Trecento (Studies in the History of Art, XXXVIII, New Haven & London, 2002, pp. 341–353). He contributed a further study, Una proposta per il giovane Raffaello, to the Festschrift for Antje Middeldorf Kosegarten published in Dresden and Kassel in 2002. For the Enciclopedia dell'Arte Medievale of the Istituto dell'Enciclopedia Italiana he wrote entries on Orvieto (1997), Spoleto (1999), and Giovanni di Bonino (1995). For many years he has maintained a regular column in the journal San Francesco Patrono d'Italia, writing each month on themes drawn from Franciscan art and spirituality, making scholarly research accessible to the widest possible audience. His most recent volume, Perugia. Cento storie, was presented in December 2025, confirming that at nearly seventy years of age his scholarly productivity shows no sign of abating.

Rome, 1993

Fabio Mari was born in Rome in 1993. He completed his undergraduate studies at the University of Rome Tor Vergata in the Faculty of Letters and Philosophy, earning a degree in Cultural Heritage. A significant part of his scholarship is devoted to Caetani patronage, the powerful medieval family that controlled broad territories in southern Lazio between the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Mari has studied the frescoes of the Caetani Palace in Fondi, especially the camera picta cycle dedicated to Virgil and commissioned by Roffredo III, presenting these results at the conference European and Mediterranean Middle Ages. Exchanges, Circulation, and Artistic Mobility. Marrone, Raffaele

active 21st century

Raffaele Marrone is an Italian scholar specializing in medieval art history, with particular focus on late medieval visual culture, devotional practices, and the relationship between images and urban religious space in central Italy. His profile is especially relevant for research on Trecento Siena and the social uses of art in confraternal contexts.

Marrone is affiliated with the Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa, one of Italy's leading research institutions, and completed his doctoral work there under the supervision of Francesco Caglioti. In July 2025 he earned his PhD with a dissertation titled Images, Spaces, and Practices of Devotional Confraternities in Siena in the Late Middle Ages, a study that integrates visual analysis, documentary evidence, and urban topography.

His declared research interests include late medieval art and medieval iconography, with a concentration on fourteenth-century Sienese painting. Among his contributions is a study of the chapel in the cemetery of Santa Maria della Scala in Siena, decorated in 1341 by Ambrogio Lorenzetti, examined through archival sources and architectural evidence connected to the cult of Saint Cristina and to relics that arrived from Constantinople in 1359.

Marrone has also published on early fourteenth-century painting in Pistoia, including work on the pictorial cycle of the Stories of the Passion, expanding his research beyond Siena while maintaining a consistent focus on the interaction between narrative imagery, devotional practice, and local institutional history.

In November 2025 he presented the paper Images, Spaces, and Practices of Devotional Brotherhoods in the Late Middle Ages: The Case of Siena within the Medieval Networks series, further consolidating his position among emerging scholars working on confraternal visual culture and late medieval Italian art.

Masetti, Anna Rosa Calderoni

Italian art historian best known as a leading scholar of medieval art and of the artistic culture of Pisa and Liguria. Born in Pisa, she pursued a distinguished academic career and eventually became full professor of History of Medieval Art at the University of Genoa, where she taught for many years before retirement.

Her research has focused on the visual culture of the medieval Mediterranean, with particular attention to Pisa's civic and religious monuments, its role as a maritime republic, and its connections with the eastern Mediterranean, the Iberian world, and Byzantine artistic traditions. She has written extensively on Pisan Romanesque art, mosaics, sculpture, and the city's urban development, often bringing together art-historical analysis, urban history, and the study of maps and historical views of Pisa.

Calderoni Masetti has also studied smaller objects and systems of ornament, including enamelled belts and jewelry, addressing questions of technique, iconography, and courtly culture in the high and late Middle Ages. Her publications range from specialist scholarship to more accessible books, making her work a key reference for scholars and students interested in Pisan and Ligurian art as well as broader Mediterranean artistic exchanges. She continues to work as an independent researcher, publishing essays and participating in scholarly projects that deepen understanding of medieval visual culture in southern Europe.

Matthiae, Guglielmo

20th century

Guglielmo Matthiae was one of the most important Italian medievalists of the 20th century, trained in the school of Pietro Toesca and destined to become the leading scholar of medieval Roman mural painting, from late antiquity to the High Middle Ages.

Matthiae earned his degree in Medieval and Modern Art History at the University of Rome under the guidance of Pietro Toesca, whose assistant he later became. The influence of Toesca—the great systematizer of medieval Italian painting—was decisive: Matthiae absorbed his philological rigor and interest in the early Christian and early medieval traditions, directing his research from the outset toward the churches and pictorial decorations of Rome and Lazio.

Matthiae began his career in the Administration of Fine Arts, rising to the rank of Superintendent. He worked first in Abruzzo—where he acquired a deep knowledge of Romanesque painting in central Italy, studying in particular the frescoes of Bominaco and other monastic complexes—and then in Rome and Lazio, where his institutional role became inextricably intertwined with art-historical research and restoration work. He was appointed to teach Medieval Art History at the University of L'Aquila, where he passed on his methodology to subsequent generations.

The pinnacle of his scholarly output is represented by the two-volume work Pittura romana del Medioevo (Rome, Fratelli Palombi, 1965–1966): the first volume dedicated to the 4th–10th centuries, the second to the 11th–14th centuries. This work remains an indispensable reference today for anyone studying medieval pictorial decoration in Rome, from the catacombs and early Christian mosaic cycles to the great Romanesque and Proto-Gothic painting projects. The text was scientifically updated in a second edition (1987–1988) edited by Maria Andaloro and Francesco Gandolfo, attesting to its enduring historiographical relevance.

A parallel and equally important strand of his work concerns the early Roman churches: Matthiae edited the entry Churches of Rome from the 4th to the 10th Century in the series dedicated to Christian Rome, and published monographs on numerous Roman sacred buildings within the series Illustrated Churches of Rome. In 1961, he published in the Memorie della Pontificia Accademia Romana di Archeologia his research on the origins of the Basilica of San Pietro in Vincoli, conducted in parallel with the restoration of the building, which he himself had directed. In the final years of his life, he devoted himself with increasing intensity to the Abbey of Grottaferrata, studying its mosaics of the Greek-Byzantine tradition and overseeing their delicate removal.

A lesser-known but highly significant aspect of his work is his pioneering interest in the photographic documentation of works of art as a tool for research and conservation. His photographic collection, which was incorporated into the Photographic Library of the Federico Zeri Foundation in Bologna in the 1990s, constitutes a visual archive of extraordinary value for the study of the Middle Ages between East and West, documenting works and monuments that are today sometimes compromised or lost. Matthiae was a member of the Gruppo dei Romanisti, the association of the most distinguished scholars of Roman culture and history, further confirming his deep roots in the scholarly tradition of the city that was the center of his entire intellectual life.

Meiss, Millard

1904 – 1975

Millard Meiss was one of the most influential American art historians of the twentieth century, specializing in late medieval and early Renaissance painting, and later in manuscript illumination — areas of direct relevance to your own research interests. Born in 1904, Meiss first studied architecture at Princeton, graduating in 1926, before turning to art history. He taught at Columbia University from 1934 to 1953, then moved to Harvard as both professor and Curator of Painting at the Fogg Art Museum. In 1958, he left Harvard to assume the chair of Professor of Art at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton — a position vacated by his former teacher Erwin Panofsky, one of the giants of iconographic studies.

Meiss is celebrated for bridging traditional connoisseurship with a broader, socially informed art history. He sought to read paintings not merely as stylistic objects but as reflections of collective consciousness — a revolutionary stance at the time. His innovative interpretation held that artists of the mid-Trecento had absorbed the traumatic shocks of economic crises and the catastrophic Bubonic Plague of 1348, and that this suffering was visibly encoded in their work.

His landmark book Painting in Florence and Siena after the Black Death (1951) remains a cornerstone of Trecento studies, analyzing how the Black Death reshaped the spiritual and aesthetic character of Italian painting. He won the Haskins Medal in 1953 — awarded annually for a distinguished book in medieval studies — for this work. In 1962, he co-authored with Leonetto Tintori The Painting of the Life of St. Francis in Assisi, innovative in its use of technical analysis of mural painting to resolve major attribution debates.

Meiss's research broadened significantly in the 1960s to include manuscript illumination, an area now at the center of your own scholarly pursuits. His monumental multi-volume series French Painting in the Time of Jean de Berry (1967–74) is a definitive study of late Gothic manuscript illumination at the Valois court, covering French, Flemish, and Italian influences on the luxury manuscripts produced for Jean, Duc de Berry. This work remains an indispensable reference for anyone studying illuminated manuscripts of the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries.

Meiss was elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, the American Philosophical Society, and the British Academy, as well as several French and Italian scholarly societies. The College Art Association named its book illustration grant in his honor. A full-length reassessment of his scholarship, Millard Meiss, American Art History, and Conservation by Jennifer Cooke (University of Turin), was published in the Routledge Studies in Art Historiography series and provides a thorough modern evaluation of his methodology and lasting influence.

Meyer, Alfred Gotthold

Germany, 1864 – 1904

Alfred Gotthold Meyer was a German art historian born in 1864 whose scholarly work focused with exceptional precision on the medieval and early Renaissance sculpture and architecture of northern Italy, particularly Lombardy. Despite dying at just forty years of age, he produced two landmark works that remain indispensable references for the study of Lombard artistic heritage.

Principal Work on 14th-Century Lombardy

Meyer's most celebrated contribution is his monograph Lombardische Denkmaler des vierzehnten Jahrhunderts: Giovanni di Balduccio da Pisa und die Campionesen (1893), subtitled Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der oberitalienischen Plastik - "A Contribution to the History of North Italian Sculpture". This ambitious study examines two of the most significant sculptural forces in 14th-century Lombard art: Giovanni di Balduccio da Pisa, the Tuscan sculptor who transplanted the refined Pisan Gothic tradition into Milan, and the Campionese masters, the dynasty of Lombard sculptors and architects from Campione d'Italia whose workshops dominated the decoration of the great northern Italian cathedrals and funerary monuments throughout the Trecento. The book remains a foundational text for any scholar researching the transmission of sculptural styles between Tuscany and Lombardy in this period.

Oberitalienische Fruhrenaissance

Meyer's second major work, Oberitalienische Fruhrenaissance: Bauten und Bildwerke der Lombardei ("North Italian Early Renaissance: Buildings and Sculptures of Lombardy"), appeared in two volumes, with the second part - Die Bluthezeit ("The Flowering") - published in Berlin by Wilhelm Ernst & Sohn in 1900. This expansive survey charts the transition from the Gothic to the Renaissance in Lombard artistic production, treating architecture and sculpture as interrelated phenomena shaped by the particular cultural context of the Po Valley. The work demonstrated Meyer's command of both formal analysis and historical documentation, synthesizing archival evidence with close stylistic observation in a manner characteristic of the rigorous German Kunstwissenschaft tradition of the late 19th century.

Significance and Scholarly Legacy

Meyer wrote at a time when the systematic study of medieval and proto-Renaissance Italian art was still being established as an academic discipline, and his focus on the often-overlooked Lombard school - long overshadowed by the Florentine and Sienese traditions - was itself a scholarly intervention of lasting importance. His analysis of the Campionese masters helped define how scholars understand the regional character of Lombard Gothic sculpture and its relationship to broader European currents. The digitized version of Oberitalienische Fruhrenaissance is preserved and accessible through the Universitatsbibliothek Heidelberg, and Lombardische Denkmaler is available through the Internet Archive, making both works accessible to researchers today. His premature death in 1904 cut short a scholarly career that, had it continued, would almost certainly have reshaped the historiography of Lombard medieval art more broadly.

Monneret de Villard, Hugo

Milan, 16 January 1881 - Rome, 4 November 1954

Hugo Monneret de Villard was one of the most extraordinary and multifaceted figures in twentieth-century Italian academia: trained as an engineer, he became Italy's leading scholar of Islamic, Coptic, and Eastern Christian art, as well as a pioneer in medieval archaeology. He stands as a rare bridge between the technical rigor of engineering and the encyclopedic breadth of Oriental studies.

Born in Milan on January 16, 1881, to Enrico Monneret and Anna Foli, Hugo belonged to a family of Burgundian origin that had moved to Piedmont during the French Revolution. He enrolled at the Polytechnic University of Milan, where he earned a degree in industrial chemical engineering in 1904. This technical training, however, did not lead him toward industry, but rather toward the history of medieval Lombard art and architecture, fields into which he ventured with an engineer's methodology and a humanist's passion.

Between 1913 and 1924, he taught History of Architecture at the Polytechnic University of Milan, becoming in 1913 the first Italian university professor of a subject called Medieval Archaeology. During this period, he engaged in depth with the medieval history of Lombardy and the Romanesque architecture of northern Italy, also contributing to the debate on the preservation of Milan's architectural heritage. These years marked the first phase of a career that would later take an increasingly Eastern and international direction.

In the second phase of his scholarly life, Monneret de Villard devoted himself to the art and archaeology of the Christian and Islamic East, working on behalf of the Italian Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Egypt, Nubia, Ethiopia, Iraq, Iran, India, and Central Asia. In Egypt and Sudan, he amassed an immense body of documentation on Nubian, Coptic, and Islamic art, compiling photographs, drawings, and architectural surveys that remain primary sources of great importance to this day. In 1937, following the Italian conquest of Ethiopia, he participated in excavation operations at Axum, contributing to the selection of the obelisk later transported to Rome, a controversial episode linked to fascist colonialism.

Monneret de Villard was the only Italian specialist in Islamic art of his time, and his bibliographic output effectively constitutes the entirety of Italian literature on the subject for that period. Among his most important works are studies on Roman and Christian Nubia, on Coptic and Muslim architecture in Egypt, and fundamental research on the art and architecture of medieval Iraq and Iran. He died before completing an ambitious catalog of Islamic art in Italy, which remained partially unpublished. His archive, comprising approximately 340 volumes and 750 pamphlets on Orientalist topics, was donated by his family to the Library of Archaeology and Art History in Rome at Palazzo Venezia in 1956 and 1966.

In 1944, after the fall of fascism in Rome, he taught archaeology at La Sapienza University for a year. In 1950, he was elected a member of the Accademia dei Lincei, the highest honor in Italian scientific culture. He died in Rome on November 4, 1954. His scientific legacy is particularly significant for anyone studying medieval art in its Mediterranean and Eastern dimensions: he demonstrated that one cannot fully understand European medieval art without taking into account the circulation of forms, techniques, and iconography among the Western Christian world, Byzantium, the Coptic world, and Islam, a perspective that anticipated the methodological debate in global art history by decades.

Oertel, Robert

Leipzig, 1907 – Freiburg im Breisgau, 1981

Robert Oertel (Leipzig, October 30, 1907 – Freiburg im Breisgau, December 1, 1981) was one of the most important German art historians of the twentieth century, specializing in medieval and Renaissance Italian painting, with particular emphasis on the period between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries.

He studied art history, history, classical archaeology, and philosophy at Leipzig, Vienna, Munich, Hamburg, and Freiburg im Breisgau. In February 1932 he completed his doctorate in Frankfurt am Main under Hans Jantzen, one of the major theorists of medieval German art, and in 1948 he obtained his habilitation in art history, consolidating his return to academic and museum life after the war.

Between 1935 and 1939, Oertel served as director of the Kunsthistorisches Institut in Florence, where he deepened his firsthand knowledge of Tuscan medieval and Renaissance painting. After 1939 he moved to the Gemaldegalerie Alte Meister in Dresden, working under Hans Posse and later Hermann Voss on inventories and acquisitions. During the war he was chiefly responsible for the relocation and safeguarding of the gallery's holdings. Drafted in 1944, he fought on the Eastern Front as an artilleryman, was captured by Soviet forces in May 1945, and was released in August of the same year.

After a brief return to work with the Dresden collections, Oertel was dismissed because of his past NSDAP membership. He rebuilt his career in West Germany, becoming chief curator at the Alte Pinakothek in Munich, where he was appointed Oberkonservator in 1960 and Landeskonservator in 1962. The peak of his institutional career came in November 1964, when he was appointed director of the Gemaldegalerie of the Prussian State Museums in Berlin-Dahlem, a role he held until 1973. There he oversaw the reinstallation of the Italian Renaissance painting section and planned the new Gemaldegalerie building at the Tiergarten.

For medieval studies, his most enduring contribution is Early Italian Painting to 1400, a major critical synthesis of Italian painting from its origins through the end of the fourteenth century. He also published a monographic study on Fra Filippo Lippi in 1942, combining broad historical framing with close analysis of a single master. His photographic archive, preserved at the Zentralinstitut fur Kunstgeschichte in Munich, documents Italian painting from the thirteenth to the sixteenth century and remains a valuable research resource.

Offner, Richard

Vienna, 1889 - Florence, 1965

Richard Offner was one of the most rigorous and influential art historians of the twentieth century, specializing in medieval and early Renaissance Florentine painting. His career marked a turning point in the systematic and critically grounded study of Duecento, Trecento, and early Quattrocento masters.

Born in Vienna on June 30, 1889, Offner moved with his family to New York in 1891. He studied at Harvard between 1909 and 1912, then trained as a Fellow at the American Academy in Rome, where he developed his visual method on early Italian painting. In 1914, he completed his doctorate at the University of Vienna under Max Dvorak, a foundational figure of the Viennese school. This formation gave Offner the strict formal and methodological discipline that would define his connoisseurship.

He began teaching at the University of Chicago in 1915, moved to Harvard in 1920 as a Sachs Fellow, and joined New York University in 1923. Promoted to full professor in 1927, he served as head of the fine arts department from 1930 to 1933 and remained at NYU until retiring in 1961. He died in Florence on August 26, 1965, after a lifetime devoted to Florentine painting.

Offner's scholarly identity was rooted in connoisseurship understood as a disciplined reading of artistic personality through style. Influenced by Bernard Berenson but also by the analytical rigor of the Viennese school, he treated line, form, and structure as historically legible evidence. He was skeptical of color photography as a primary analytical instrument, preferring black-and-white documentation to minimize interpretive distortion and isolate compositional relations.

His central achievement was A Critical and Historical Corpus of Florentine Painting, an extraordinarily ambitious, multi-volume cataloguing project conceived in the 1920s to provide exhaustive critical coverage of Florentine painting from its medieval foundations through the Renaissance. Planned across six sections and thirty volumes, the Corpus was organized by artists and structured through detailed historical and attributional analysis. Offner published twelve volumes, working closely with Klara Steinweg, but the enterprise remained unfinished at his death.

After 1965, the Corpus was continued by Miklos Boskovits (1984-2011) and remains active under the direction of Sonia Chiodo, with Andrea De Marchi in a leading institutional role and support from the Kunsthistorisches Institut in Florenz. Offner's early books, including Studies in Florentine Painting: The Fourteenth Century (1923) and Italian Primitives at Yale University (1927), remain foundational references.

His documentary legacy is equally significant: the Offner photographic archive, now preserved at the National Gallery of Art in Washington through the Institute of Fine Arts, contains roughly 50,000 photographs, negatives, notes, clippings, and teaching materials. Preserved with his original attributional structures intact, it remains one of the most important research tools for scholars of Florentine medieval painting.

Pace, Valentino

Lanciano (Abruzzo), 2 October 1944

Valentino Pace is one of Italy's leading scholars of medieval and Byzantine art. His academic career has spanned universities across Europe and North America and has brought him recognition from major international scholarly institutions.

Pace earned his degree in Humanities with a specialization in Medieval Art History at the University of Rome (academic year 1968-69). Between 1982 and 1994 he worked in Rome as a researcher at the Institute of Art History at La Sapienza, consolidating a methodological approach centered on early Christian and early medieval art and on the relations between Rome, the Latin world, and the Byzantine East.

From the 1980s onward he established an international profile through teaching appointments in Heidelberg, Bonn, Basel, Johns Hopkins University, and Princeton. He also held a chair in Early Christian and Medieval Art at Trinity College (Hartford, Connecticut), on the Rome campus. These experiences fostered sustained dialogue with Germanic, Anglo-Saxon, and Scandinavian historiographical traditions.

In 1998 he was appointed associate professor of Medieval and Byzantine Art History at the University of Udine, becoming full professor in 2004 and serving until retirement in 2014. International recognition followed: foreign member of the Academy of Sciences and Letters at the University of Oslo (from 2003), recipient in 2010 of the University of Belgrade award for outstanding medieval and Byzantine art-historical research, Richard-Krautheimer-Professor at the Bibliotheca Hertziana / Max-Planck-Institut fur Kunstgeschichte in Rome (2010-2012), and Rudolf-Arnheim-Gastprofessur at Humboldt University in Berlin (2015-2016).

In his honor, the anthology Un Medioevo in lungo e in largo. Da Bisanzio all'Occidente (VI-XVI secolo) was published, confirming his importance in international medieval studies. On Academia.edu he has made 232 essays freely available and has built a substantial scholarly following.

Paolini, Gilberto

L'Aquila, 22 December 1928; later active in the United States

Gilberto Paolini was born in L'Aquila on 22 December 1928 and later emigrated to the United States. He developed his academic career at Tulane University in New Orleans, where he taught in the Department of Spanish and Portuguese and eventually became Professor Emeritus. His institutional profile was therefore primarily situated in Hispano-medieval studies rather than in Italian medieval studies, while still reflecting his Italian origins and literary training.

Paolini's scholarship engaged with medieval Iberian literature and culture, especially the world of Alfonso X of Castile (Alfonso el Sabio, 1221-1284), whose court produced the Cantigas de Santa Maria. A JSTOR study, Iconography and Literature: Alfonso Himself in Cantiga 209, identifies Paolini as a member of the Relations Committee overseeing scholarship in this field, confirming his active role within Alfonsine and medieval Iberian studies.

Earlier in his career, Paolini also published in Forum Italicum: A Journal of Italian Studies, including the 1968 article La moralita zeniana, indicating that his interests extended to modern Italian literary culture alongside medieval Iberian research.

The scope and impact of his scholarly contribution were recognized by the publication of Studies in Honor of Gilberto Paolini in the Juan de la Cuesta Hispanic Monographs series. Contributions to that volume include work explicitly addressing medieval tradition and Islamic cultural currents, suggesting an intellectual horizon centered on the cross-cultural dimensions of the medieval Mediterranean and Iberian worlds at the intersection of Christian, Islamic, and Jewish learning.

Pasqui, Ubaldo

Arezzo, 20 March 1859 – Arezzo, 29 November 1939

Ubaldo Pasqui was born in Arezzo on 20 March 1859 to Pietro Pasqui, an appreciated woodcarver, and Angelo (Angiolo) Pasqui, the archaeologist who became famous as the discoverer of the Ara Pacis Augustae. He left the local technical schools quite early and undertook a largely self-directed course of study in the libraries and archives of his native city, a choice that shaped his entire intellectual identity. Before the age of twenty he had already conceived the ambitious plan of writing an organic, document-based history of Arezzo, inspired in part by the massive Memorie manoscritte della città di Arezzo left by the marchese Antonio Albergotti. This early project explains the precocious breadth of his archival explorations and his life-long attachment to municipal history in a decidedly Romantic and Risorgimento-influenced key.

His first publication, written together with his brother Angelo, was La cattedrale aretina e i suoi monumenti (1880), a broad and systematic study of the cathedral of Arezzo and its artworks, illustrated with drawings by his own hand. Two years later, in 1882, on the occasion of the great Guidonian celebrations, he published the Guida di Arezzo, explicitly presented as "nuova" and "compilata sui documenti", already signalling his programmatic insistence on archival foundations for any description of the city. In order to collect documentary material on Arezzo — whose medieval communal archive had been destroyed in the fire of 1384 — Pasqui frequented the archives of Florence, Rome, Siena and Perugia, an activity that broadened both his documentary base and his scholarly network.

Pasqui's professional life was deeply rooted in archival work. From the 1880s he collaborated with the distinguished scholar Gian Francesco Gamurrini and came into contact with the great diplomatist Cesare Paoli, who between 1889 and 1891 published three of Pasqui's monographs in the prestigious Archivio Storico Italiano. In 1890–1892 he reorganized the pre-unification historical archive of the Comune di Arezzo, serving for decades as its conservator and compiling an inventory that, reworking earlier tools by Gustavo Mancini and Pasquale Leoni, remained the principal instrument for accessing the material for more than a century.

His archival career coincided with his broader role within the emerging Italian state archival administration; later surveys of the Archivio di Stato di Arezzo recall that some of the earliest concentrations of historical fonds in the city were the result of Pasqui's efforts from 1885 onwards. His training and practice as a diplomatist and paleographer shaped his methodological stance: he was deeply convinced that only rigorous archival investigation and critical analysis of documents could yield reliable, scientifically grounded historical knowledge. This conviction underpinned not only his documentary editions but also his work in local historiography and art history, making him a key figure in the renewal of humanistic studies in Arezzo at the turn of the century.

Although archivist and diplomatist by profession, Pasqui cultivated a remarkably wide range of scholarly interests, being described by the Società Storica Aretina as a man of "multiforme ingegno". He worked as historian, art historian, archaeologist, glottologist, bibliographer and sigillography specialist, publishing around forty studies on Arezzo and its territory. Among his main works are studies on the Casa del Petrarca (1900, 1904, 1933), on the family of Giorgio Vasari (1911), on Aretine painters of the late Middle Ages and Renaissance (notably the 1917 essay on painters active from the mid-twelfth century to 1527), on pottery (vasi fittili, 1921), on Bartolomeo della Gatta (1926) and on the Aretine dialect (1928).

His best-known and most substantial achievement is the monumental Documenti per la storia della città di Arezzo nel medio evo, published in four volumes between 1889 and 1937 and amounting to over two thousand pages. This collection, conceived as preparatory to a complete Storia di Arezzo that he never managed to write, gathers nearly 900 documents dated between 650 and 1385, along with some ten chronicles, including the third-rhyme chronicle of ser Bartolomeo di ser Gorello, whose authorship Pasqui correctly identified. The enterprise, with its strong "Muratorian" spirit, became a fundamental quarry for all subsequent studies on medieval Arezzo and remains a standard reference in diplomatic and historical research.

In parallel, Pasqui's Guida illustrata storica, artistica e commerciale di Arezzo e dintorni, published in 1925 together with Ugo Viviani and enriched with an extraordinary photographic apparatus, represented a synthesis of his activity as historian, art historian and archaeologist. For Pasqui, the description of monuments and the review of the city's illustrious men formed an organic part of local history, an approach that prefigures later notions of integrated cultural heritage. This guide, which combines historical narrative, topographical description and artistic documentation, is still considered an indispensable working tool for art historians dealing with Arezzo.

Beyond his archival post, Ubaldo Pasqui held several public and cultural positions in the city and province. From 1890 to 1913 he served as regio ispettore of monuments and excavations for the province of Arezzo, then as conservator of the municipal picture gallery (pinacoteca), and in 1914 he was made a knight of the Kingdom of Italy. He was a member of various Tuscan learned societies, including the Accademia Etrusca of Cortona, the Florentine society "La Colombaria", and the Deputazione di storia patria per la Toscana, and later also entered the Accademia Petrarca in Arezzo. The city itself commemorates him with plaques and a small monument near his birthplace, where he is paired with his older contemporary and sometime mentor Gamurrini.

Contemporary testimonies and later assessments present Pasqui as a figure of difficult and somewhat angular character, inclined to non-conformist positions and often isolated within the Aretine cultural milieu. His vision of the city's past was strongly marked by a narrow, municipal patriotism of Romantic and Risorgimento derivation, a mindset widespread in many Italian smaller cities between the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In at least one case this led to a significant historical misunderstanding: it was Pasqui's interpretation that prompted the podestà Guidotti Mori, in 1929, to formalize an anomalous version of Arezzo's civic coat of arms. Yet the very body of archival evidence assembled and published by Pasqui has since allowed later scholars to move beyond some of his interpretative limits, demonstrating how his empirical groundwork outlived his ideological framework.

Ubaldo Pasqui died in Arezzo on 29 November 1939, having spent his entire life working for and on his native city. The archival fonds he concentrated and inventoried formed the initial nucleus of what later became the local section of the Archivio di Stato, established in the city by ministerial decree in 1941, ensuring institutional continuity for his work as archivist and diplomatist. Generations of Aretine scholars — historians, philologists, art historians and archaeologists — have depended on his documentary editions and guides, which remain essential points of reference for the medieval and early modern history of the city.

Modern evaluations underline the dual nature of his legacy: on the one hand, an at times restrictive municipal perspective; on the other, an extraordinary depth of archival research and methodological rigour that renewed historical and humanistic studies in Arezzo. Because of this, current historiography on Arezzo routinely treats Pasqui as both an object and an instrument of research: his writings are critically scrutinized, yet his Documenti and guides are still used daily as basic tools. In short, Ubaldo Pasqui can be seen as an exemplary figure of late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Italian local erudition at its best: deeply rooted in place, passionately attached to municipal history, but also capable of producing documentary work of lasting national scholarly value.

Pasut, Francesca Rosa

active late 20th–21st century

Francesca Rosa Pasut is one of Italy's leading specialists in medieval illuminated manuscripts, with a scholarly career spanning nearly three decades and focused primarily on Florentine and Bolognese manuscript painting from the thirteenth to the fifteenth centuries. Her work sits at the intersection of art history, codicology, and Dante studies, making her a central figure in contemporary research on Italian medieval book culture.

Pasut received her graduate training at the Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore in Milan, and then pursued a three-year specialization (Scuola di Specializzazione triennale in Storia dell'Arte) at the Università degli Studi di Milano. A decisive formative experience came through her fellowship at the prestigious Fondazione di Studi di Storia dell'Arte Roberto Longhi in Florence, where she held a scholarship for the academic years 1997–98 and 1998–99. This institution, founded by the great art historian Roberto Longhi, has long been a crucible for connoisseurship-based approaches to Italian painting, and its influence is clearly visible in Pasut's method of close stylistic and attributional analysis.

Her institutional career has centered on two major universities. She has been affiliated with the Università degli Studi di Firenze, within the Department of History, Archaeology, Geography, Art and Performing Arts (SAGAS), where she held a post-doctoral position. More recently, she has been associated with the Università degli Studi di Napoli "Federico II," within its Department of Humanistic Studies. These affiliations reflect the broad national reach of her scholarly network, connecting the great Florentine tradition of art historical research with the important medieval manuscript holdings of Southern Italy.

One of Pasut's most significant early scholarly contributions is her volume Ornamental Painting in Italy, 1250–1310: An Illustrated Index (Florence: Giunti, 2003), published as part of the foundational Corpus of Florentine Painting series edited by Miklós Boskovits. This work systematically catalogued decorative painting in Italian manuscripts of the late Duecento and early Trecento, providing the field with an essential reference tool for understanding the stylistic relationships between workshops operating during a period of remarkable artistic transformation. The volume addresses a category of medieval visual production — border and ornamental work in manuscripts — that had often been treated as peripheral, but which Pasut demonstrated to be a rich source of evidence for questions of dating, attribution, and inter-workshop exchange.

Pasut has dedicated a substantial portion of her career to the study of Pacino di Bonaguida, the early fourteenth-century Florentine painter and illuminator who was among the first artists to create illustrated copies of Dante's Divine Comedy. She contributed the authoritative entry on Pacino to the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani (vol. 80, Treccani, 2014), the national biographical encyclopedia of Italy. Her essay "Codici miniati della Commedia a Firenze attorno al 1330: questioni attributive e di cronologia" (Rivista di Studi Danteschi, 2007) is considered a landmark study on the attribution and dating of the earliest Florentine illustrated Dante manuscripts, having accumulated over 2,400 views on Academia.edu. She also published research on Pacino's illumination of the Trivulzian Bible (Codice Trivulziano 2139), co-authored with M. Pontone in Libri & Documenti (2014), demonstrating the collaborative, workshop-based nature of this major commission.

A particularly original thread in Pasut's research concerns the Danti del Cento, a compact cluster of Florentine Commedia manuscripts identifiable through shared philological, graphic, and codicological features — replicated from one exemplar to the next in codified form. At the 2021 Bargello Museum exhibition marking the seventh centenary of Dante's death, she argued that the key impulse for producing illustrated Commedia manuscripts emerged around 1337, the year Giotto's image of Dante appeared among the blessed in Paradise in the Bargello's Cappella del Podestà. This thesis connects the production of illustrated Dante manuscripts directly to a specific historical and devotional moment in Florentine civic life, and was central to the exhibition's scholarly apparatus.

Alongside her Florentine research, Pasut has made lasting contributions to the study of Bolognese manuscript painting, particularly the work of Nicolò di Giacomo, the most prominent illuminator of late Trecento Bologna. Her early article "Qualche considerazione sul percorso di Nicolò di Giacomo miniatore bolognese" (Arte Cristiana, 1998) established key aspects of his chronology and stylistic development. She later published an essay on his illustrations for the Ambrosian manuscript of Seneca's Tragedies (co-authored with C.M. Monti, in Miscellanea Graecolatina, 2013), a study that showed how classical literary texts circulated in richly illustrated form within the learned milieu of Trecento Northern Italy. She also wrote a profile of the master for the popular illumination journal Alumina. Pagine Miniate (2009), bringing her specialist research to a wider audience.

Pasut's interests extend naturally to the other two crowns of the Trecento literary canon. Her essay "Il Dante illustrato di Petrarca: problemi di miniatura tra Firenze e Pisa alla metà del Trecento" (Studi Petrarcheschi, 2007) traced the illuminated copy of the Commedia owned by Petrarch, addressing complex questions about artistic interchange between Florence and Pisa around 1350. Her contribution to the 2013 exhibition catalogue Boccaccio autore e copista (Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana) treated Boccaccio as a visual artist, analyzing his drawings in relation to the broader manuscript culture of his time.

Pasut's chronological range extends into the Quattrocento through her research on manuscript production connected with the early Vatican Library. Her major essay "Libri, miniatori e artisti alle origini della Vaticana tra Niccolò V e Sisto IV" appeared in the volume Le origini della Biblioteca Vaticana tra Umanesimo e Rinascimento (Vatican City, 2010), exploring the illuminators and artists who contributed to the Library's founding collections under popes Nicholas V and Sixtus IV. This work positions her as a scholar equally at home in the Trecento world of Gothic illumination and in the transitional moment of the early Renaissance.

Beyond her individual research, Pasut has played an important role in organizing the field. She co-edited the conference proceedings Da Giotto a Botticelli: Pittura fiorentina tra Gotico e Rinascimento (Florence, 2008), the acts of an international symposium held at the University of Florence and the Museo di San Marco in 2005. This volume brought together international specialists on Florentine painting and illumination across a critical century of artistic transformation. She has also contributed to the Illuminated Dante Project (IDP), an international digital humanities initiative cataloguing the approximately 280 illuminated manuscripts of the Divine Comedy from the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Piccirilli, Pietro

Sulmona, 18 July 1849 – Sulmona, 8 March 1921

Pietro Piccirilli was the first scholar from Abruzzo to approach the history of medieval art and architecture in his region in a systematic and critically grounded manner, documenting the artistic heritage of inland Abruzzo through rigorous methodology and tireless fieldwork, which established him as an irreplaceable figure in southern medieval studies.

Born in Sulmona on July 18, 1849, Piccirilli was entirely self-taught, graduating with top honors in August 1875 from the Institute of Fine Arts in Naples, then directed by Edoardo Dalbono. By 1872 he was already working as a teacher in Sulmona's secondary schools, a career he never abandoned until his death, alongside his historical-artistic research: he was responsible for founding the School of Arts and Crafts in Sulmona, an institution that had a tangible impact on the technical and artistic education of local generations. An acquaintance and friend of Benedetto Croce, he was the second scholar from Abruzzo—after Antonio De Nino—to engage systematically and not merely sporadically with the region's historical and artistic heritage, distinguishing himself from his predecessors precisely through his ability to compare sources and subject attributions to a precise stylistic examination, without succumbing to the parochial biases that had marred many local studies.

Piccirilli's most enduring contribution concerns two distinct yet complementary fields: medieval architecture in Abruzzo and the luxury arts—goldsmithing, silversmithing, and woodcarving. In 1888, he published in Lanciano Monumenti architettonici e artistici di Sulmona (Architectural and Artistic Monuments of Sulmona), the first critical survey of medieval architecture in the Peligna region, followed between 1893 and 1898 by the volumes La Basilica di San Clemente a Casauria (The Basilica of San Clemente in Casauria) and Monumenti sulmonesi architettonici descritti ed illustrati (Described and Illustrated Architectural Monuments of Sulmona) (ten issues with thirty-six plates). At the same time, Piccirilli was the first scholar to reconstruct, with historiographical rigor, the great medieval goldsmithing tradition of Sulmona and to systematically analyze the work of the leading 15th-century goldsmith of Abruzzo, Nicola da Guardiagrele, grouping the relics scattered throughout the region's churches into coherent stylistic families through an attributive method that anticipated the formalistic approach later codified by Longhi's criticism. His essays on medieval goldsmithing were published in Adolfo Venturi's journal L'Arte, which attests to the recognition of his expertise by the national scholarly community.

Piccirilli was the first scholar to provide a detailed and systematic description of the frescoes in Santa Maria ad Cryptas a Fossa and the oratory of San Pellegrino a Bominaco, the two most important medieval pictorial cycles in inland Abruzzo. These descriptions, published in local journals between the late 19th century and the first decade of the 20th century, remained for decades the sole scientific reference for anyone wishing to study those monuments, and were explicitly cited by all the great medievalists who studied Abruzzese painting during the 20th century—from Guglielmo Matthiae to Ferdinando Bologna.

In 1897, together with Giovanni Pansa, Piccirilli founded the Rassegna Abruzzese di Storia e Arte (RASA), the first scholarly journal entirely dedicated to the region's historical and artistic heritage. He was also among the founders of the Società Regia di Storia Patria degli Abruzzi (later the Deputazione Abruzzese di Storia Patria), based in L'Aquila, an institution that brought together scholars and historians from the region in a joint project of archival and monumental research. Among his foreign scholarly counterparts were Émile Bertaux—author of the seminal L'art dans l'Italie méridionale (1903)—and G. Rohault de Fleury, with whom he collaborated on studies of medieval goldsmithing, fostering dialogue between Abruzzo research and French medieval studies.

The most emblematic moment in Piccirilli's life was his response to the Marsica earthquake of January 13, 1915, which completely destroyed Avezzano and devastated dozens of towns in western Abruzzo, burying sculptures, frescoes, liturgical furnishings, and archives of immense value under the rubble. Piccirilli, by then sixty-five years old, rushed to the affected areas and remained there for weeks, working in conditions of extreme cold and hardship, leading the recovery of salvageable works with an energy and determination that Superintendent Carlo Ignazio Gavini, in his seminal History of Architecture in Abruzzo (1927), recalled as one of the most shining examples of dedication to artistic heritage. His son Guido Piccirilli (1885–1968) continued his father's research, becoming himself a scholar of Abruzzo art history. Pietro Piccirilli passed away on March 8, 1921, leaving behind a bibliographic output of over fifty-five contributions published between 1882 and 1919 in journals such as Venturi's L'Arte, Napoli Nobilissima, the Rassegna Abruzzese, and the Rassegna d'Arte.

Post, Chandler Rathfon

American art historian renowned for his expertise in Spanish and Italian Renaissance art, particularly iconography and painting traditions. Procacci, Ugo

Florence, 31 March 1905 – Florence, 19 February 1991

Ugo Procacci was born in Florence on 31 March 1905 into a lower-middle-class family; his father Virgilio worked as a railway inspector and his mother Amelia Faini was a housewife. He studied at the University of Florence, where he graduated in 1927 with a thesis in art history, coming into contact with the environment of the Rivista d'Arte and with the Florentine school of historians of medieval and Renaissance art. After a short period as an assistant at the university, he entered the Soprintendenza alle Gallerie of Florence at the end of the 1920s, beginning a career as a state official that would last more than four decades. In 1932 he became director of the newly founded Gabinetto dei Restauri of the Florentine Soprintendenza, a laboratory that he built up into a crucial centre for the study and restoration of panel painting.

During the 1930s Procacci combined his work as a civil servant with an intense scholarly activity, publishing in the Rivista d'Arte and other journals studies on Tuscan painting between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. He participated in the organization of major exhibitions such as the 1933 Tesoro di Firenze sacra and the 1937 Mostra giottesca, for which he carried out systematic surveys of churches, pievi and minor collections in the Florentine countryside, including the Mugello. These campaigns led to the identification and "rediscovery" of many neglected medieval and early Renaissance works, which Procacci studied and often re-attributed on the basis of stylistic and documentary analysis.

From an early stage, Procacci was recognized as a specialist in medieval and early Renaissance painting, particularly in the Tuscan and Florentine context. His research addressed both major figures — Giotto, Masaccio, Vasari — and lesser-known masters, insisting on the importance of documentary evidence and on the study of the "life" of works of art, including their later alterations and restorations. At the same time, he developed an innovative conception of restoration, which he helped to ground on scientific, technical and historical bases through the work of the Florentine restoration laboratory.

Under his direction, the Gabinetto dei Restauri became a pioneering institution in which art historians, chemists, photographers and restorers collaborated closely. Among the best-known episodes of his technical and connoisseurship acumen is the discovery, during investigations on Masaccio's Trinità in Santa Maria Novella, of the painted skeleton underlying the lower part of the fresco, a memento mori long hidden by later interventions. In other cases, like the cleaning of the Madonna with Child from San Giovanni Battista a Remole, the laboratory under Procacci experimented with mechanical cleaning techniques using a scalpel, inaugurating practices that would become widely discussed in the theory of restoration.

His approach to restoration sought to "return to the work its authentic image" through the removal of repaintings and accretions, but also insisted on respect for the material history of the object and on prudence in reconstruction, often opting for neutral reintegration and reversible solutions. Because of these contributions, later accounts describe him as one of the founders of modern scientific restoration in Italy and as a fundamental reference point for debates on conservation ethics.

Procacci's career as a civil servant and historian intersected dramatically with the events of the Second World War and the flood of 1966. During the war he was among the Italian officials who organized the transfer and protection of artworks from Florence and Tuscany, actions that later earned him recognition as a kind of civilian "monuments man". After the devastating flood of the Arno in November 1966, by then Soprintendente of Florence, he was among the first to rush to Santa Croce, where Cimabue's great Crocifisso and many other works had been gravely damaged.

In his report to the Ministry of Education of 30 November 1966, Procacci described the destruction as worse than any "più infausta previsione" and recounted how, in the absence of fire brigade support, the staff of the Soprintendenza and the restorers had to work alone in the mud to dismantle and secure the crucifix. He took the crucial decision, later considered decisive, to carry out all restoration work in Florence, despite difficulties and international offers of help from laboratories abroad. This choice ensured homogeneity of methods, central coordination of operations and the creation, in Florence, of an extraordinary experimental hub that accelerated the development of restoration techniques and research.

In the decades after the flood, Procacci continued to oversee restoration campaigns and to publish on the results, helping to shape both the practical and theoretical framework within which Italian conservation was reinvented. His role as Soprintendente and as a public intellectual made him a central figure in discussions on the relationship between modernization, urban planning and the protection of historic centres, especially in Florence.

Alongside his activity as official and restorer, Ugo Procacci was also a prolific scholar and editor. He published numerous studies on Florentine and Tuscan art from the Trecento to the Seicento, many of them in the Rivista d'Arte, a journal with which his career was closely intertwined and which he eventually directed. His writings combined connoisseurship, archival research and technical observation, often reattributing works, clarifying workshop practices and reconstructing the historical contexts of commissions.

Even after his retirement from the Soprintendenza in 1970, Procacci continued to work as a professor at the University of Florence, where he transmitted his integrated vision of art history — concerned with the material, social and institutional life of artworks — to new generations of scholars. Colleagues and pupils later described him as a master of a history of art attentive to "la vita degli artisti e delle opere in tutti i loro aspetti", from creation to conservation and reception. His essays were later collected and re-read in volumes such as Tra Giotto e Vasari, which emphasize his contribution to understanding the long continuity of Florentine artistic traditions.

Recognition of his importance continued after his death: in 2006 the library of the Opificio delle Pietre Dure in Florence was named "Biblioteca Ugo Procacci" in honour of his role in founding the first modern restoration laboratory in Italy. A prize for young scholars in the field of art history and conservation, the "Premio Ugo Procacci", further testifies to his lasting prestige and to the desire to link his name to innovation in research.

Ugo Procacci died in Florence on 19 February 1991. By then he was widely regarded as one of the foremost Italian art historians of the twentieth century, particularly in the field of medieval and early Renaissance painting, and as a founding father of scientific conservation in Italy. His connoisseurial discoveries contributed to the re-evaluation and "rediscovery" of many medieval works and contexts, both in the great museums and in minor ecclesiastical settings.

Perhaps even more important is the model he offered of an art historian who is simultaneously scholar, civil servant and custodian of a city's heritage, actively engaged in protecting and rescuing works in times of crisis. Thanks to his decisions during the flood and to his long work at the Gabinetto dei Restauri, Florence became an international centre of excellence for restoration, attracting expertise and resources from around the world. For these reasons, recent commemorations in Florence and in the scholarly world continue to present him as a "custode di Firenze" and as a reference figure for those working at the intersection of medieval art history and conservation.

Ragghianti, Carlo Ludovico

Lucca, 1910 – Florence, 1987

Carlo Ludovico Ragghianti (Lucca, March 18, 1910 - Florence, August 3, 1987) was one of the leading Italian art historians, critics, and theorists of the twentieth century. His career united rigorous methodological reflection with an exceptional level of civic and political engagement, making him a central figure in modern Italian intellectual life.

He received his early formation in Lucca and developed a precocious anti-fascist stance by the mid-1930s. He then moved to Pisa, studying art history at the Scuola Normale Superiore and the University of Pisa in an environment of high critical discipline. Giovanni Gentile, despite deep ideological differences, recognized Ragghianti's intellectual gifts and supported him at the start of his career. In 1935, together with Ranuccio Bianchi Bandinelli and with Gentile's backing, he founded Critica d'Arte, a journal that also involved Roberto Longhi and became one of the key instruments for methodological renewal in postwar Italian art history.

Ragghianti's most original contribution was his formulation of a rigorous aesthetic historicism, grounded in both formal analysis and historical interpretation. He rejected both pure formalism and reductive social-documentary readings of art, seeking instead to reconstruct the internal development of visual language over time. In contrast to approaches centered on stylistic rupture, he emphasized continuity and progression, a perspective that shaped his reevaluation of neglected periods, including nineteenth-century Italian art, and also informed his long-range inquiry into prehistoric visual culture, culminating in L'uomo cosciente, arte e conoscenza nella paleostoria (1981).

His biography was inseparable from political action. During the Resistance he became one of the main leaders in Tuscany, heading the Actionist Military Committee, founding and commanding the Rosselli Brigades until June 1944, and then presiding over the Tuscan National Liberation Committee. In this role he directed the provisional government that led the uprising against Nazi-Fascist forces in Florence, liberated on August 11, 1944. In 1945 he served as Undersecretary of Education in the Parri government, with responsibility for Fine Arts and Performing Arts, promoting urgent measures for postwar cultural reconstruction.

His institutional work remained marked by a reformist vision. In 1957 he founded the Cabinet of Drawings and Prints at the University of Pisa, developing it into both a major twentieth-century graphic collection and a center for research and exhibitions. In 1964 he founded and directed the Italian Society of Archaeology and Art History to strengthen collaboration between university and heritage administration. In 1967 he curated the landmark exhibition Arte moderna in Italia 1915-1935 at Palazzo Strozzi, a major application of his historical-critical method to twentieth-century art.

Ragghianti's legacy is preserved through the Fondazione Centro Studi sull'Arte Licia e Carlo Ludovico Ragghianti in Lucca, established according to his and his wife Licia's wishes as a permanent institution for research, exhibitions, and cultural dissemination. His archival papers, conserved in the Tuscan State Archives, remain a primary source for the history of twentieth-century Italian art criticism. He continues to be an essential reference point for scholars studying the relationship between artistic interpretation, public responsibility, and intellectual vocation in modern Europe.

Romagnoli, Ettore

Siena, 22 October 1772 - Siena, 13 January 1838

Ettore Romagnoli was one of the most versatile figures in early nineteenth-century Sienese culture: art historian, musicologist, composer, and interpreter of the city's heritage for Italian and foreign visitors. For scholars of medieval Sienese art, he remains an indispensable historical source, especially for the reconstruction of local artistic biographies and archival traditions.

Born in Siena, he remained closely tied to the city throughout his life and also performed a quasi-diplomatic cultural role, accompanying foreign dignitaries on behalf of local noble circles. He was a member of the Societa Colombaria Fiorentina, one of the major learned societies of Grand Ducal Italy, and collaborated with the music theorist Carlo Gervasoni on the drafting of the Nuova teoria di musica.

His magnum opus is the autograph manuscript Biografia cronologica de' Bellartisti senesi dal secolo XII a tutto il XVIII, organized in twelve volumes plus a thirteenth volume of indexes, for a total of roughly 11,000 pages. Donated by the author to the Municipal Library of Siena in 1835, it gathers biographies of painters, sculptors, architects, and artisans, with documentary transcriptions, archival references, and critical observations. The first volume, dedicated to artists active between 1100 and 1300, is directly relevant to research on Duecento and Trecento Siena.

The manuscript was used by contemporaries such as Rumohr, Gaye, and Milanesi, later noted by Julius von Schlosser in Letteratura artistica (1924), and consulted by John Pope-Hennessy in his 1937 study of Giovanni di Paolo. After long periods of relative neglect, it has returned to the center of scholarship thanks to the complete digital transcription project promoted by Fondazione Memofonte. A facsimile reprint in thirteen volumes was published by S.P.E.S. (Studio per Edizioni Scelte) in 1976.

Among his printed works, Romagnoli published the Guide to the City of Siena for Lovers of the Fine Arts (Siena, Marco Ferri e Figlio, 1832), one of the earliest systematic guides to the city's artistic heritage. Posthumously appeared the Historical and Artistic Notes on Siena and Its Suburbs (Siena, Onorato Porri, 1840), republished in a third edition in 1852 with a biographical essay on the author and his writings. Together with the manuscript corpus, these works confirm his role as a foundational mediator between local erudition and modern art-historical method.

Another strong line of work concerns Lombard art under the Visconti and Sforza, including studies on arte lombarda and the role of court patronage in the later Middle Ages and early Renaissance.

Romano has published monographs and edited volumes with major Italian publishers such as Jaca Book and Vita e Pensiero, and her titles appear in both scholarly and museum-oriented catalogues. She has also curated or co-curated exhibitions, for example the 2015 show Arte lombarda dai Visconti agli Sforza, which brought together paintings, manuscripts, and decorative arts to trace the development of Lombard visual culture under those dynasties. Her bibliography ranges from technical studies of individual fresco cycles to broader synthetic accounts of Roman and Italian medieval art, making her a key reference for students and researchers working on Italian medieval painting and iconography.

Rotili, Mario

Benevento, 12 August 1920 - Benevento, 26 February 1981

Mario Rotili was one of the most multifaceted figures in twentieth-century Southern Italian culture: a historian of medieval and modern art, museologist, librarian, archivist, and politician, he combined a distinguished academic career with direct civic commitment to the reconstruction of his city. His figure uniquely combines scientific research with administrative responsibility, in an intellectual profile reminiscent of certain civic humanists of the fifteenth century.

He was born in Benevento on August 12, 1920, and spent most of his life there. After attending a classical high school in his hometown, he enrolled in the Faculty of Letters at the University of Naples, where he earned his degree and took his first steps in art-historical research. He also began teaching art history at the classical high school in Benevento and contributed to local newspapers with articles on literary, theatrical, and film criticism, revealing from a young age a cultural openness that would characterize his entire body of work.

A member of the Christian Democrats, Rotili entered city politics at a delicate moment: between 1952 and 1956 he led the opposition to right-wing administrations, and in 1956 he was elected mayor of Benevento, a position he held for seven years, until June 1963. His term was marked by a measured and careful policy of reconstruction of the areas destroyed by Anglo-American bombing in 1943, with a General Urban Plan entrusted to the renowned urban planner Luigi Piccinato. He took stock of that period in the book Seven Years in the Service of My City (Benevento, Abete, 1963), and for his civic commitment he was awarded the title of Grand Officer of the Order of Merit of the Italian Republic. Nearly ninety percent of the City Council's resolutions were approved unanimously during his tenure, a sign of his exceptional ability to mediate.

In 1959, while still serving as mayor, Rotili assumed the directorship of the city's cultural institutions: the Museo del Sannio, the Provincial Library, and the Provincial Historical Archives of Benevento. During his eleven years as director, until 1970, he completely reorganized the museum, established an art gallery within it, divided it into four specialized sections, and promoted exhibitions, conferences, concerts, and meetings with leading figures in Italian and international culture, envisioning the museum as a public hub of cultural life and not merely a repository of objects. He oversaw the restoration of the rich collection of parchment materials preserved in the museum's holdings, and for this work he was awarded the Gold Medal of Merit for Education, Culture, and the Arts by presidential decree in 1965.

After obtaining the title of Lecturer in Medieval and Modern Art History, Rotili initially taught at the Academy of Fine Arts in Naples, then at the University of Naples as an adjunct professor, and finally as a full professor, holding the chair of History of Miniature and Minor Arts and subsequently of Medieval and Modern Art History. He also taught at the Suor Orsola Benincasa University Institute of Education in Naples and was a lecturer in the Advanced Culture Courses at the Italian University for Foreigners in Perugia. A full member of the Pontaniana Academy and the National Society of Sciences, Letters, and Arts of Naples, he was also an Honorary Academician of the Pontifical Insigne Academy of the Virtuosi al Pantheon.

The core of Rotili's work is the history of medieval art in southern Italy, with Benevento and the Lombard duchy at the center of his focus. His first major volume, L'Arte nel Sannio (Benevento, 1952), won the Premio Napoli for Art History in 1956. This was followed by a series of studies on early medieval architecture and sculpture in the Benevento area, culminating in the monumental edition of L'Arco di Traiano a Benevento (Rome, Istituto Poligrafico dello Stato, 1972), which examined one of the most important monuments of classical art in Southern Italy. The volume Byzantine Art in Calabria and Basilicata (Cava dei Tirreni, Di Mauro, 1980) broadened the scope to encompass the landscape of late antique and medieval art in southern Italy. Rotili also contributed to the Corpus della scultura altomedioevale edited by the Italian Center for Studies on the Early Middle Ages in Spoleto, for which he authored the volume dedicated to the Diocese of Benevento (1966).

A second, equally original line of research concerns the history of miniature painting: Rotili was among the first Italian scholars to devote systematic research to book illustration in medieval Southern Italy. His major works in this field include La miniatura gotica in Italia (Naples, Libreria Scientifica, 1968-1969), Introduzione alla storia della miniatura e delle arti minori in Italia (1970, with Antonella Putaturo Murano), The Illuminated Dante Manuscripts of Naples (1972) and, above all, the two-volume work Miniature Painting in the Abbey of Cava (Cava dei Tirreni, Di Mauro, 1976-1978), dedicated to the Benedictine scriptorium of the Abbey of the Trinity in Cava. In 1963, he published Origins of Italian Painting (Bergamo, Italian Institute of Graphic Arts), a richly illustrated volume offering an overview of Italian painting from its Christian origins through the High Middle Ages, in which he included, among others, G. Salvi's thesis on the true identity of the painter Conxolus of the Sacro Speco in Subiaco.

His interest in architecture is also evident in his studies on Filippo Raguzzini, the Neapolitan architect of the Roman Rococo, to whom Rotili dedicated a volume in 1951 and a posthumous concluding essay in 1982; and in the series of studies on Luigi Vanvitelli, beginning in 1954, which culminated in the critical edition of the Vita di Luigi Vanvitelli written by his son (Naples, Banca di Roma-S.E.N., 1975). He contributed numerous entries to the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani and the Enciclopedia Dantesca, and contributed to the major collective work L'art dans l'Italie meridionale, the updated edition of Emile Bertaux's monumental work edited by Adriano Prandi (Rome, Ecole francaise, 1978).

Mario Rotili died in his hometown of Benevento on February 26, 1981, at the age of sixty. One year after his passing, the City Administration, the Provincial Administration, and the Rotary Club organized an official commemoration, the proceedings of which were published in 1987. In 1984, friends and students published Studi di Storia dell'Arte in memoria di Mario Rotili (Naples, Banca Sannitica, 2 vols., 954 pp.), a miscellaneous collection featuring his complete bibliography, edited by Alessandra Perriccioli Saggese, and a tribute by the great historian Giuseppe Galasso. The City of Benevento later named a street in his honor.

Salvini, Roberto

Modena, 1912 – Florence, 1985

Roberto Salvini was one of the most important Italian art historians of the twentieth century, making fundamental contributions to the study of medieval art, Romanesque sculpture, and Flemish painting. A student of the medievalist Mario Salmi, Salvini received his education in Florence, Munich, and Berlin, acquiring a European methodological perspective that would characterize his entire body of work. In 1937, he joined the Fine Arts administration, serving as superintendent of galleries in various locations — Trento, Palermo, and Modena — during the years of World War II. His institutional career culminated in his directorship of the Uffizi Gallery in Florence, where he promoted significant museographic innovations.

In 1956, Salvini began his university career as a professor at the University of Trieste, before moving to the University of Florence, where he taught for many years. He was actively engaged in the methodology of teaching art history, a topic he had already addressed in his theoretical essay La pura visibilità (1947), which reflected the influence of the German formalist tradition on his education. In 1983, he was elected a National Member of the Accademia dei Lincei, the highest academic honour in Italy. He also served as President of the Art History Section of the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno in Florence and held the position of Secretary General.

His studies ranged from Italian medieval art to the Renaissance, from Flemish art to European Romanesque sculpture. Among his most significant works in medieval studies are Medieval Mosaics in Sicily (1949), Wiligelmo and the Origins of Romanesque Sculpture in Europe (1956) — a pioneering study on the origins of Romanesque sculpture — and The Cloister of Monreale (1962). He also made significant contributions to Byzantine studies, writing seminal volumes such as The Cathedral of Modena (1966) and the volume Medieval Sculpture for the international series History of Western Sculpture.

A recurring theme throughout his work was his interest in the relationship between Italian patrons and Flemish painting, explored both in Flemish Painting (1958) and in his last published work, Florentine Bankers and Flemish Painters (1985), released in the year of his death. Salvini left a profound mark on half a century of national and international art history, and in 2018 and 2022 his legacy was celebrated with conferences and publications dedicated to his scholarly contributions.

Sandberg-Vavalà, Evelyn

Compton, Berkshire, 1888 – Florence, 8 September 1961

Evelyn Sandberg-Vavalà was born Evelyn May Graham Sandberg in Compton, Berkshire, in 1888. She was the only child of the Reverend George Alfred Sandberg (1848-1910), vicar of the church of Saints Mary and Nicholas, and Annie Sandberg (1858-1894), who died when Evelyn was still a child. She studied geography and went on to teach in a girls' grammar school and at a university college — an education and career path typical of the small number of professionally active women of her generation in Britain. She was also known by her married name, Evelyn Kendrew, though it was her pseudonym, Sandberg-Vavalà, that she used exclusively in her scholarly life.

In 1921 Sandberg-Vavalà made the decisive choice of her intellectual life: she moved to Florence, where she would remain for thirty-five years — interrupted only by the years of the Second World War. In Florence she placed herself under the guidance of Bernard Berenson, the great Lithuanian-American connoisseur and art historian who had established his villa I Tatti as the pre-eminent centre for the study of Italian Renaissance painting. Under Berenson's influence she developed into a rigorous scholar of iconography and early Italian painting, and she established close intellectual relationships with the most important art historians of the age: John Pope-Hennessy, Roberto Longhi, and Giuseppe Fiocco. She became well known in Florence not only as a researcher but also as a tutor and guide for students of art at the Galleria degli Uffizi, accompanying them on visits throughout Italy.

Her first and most enduring major work was published in 1929: La Croce Dipinta Italiana e l'Iconografia della Passione (Italian Painted Crucifixes and the Iconography of the Passion). This book remains a foundational reference in the study of the medieval painted cross — the croce dipinta — a distinctly Italian devotional form that was central to the visual culture of the Duecento and Trecento. For the first time, Sandberg-Vavalà subjected the entire corpus of surviving Italian painted crucifixes to systematic iconographic and stylistic analysis, tracing the development of the form from the Christus Triumphans of the Romanesque period to the suffering Christus Patiens of the Gothic age. Her work in this field was so foundational that a full century later, the Römisches Jahrbuch of the Bibliotheca Hertziana published a special section entitled Aspekte der Tafelkreuzforschung im Jahrhundert nach Evelyn Sandberg-Vavalà — "Aspects of panel cross research in the century after Evelyn Sandberg-Vavalà", a tribute to the lasting relevance of her pioneering study.

In 1934 she published her study on the iconography of the Virgin and Child in thirteenth-century Italian painting: L'iconografia della Madonna col bambino nella pittura italiana del Dugento, a work that brought the same systematic iconographic method she had applied to the crucifix tradition to bear on the Madonna type. She also made important contributions to the study of Venetian Trecento painting: she is cited by scholars of Venetian art — alongside Roberto Longhi — for her analyses of the workshop of Paolo Veneziano and his followers. Her 1926 monograph La pittura veronese del Trecento established her as one of the first scholars to treat Veronese medieval painting with the rigour it deserved.

In the postwar years Sandberg-Vavalà produced two major synthetic works that confirmed her reputation as the leading English-language authority on central Italian medieval and early Renaissance painting. Uffizi Studies: The Development of the Florentine School of Painting appeared in 1948, followed by Sienese Studies: The Development of the School of Painting of Siena in 1953. Both works combined iconographic analysis with careful attention to stylistic development and workshop tradition, and both remain useful reference tools. Her final monograph, Studies in Florentine Churches (Olschki, Florence, 1959), extended the investigation to the specific devotional and institutional contexts in which Florentine painting was produced and displayed.

One of the most remarkable legacies of Sandberg-Vavalà's scholarly life was her photographic archive of over 25,000 photographs of Italian paintings from the thirteenth to the sixteenth centuries. This archive, which she assembled and organised over four decades, constitutes an extraordinary documentary resource covering medieval mosaics and frescoes, Romanesque sculpture, and applied arts including cassoni, ivories, and jewellery. From 1957, aware of her declining health, Federico Zeri worked to ensure that the archive would be preserved intact. He persuaded Count Vittorio Cini to purchase it, and the archive passed after her death to the Fondazione Giorgio Cini in Venice, where it is preserved today as the Fondo Sandberg-Vavalà. A further portion of her photographic materials is held at the Fondazione Federico Zeri at the Università di Bologna.

Sandberg-Vavalà died in Florence on 8 September 1961, cared for in her final illness by the nuns of a religious community. She was buried in the small cemetery of Moggiona, a hamlet of Poppi in the Casentino, the forested valley southeast of Florence that she evidently loved. She was seventy-three years old. Her scholarly achievement was remarkable not only for its range and depth but for the era in which it was accomplished: as a self-taught British woman working in Florence in the interwar years, without a university chair or institutional support, she produced a body of work that shaped the study of medieval Italian painting for generations. The centenary of her great work on the painted crucifix has been marked by new international scholarship, the best possible tribute to an historian whose questions have not yet been fully answered.

Schapiro, Meyer

Šiauliai, 1904 – New York, 1996

Pioneering American art historian, critic, and teacher whose interdisciplinary approach revolutionised the study of medieval, Romanesque, and modern art. Born on September 23, 1904, in Šiauliai, Lithuania, he immigrated to the United States with his family in 1907, settling in New York City, where he was profoundly shaped by the city's vibrant intellectual and artistic milieu.

Schapiro attended Columbia University, earning his bachelor's degree in 1924, his master's in 1926, and his Ph.D. in 1929 with a groundbreaking dissertation on twelfth-century Romanesque sculpture in Burgundy, which challenged prevailing stylistic chronologies and emphasised socio-political contexts. His early work — including articles on Mozarabic art and cloister frescoes at Santo Domingo de Silos — established him as a meticulous scholar who integrated formal analysis with historical, theological, and economic factors, methods that contrasted sharply with the era's more rigid iconographic or formalist schools. Joining Columbia's faculty in 1928, he taught there for over fifty years, mentoring figures including Arthur Danto and Robert Rosenblum, and fostering dialogues between art history, Marxism, psychoanalysis, and phenomenology.

His seminal publications include Romanesque Art (1977) and essays collected in Words and Pictures (1973), which dissected the emotional expressivity in medieval sculpture — arguing, for instance, that the "chaotic" sculptural heads at Moissac reflected deliberate spiritual intensity rather than primitivism — as well as later monographs on Van Gogh (1950) and Cézanne (1952), which humanised modern masters by linking their innovations to personal psychology and cultural upheaval. His insistence on viewing art as a dynamic interplay of form, content, and human agency — epitomised in his critique of Erwin Panofsky's iconology — bridged medieval monastic traditions with twentieth-century abstraction, influencing fields from semiotics to New York School painting; he was personally acquainted with artists including Willem de Kooning. He died on March 26, 1996, leaving an enduring corpus that taught generations to see art not as isolated objects but as vital expressions of historical consciousness.

Sirén, Osvald

Helsinki, April 6, 1879 – Stockholm, June 26, 1966

Osvald Sirén was one of the most important and versatile art historians of the twentieth century, a figure bridging the European tradition of connoisseurship and the new horizons of scientific Orientalism, and the author of a monumental body of work that uniquely encompasses both medieval and Renaissance Italian painting and Chinese art.

Born in Helsinki on April 6, 1879, into Finland's Swedish-speaking minority, Sirén grew up in a culturally vibrant environment open to the most advanced European intellectual currents. He completed his university studies in art history, training in the tradition of the connoisseurship method of Morelli and Berenson, which in those years dominated the discipline throughout Europe. From the outset, his scholarly work was deeply influenced by the formalist methods of Bernard Berenson, with whom he shared an interest in medieval Tuscan painters and Leonardo da Vinci, aiming to situate works within their historical context without making a priori aesthetic judgments.

In 1908, Sirén was appointed to the J.A. Berg Chair of Art History and Theory at the University of Stockholm, thus becoming the first professor of art history in Sweden, a distinction that attests both to his exceptionally early academic success and to the youth of the discipline as an independent university subject in the Nordic world. He held that chair until 1923, devoting himself with great systematic rigor to the study of medieval and Renaissance Italian painting. During this period, he produced fundamental contributions: in 1905, he proposed attributing a painting preserved at the Bandini Museum in Fiesole to the Florentine painter and illuminator Lorenzo Monaco, a proposal that ushered in a new era of studies on late Gothic Florentine art. He published studies on Giotto, on the early Tuscan and Pisan painters, and was among the first to attempt a systematic organization of the corpus of the Maestro di Tedice, the anonymous thirteenth-century Pisan painter whose stylistic identification Sirén proposed by grouping under that conventional name a coherent body of thirteenth-century works.

Sirén belongs to that extraordinary generation of art historians born between 1870 and 1890 who transformed the discipline: he corresponded with Lionello Venturi and shared with him an interest in medieval Tuscan painters and Leonardo. Like Venturi, Sirén was influenced by the aesthetic thought of Benedetto Croce, by Alois Riegl's Kunstwollen, the concept of "artistic will" as a key to interpreting style, and by Berenson's Kennerschaft. His intellectual circle also included Tancred Borenius and Onni Okkonen, other Finnish art historians working internationally with similar methodologies. He was also a member of the theosophical movement, of which he was among the first adherents in the Scandinavian region, and this spiritual sensitivity toward Eastern traditions likely paved the way for his subsequent turn to Chinese art.

After World War I, Sirén made the most surprising intellectual shift of his career: he gradually abandoned the history of Italian art to devote himself increasingly exclusively to Chinese art, a field that was virtually unexplored by Western historiography at the time. This change of course was not sudden: the scholarly method he had applied to Italian primitives, meticulous formal analysis, classification by hand, and the construction of attributive corpora, proved extraordinarily well suited to the study of a figurative tradition that was itself extremely rich and still largely unknown in the West. From 1928 to 1945, he served as Curator of Paintings and Sculptures at the Nationalmuseum in Stockholm, thus combining his research activities with a leading role in museum operations.

The fruits of Sirén's Orientalist period were of extraordinary magnitude. In 1924, he published The Walls and Gates of Peking, a documentary work on imperial Beijing. In 1926, Chinese Sculpture: Fifth to Fourteenth Centuries was published in five volumes, accompanied by an impressive collection of photographs. In 1929, he published A History of Early Chinese Painting, published by the Medici Society in London. In 1935, he published La scultura e la pittura cinesi in Italian for ISMEO in Rome. His magnum opus was Chinese Painting: Leading Masters and Principles (1950-1958), a seven-volume work that compiled, updated, and systematized decades of research, becoming the indispensable reference text for anyone studying Chinese painting in the English-speaking world. He also taught courses on Chinese art at Harvard University, bringing his research to the heart of American academia.

In 1956, Sirén was awarded the Charles Lang Freer Medal, the most prestigious American honor in the field of Asian art studies, presented by the Smithsonian Institution, an award that cemented his status as the world's foremost authority in the field. He died in Stockholm on June 26, 1966, at the age of eighty-seven, leaving a bibliographic legacy of rare breadth and quality.

Osvald Sirén stands out for his dual excellence: very few art historians have achieved leading positions in two fields as diverse as medieval Italian painting and Chinese art. For scholars of Italian medieval art, his contributions to fourteenth-century studies, on Pisan painted crosses, the Maestro di Tedice, Lorenzo Monaco, and fourteenth-century Florentine frescoes, remain historiographical reference points that are still discussed and cited. For the world of East Asian studies, his monumental work on Chinese painting still retains irreplaceable documentary value today, the result of decades of travel, access to private and public collections, and a systematic application of Western connoisseurship to a visual tradition of extraordinary complexity.

Stubblebine, James H.

1920 – 1987

James H. Stubblebine was an American art historian who specialized in early Italian painting, especially the Sienese school of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. He is regarded as one of the leading scholars of Italian Duecento and Trecento painting and made a major contribution to the institutional development of art history as a discipline in the United States.

Educated at Harvard University, where he graduated in 1942, Stubblebine became professor of art history at Rutgers University and was later named Distinguished Professor II. He helped build Rutgers' graduate program in art history and also taught at New York University's Institute of Fine Arts in the late 1970s and 1980s. His long-standing association with major American research institutions and museums, including the National Gallery of Art, anchored his work in both scholarly and curatorial practice.

Stubblebine's primary research focused on thirteenth- and fourteenth-century Italian panel painting, with particular emphasis on Duccio di Buoninsegna and his circle in Siena. His monumental two-volume study Duccio di Buoninsegna and His School combines iconographic, stylistic, and document-based analysis with an extensive photographic archive, reproducing essentially all of Duccio's surviving works and many by his contemporaries. He also published extensively on related figures such as Coppo di Marcovaldo and Guido da Siena, and his essays on Byzantine influence and the development of throne imagery in Duecento Tuscan painting remain frequently cited in debates on Italo-Byzantine transition.

For scholars working on twelfth- to fourteenth-century Italian painting and illuminated manuscripts, Stubblebine's work remains essential for understanding chronology, workshop practice, and stylistic evolution in late Duecento and early Trecento Siena. His method, combining meticulous formal analysis with documentary and archival research supported by systematic photographic evidence, continues to serve as a model for attributional and school-based studies in medieval Italian art.

Strzygowski, Josef Rudolph Thomas

Biala (Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria), 7 March 1862 - Vienna, 2 January 1941

Josef Rudolph Thomas Strzygowski was a Polish-Austrian art historian and one of the most controversial and provocative scholars of his era, celebrated for radically expanding the geographical horizons of art history while simultaneously drawing condemnation for his ideologically compromised later work. He is remembered as a central figure of the Vienna School of Art History and, arguably, Europe's first professor of non-Western art history.

Strzygowski was born on March 7, 1862, in Biala, in the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria (present-day Poland, then part of the Austrian Empire). His mother, Edle Trass von Friedelfeldt, came from minor nobility, while his father was a cloth manufacturer. Following family tradition, the young Strzygowski began an apprenticeship at a weaving plant in 1880, but abandoned this path in 1882 to enroll at the University of Vienna. He soon transferred to the University of Munich, where he immersed himself in art history and completed a dissertation on the iconography of the Baptism of Christ, published in 1885 under the title Ikonographie der Taufe Christi.

After completing his studies, Strzygowski spent three years in Rome, where he produced Cimabue und Rom (1887), an early and significant work that emphasized the Byzantine sources underlying the painting of Cimabue, a subject of direct relevance to the study of medieval Italian art. This work already signaled his lifelong conviction that the roots of Western European art lay not in Rome alone but in the broader Eastern Mediterranean world. He subsequently traveled to Thessaloniki, Mount Athos, Saint Petersburg, and Moscow, deepening his knowledge of Byzantine and Russian art.

In 1892, Strzygowski was appointed to the faculty of the University of Graz. Between 1894 and 1895, he lived in Cairo, where he studied early Byzantine and Islamic art in Egypt and compiled a landmark catalogue of Coptic art held in the Cairo Museum. This fieldwork positioned him as a pioneer in areas that were then largely unexplored by Western scholars, and his expertise soon attracted the attention of major institutions: Wilhelm von Bode, director of the Berlin Museums, enlisted Strzygowski to help build the Byzantine collection before the opening of the Kaiser-Friedrich Museum in 1904, expanding the holdings from fewer than 100 to approximately 2,000 objects.

The most intellectually explosive moment of Strzygowski's career came in 1901, with the publication of Orient oder Rom: Beitrage zur Geschichte der spatantiken und fruhchristlichen Kunst ("The Orient or Rome: Contributions to the History of Late Antique and Early Christian Art"). In this polemical work, he directly challenged the dominant paradigm that Rome was the fountainhead of early Christian and medieval art, arguing instead that the Near East, particularly Syria, Mesopotamia, Armenia, and Egypt, played the decisive formative role. This thesis shook the foundations of the discipline and ignited a scholarly debate that resonated for decades.

Upon the death of Franz Wickhoff, Strzygowski was appointed in 1909 to his successor's chair at the University of Vienna, a position secured in part through the breadth of his research and, reportedly, the advocacy of Archduke Franz Ferdinand. From this prestigious platform, he continued to publish prolifically on Byzantium, Islam, Armenia, Norse art, and Slavic visual culture, developing a sweeping, non-Eurocentric theoretical framework for art history that was radical for its time.

His two-volume Die Baukunst der Armenier und Europa (1918), "The Architecture of the Armenians and Europe," advanced the daring claim that the origins of Gothic architecture could be traced to Armenian building traditions, a thesis that was largely rejected by subsequent scholarship. He also entered a notable controversy with the archaeologist Ernst Herzfeld over the origins of the Mshatta facade; in this dispute, Herzfeld's position was ultimately vindicated.

Strzygowski retired from the University of Vienna in 1933 and founded the Gesellschaft fur vergleichende Kunstforschung ("Society for Comparative Art History") in 1934 to propagate his theoretical views. Tragically, in his final years he embraced the nationalist and racist ideology of National Socialism, a drift that scholars have interpreted not merely as political opportunism but as the disturbing logical conclusion of the ethnic and racial categories that had gradually infiltrated his art historical thinking.

He died on January 2, 1941, in Vienna. His private library was bequeathed to the Art History Department of the University of Vienna, where it remains.

Despite his methodological inconsistencies and the deep ideological stain on his later work, Strzygowski's intellectual legacy is complex and cannot be dismissed outright. His insistence on looking beyond the Greco-Roman world helped legitimize Islamic art, Coptic art, and non-Western visual traditions as serious fields of academic inquiry. Several of his most gifted students, most notably Otto Demus and Fritz Novotny, successfully pursued these broadened horizons without adopting their teacher's ideology. Today, in an era of global art history and decolonized museum practice, Strzygowski's restless geographical curiosity finds a kind of posthumous vindication, even as his racial theorizing remains a cautionary chapter in the history of the discipline.

Tambini Strocchi, Anna

c. 1942 - 2025

Anna Tambini Strocchi was one of the most authoritative Italian scholars of medieval and Renaissance art in the Romagna region, particularly celebrated for her pioneering studies on the Giottesque School of Rimini and on manuscript illumination between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. She died at the age of 82, mourned by the cultural community of Faenza and beyond.

Tambini studied Art History at the Universita di Bologna under the guidance of Carlo Volpe, obtaining her Diploma di perfezionamento in Storia dell'Arte in 1979. Her graduation thesis became the foundation for her first major book, Pittura dall'Alto Medioevo al Tardogotico nel territorio di Faenza e Forli (Faenza, 1982), which established her as a rigorous and original voice in the study of Romagnole art history.

Her primary field was the art of the Middle Ages and Renaissance in Romagna, though she also devoted significant attention to eighteenth-century painting. Among her most appreciated contributions are studies on the Giottesque Riminese School, on Forli's medieval painting tradition, on Late Gothic art in Romagna, and on manuscript illumination in Romagna from the Duecento to the Quattrocento.

Over the course of her career, Tambini produced more than one hundred publications in specialized journals, including international venues. She contributed to the catalogues of the Pinacoteca di Ferrara and the Pinacoteca di Bologna, and she curated the medieval and Renaissance painting section in the catalogue of the Pinacoteca Comunale di Ravenna (2001). She also wrote entries for the Enciclopedia Treccani and the German Kunstler Lexikon.

Her most ambitious publishing project was a three-volume series on the history of figurative arts in Faenza: Le origini (2006), Il Gotico (2007), and Il Rinascimento (2009), followed by a contribution on Faentine painters of the first half of the sixteenth century in a fourth volume. These works, together with her cataloguing activity at the Biblioteca di Faenza and her contributions to the five-volume series on Faenza's historic neighborhoods edited by Lorenzo Savelli, form a major scholarly corpus on local art history.

In 2001 she also co-authored with Morelli and Tabanelli Quella chiesa nata dalle acque. L'oratorio di Croce Coperta a Lugo, a focused study on a lesser-known but artistically significant oratory in the Ravenna province.

Tambini lectured at the universities of Trieste and Urbino and supervised theses in both institutions. She presented papers at symposia in Mercatello, Tolentino, Vignola, Camerino, and Urbino, reflecting the breadth of her recognized authority across central Italy. In 2021 she received the honorary distinction Faentina Lontana, conferred by the city of Faenza.

Her legacy is that of a meticulous and passionate researcher who dedicated her scholarly life to recovering and illuminating an artistic heritage that might otherwise have remained largely forgotten.

Tartuferi, Angelo

Florence, 16 August 1957

Angelo Tartuferi is one of Italy's most authoritative art historians in the field of medieval and early Renaissance Tuscan painting, with a particular focus on the period from the thirteenth to the fifteenth century. His scholarship combines connoisseurship, archival discipline, and museum practice, and has made him a major reference point in studies of Florentine painting between the Duecento and the Quattrocento.

Decisive to his formation was also his collaboration with Miklós Boskovits, a leading scholar of medieval Florentine painting at the Germanic Institute of Art History in Florence, an experience he has described as a true scholarly bottega in the most concrete and demanding sense.

On 25 July 1990 he entered the Italian Ministry for Cultural Heritage as an art-historical official. He served as Deputy Director of the Galleria dell'Accademia in Florence, then moved in 2006 to the Uffizi as Director of the Department of Duecento-to-Quattrocento painting. Between 2009 and 2013 he also held the position of Deputy Director of the Uffizi. In 2013, after the retirement of Franca Falletti, he became Director of the Galleria dell'Accademia, a role he held until the 2015 ministerial reform. From 2016 to 2020 he directed the department dedicated to Michelangelesque sculpture and Duecento-to-Quattrocento painting at the same museum. From 2020 to 2024 he served as Director of the Museo di San Marco in Florence, where he oversaw, among other projects, the new installation of the room dedicated to Fra Angelico.

Todini, Filippo

Rome, 1957–

Italian art historian best known for his studies on Umbrian painting and the painting schools of Central Italy between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries. Todini specializes in Italian art history with a particular focus on Umbria and on the artistic dynamics between Bologna, Perugia, and Florence from the late Middle Ages to the modern era. His work is frequently cited in studies on minor painters, anonymous masters, and attributional problems, and he is regarded as an authority on the cataloging and reconstruction of Perugian and Umbrian workshops.

Among his best-known works is the two-volume set La pittura umbra dal Duecento al primo Cinquecento (1989), which functions as a large-scale critical survey and catalogue-style reference for Umbrian painting. He has also edited volumes such as Studi di storia dell'arte (conference proceedings and research collections) and has published studies on seventeenth-century Umbrian painting, continuing his analysis of local history, patronage structures, and workshop formation.

Todini has repeatedly intervened in attribution debates, proposing or confirming identities of "anonymous masters" and reattributing works to lesser-known painters or collaborative workshop contexts. His criteria are often cited in articles and exhibition catalogues for their close attention to stylistic details, internal workshop chronology, and the circulation of forms between Umbria and Tuscany.

Toesca, Pietro

Turin, 1877 – Rome, 1962

The preeminent Italian art historian of the medieval period and the founding figure of the systematic study of Lombard painting. After formative studies in Turin under Adolfo Venturi, Toesca held the chair of history of medieval and modern art successively at Turin and at the Università degli Studi di Roma, where he taught from 1923 until his retirement. His La pittura e la miniatura nella Lombardia dai più antichi monumenti alla metà del Quattrocento (Milan, 1912) constitutes the first comprehensive scholarly treatment of Lombard painting from late antiquity through the early Renaissance, and it established the Maestro di Galliano as a major artistic personality and defined the terms of all subsequent discussion of the Galliano frescoes. His two-volume Storia dell'arte italiana — comprising Il Medioevo (Turin, 1927) and Il Trecento (Turin, 1951) — established the canon of Italian medieval art history for much of the twentieth century and remains an indispensable reference. Toesca is cited across virtually the entire database as the ultimate scholarly authority on the pre-Duecento period.

Torriti, Piero

Montisi, 1924 – Siena, 2015

Italian art historian, critic, and museum professional who played a central role in the study and stewardship of Sienese art throughout the second half of the twentieth century. Born in the hill town of Montisi in the Sienese countryside, he developed an enduring sensitivity to local artistic traditions and devotional imagery. He studied art history at the University of Florence under Mario Salmi, receiving a formation grounded in connoisseurship, contextual analysis, and the critical use of archival sources. Entering the state art-heritage administration after graduation, he eventually became Superintendent of the Soprintendenza per i Beni Artistici e Storici for the provinces of Siena and Grosseto, a role that placed him at the intersection of conservation policy, museum management, and scholarly research. As a scholar, Torriti focused especially on Sienese painting and sculpture from the Duecento to the early modern period, studying artists such as Duccio, Jacopo della Quercia, and the Lorenzetti circle, while also addressing the devotional culture, architecture, and urban image of Siena and its territory, including Pienza and the impact of the Renaissance papacy on local commissions. Through his work as superintendent, scholar, and popularizer, he helped define the modern understanding of Sienese art and left a lasting legacy in both academic and museum circles.

Tronzo, William

active late 20th-21st century

William Tronzo received his Ph.D. in the History of Art and Architecture from Harvard University (1973-1982) and his undergraduate degree in History from Haverford College (1969-1973). After completing his doctorate he taught at Johns Hopkins University, Duke University, and Tulane University before settling at the University of California, San Diego (UCSD), where he currently holds the position of Senior Teaching Professor in the History of Art, Architecture and Landscape, and serves as Director of European Studies.

Tronzo has held research fellowships and appointments at many of the most prestigious institutions in art-historical scholarship, including the American Academy in Rome, Dumbarton Oaks in Washington, the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, the Center for Advanced Study in the Visual Arts (CASVA) at the National Gallery of Art, the Bibliotheca Hertziana in Rome, the Ecole des hautes etudes en sciences sociales in Paris, the Huntington Library, the Clark Art Institute, and the Stanford Humanities Center. This range of affiliations reflects the interdisciplinary breadth of his work across Byzantine studies, medieval Latin history, Islamic art history, and Renaissance studies.

He is also a member of the Collegio dei Docenti del Dottorato di Ricerca in the Department of Humanistic Studies at Universita degli Studi Roma Tre, consolidating his ongoing connection to Italian academic life.

Tronzo's most celebrated and controversial contribution to medieval art history is his monograph The Cultures of His Kingdom: Roger II and the Cappella Palatina in Palermo (Princeton University Press, 1997). The study advanced a major reinterpretation of the Cappella Palatina, arguing that the building in its present form reflects two distinct planning projects with divergent aims in the twelfth century, rather than a single unified program of multicultural display. This thesis generated sustained debate because it challenged the long-dominant narrative of Norman Sicilian harmony and instead emphasized political tension and competing ideological agendas in the chapel's formation. The book remains a standard reference for scholarship on the monument.

His interpretation emphasizes not only decoration, but also patronage process: who commissioned what, in what sequence, and for what political purpose. This focus reflects his broader interest in the relationship between visual culture and power in the medieval world.

Beyond the Cappella Palatina, Tronzo has pursued sustained research on what he calls the hybrid culture of the Italian South, the multicultural civilization of Sicily and southern Italy from the Norman period through the court of Frederick II and into the early Renaissance. He has co-directed major projects on buildings, cities, and landscapes of the later medieval Italian South funded by the National Endowment for the Humanities (NEH), as well as a three-year program of research, seminars, and conferences on the medieval Mediterranean at the American Academy in Rome, funded by the Getty Foundation.

His monograph Petrarch's Two Gardens: Landscape and the Image of Movement (Italica Press, 2014) extends this inquiry into the early Renaissance, with a significant opening chapter on La Zisa and La Cuba in Palermo, surviving Fatimid-Norman garden pavilions of the twelfth century. Through these sites, Tronzo explores landscape as a social network and as a mediator of images. The study illustrates his characteristic method: beginning with a specific object or place to open broader questions of cultural exchange, representation, and the movement of ideas across time and space.

He has also published extensively on historiography and method in art history, examining how the discipline constructs narrative and what assumptions underlie scholarly judgment. For research on the art of the twelfth-century Mediterranean, including Monreale and the Cappella Palatina, Fatimid elements in Norman art, and the interplay of Byzantine, Arab, and Latin visual traditions, Tronzo is essential reading. If Jeremy Johns approaches Norman Sicily primarily through documentary and archaeological evidence grounded in Islamic studies, Tronzo approaches it through formal analysis and the interpretation of buildings and images; together, their work offers complementary lenses on Roger II's kingdom and its artistic legacy.

Van Marle, Raimond

The Hague, 1888 – Perugia, 1936

Dutch art historian who spent much of his career in Italy and produced the monumental eighteen-volume The Development of the Italian Schools of Painting (The Hague, 1923–1938), the most encyclopaedic survey of Italian painting from its origins to the sixteenth century ever attempted by a single scholar. Valagussa, Giovanni Francesco Maria

Milan, 1963 –

Giovanni Francesco Maria Valagussa (born 1963, Milan) is one of Italy's most distinguished art historians specializing in medieval and Renaissance painting, best known for his long tenure as curator of the Accademia Carrara in Bergamo and his fundamental contributions to the study of Italian Trecento and Quattrocento art.

Valagussa graduated in Lettere with a specialization in Art History from the Universita Cattolica del Sacro Cuore in Milan, where the eminent Hungarian-Italian medievalist Miklos Boskovits served as co-supervisor of his thesis. This formative relationship with Boskovits, one of the great connoisseurs of Italian medieval painting, would leave a lasting imprint on his scholarly approach. He then deepened his training at the prestigious Fondazione Roberto Longhi in Florence, studying under Mina Gregori, and subsequently completed his PhD at the Universita degli Studi di Torino under the guidance of Giovanni Romano. His educational path from 1982 to 1994 thus united three of Italy's most rigorous schools of art historical connoisseurship.

After a brief period teaching in secondary schools in Milan following his graduation, Valagussa entered the museum world by winning a competitive examination for the post of conservatore at the Museo Civico Ala Ponzone in Cremona (1998-1999), an experience he did not find entirely fulfilling. He subsequently held the role of conservator at the Accademia Tadini in Lovere (2000-2001), a small but significant institution on Lake Iseo, where he worked with great satisfaction. Simultaneously, beginning in 1998, he began teaching regularly at the Universita Cattolica, both in Milan and in Brescia, offering courses in art history and museology that he has continued to this day.

In 2001, Valagussa won the competitive selection for the position of conservator at the Accademia Carrara in Bergamo, a contest promoted by the then-director Francesco Rossi. He would remain in that role for over twenty years, until April 2022, becoming the institution's most enduring scholarly figure of the modern era. During the museum's lengthy closure for renovation (2008-2015), he was the principal curator of the Accademia Carrara's travelling exhibitions, bringing the collection to Lausanne, Rome, Brussels, Canberra, and Moscow, among other international venues. He also co-curated with Simone Facchinetti important publications on the collection, including catalogues covering Italian Renaissance and eighteenth-century painting.

Valagussa's most significant single publication is the Catalogo completo dei dipinti italiani del Trecento e Quattrocento dell'Accademia Carrara (Officina Libraria, 2018), the first volume of the museum's scientific catalogues. The work is notable for introducing a number of relevant new attributions and providing a complete bibliographic apparatus for the collection, making it an essential reference for anyone working on Italian medieval and early Renaissance painting. His attribution of a Virgin and Child (Forli, Pinacoteca Civica) to the Master of the Borgo Crucifix is among the scholarly contributions that have drawn international attention, cited even in the National Gallery's catalogues. Beyond the catalogue, he has published numerous essays and articles focusing primarily on medieval and Renaissance art.

Throughout his career, Valagussa has curated a wide range of major monographic exhibitions, including those dedicated to Fra Galgario, Giovanni Battista Moroni (Piccio), Carlo Ceresa, and Andrea Mantegna. In 2017, he co-curated the exhibition Serenissime Trame at the Ca' d'Oro in Venice. In 2021, he organized an exhibition of masterpieces from the Accademia Carrara in Shanghai, demonstrating the international reach of his curatorial work. He has also presented individual works for broader public audiences, including a presentation of Lorenzo Lotto's Madonna col Bambino e i santi Giovanni Battista e Caterina d'Alessandria for RAI Radio 3's Museo Nazionale.

Since January 2022, following the conclusion of his curatorial post, Valagussa has continued as a studioso associated with the Comune di Bergamo and remains a docente a contratto in the Department of History, Archaeology, and Art History at the Universita Cattolica. He is described by those who know him as a reserved and modest person (una persona schiva, modesta), dedicated entirely to scholarship rather than public visibility. Married with two children, he has spent much of his professional life in the Bergamo area, contributing enormously to the visibility and scholarly reputation of one of northern Italy's most important picture galleries. His expertise in the attributional study of medieval Italian painting, shaped by the Boskovits and Longhi traditions, makes him a key reference figure for researchers working on Trecento and Quattrocento art today.

Vasari, Giorgio

Arezzo, 30 July 1511 – Florence, 27 June 1574

Giorgio Vasari was an Aretine painter, architect, courtier and writer whose Le vite de' più eccellenti pittori, scultori e architettori effectively founded early modern art history and shaped how later generations understood Italian medieval and Renaissance art.

Vasari was born in Arezzo on 30 July 1511 into an artisan family and showed precocious artistic talent. A cousin, Luca Signorelli, recommended the boy, and Vasari first trained locally with the French glass-painter Guglielmo da Marsiglia, absorbing a solid craft and drawing discipline. Around sixteen he was sent to Florence under the patronage of Cardinal Silvio Passerini, entering the circle of Andrea del Sarto and his pupils Rosso Fiorentino and Pontormo, and receiving a humanist education that included Latin authors and Tuscan literary culture. In the early 1530s he travelled to Rome, where he studied both ancient remains and the works of Raphael and Michelangelo; this encounter with the High Renaissance and antiquity decisively marked his taste and his theoretical conception of artistic progress.

These formative years were peripatetic: Vasari worked between Arezzo, Florence, Rome, Venice and other centres, executing altarpieces, devotional pictures and decorative cycles. His first securely dated work is the Burial of Christ (1532) now in Casa Vasari at Arezzo, already reflecting Medicean patronage and a Mannerist vocabulary indebted to Rosso and Salviati. Short stays in Venice (1541–42) brought him into contact with Venetian colour and with patrons such as the Corner-Spinelli, for whom he painted allegorical ceiling decorations and works like Patience, Justice and Hope. This early career built his reputation as a versatile decorator capable of handling large narrative and allegorical programs in a courtly idiom.

From the 1540s Vasari's career became increasingly tied to the Medici court. After various commissions in Rome and Naples, he returned definitively to Florence in the 1550s at the service of Duke Cosimo I, becoming the principal designer and organizer of large-scale decorative enterprises celebrating Medicean power. His painting is best represented by the vast cycles in the Palazzo Vecchio, where he and his workshop frescoed the Salone dei Cinquecento and other rooms with scenes exalting Florence and the Medici dynasty, as well as by the Cento giorni fresco in the Palazzo della Cancelleria in Rome, with episodes from the life of Paul III.

As an architect Vasari produced projects in several cities, but his most celebrated work is the Palazzo degli Uffizi in Florence, begun in 1560 as the administrative "offices" of Cosimo I. The long, narrow courtyard opening toward the Arno, with its regular rhythm of columns and loggias, is one of the iconic urban spaces of late Renaissance Florence. He also designed the Vasari Corridor (1565), the elevated passage linking Palazzo Vecchio to Palazzo Pitti via the Uffizi and Ponte Vecchio, a striking expression of courtly control over the city's space. Other architectural achievements include interventions in Santa Croce, the arrangement of artistic spaces in the Palazzo Vecchio, and work on fortifications and villas for Medicean patrons.

Vasari maintained a house in Florence and built a richly decorated residence in his native Arezzo (1547 onwards), where he frescoed walls and ceilings with allegories of the arts and scenes celebrating his own career; the Casa Vasari is now a museum and important document of his self-fashioning as artist and man of letters.

Despite his substantial artistic output, Vasari's enduring fame rests principally on his literary work, Le vite de' più eccellenti pittori, scultori e architettori. First published in Florence in 1550 and then in a greatly expanded, three-volume edition in 1568, the Lives offer a sequence of biographies from Cimabue and Giotto to Michelangelo, prefaced by theoretical introductions in which Vasari advances a teleological history of Italian art. He divides this history into three ages: an "infancy" with Cimabue and Giotto, a "youth" with masters such as Donatello, Brunelleschi, Ghiberti and Masaccio, and a "maturity" or "perfection" embodied by Leonardo, Raphael and above all Michelangelo.

The Lives are a hybrid work, combining anecdote, workshop gossip, documentary facts, stylistic judgments and theoretical reflections about design, imitation and artistic progress. Vasari consistently privileges drawing (disegno), primarily Florentine, as the root of artistic excellence and tends to view Venetian colour with some suspicion, although the second edition gives more space and praise to Venetian masters than the first. He frames the arts as moving from decline after antiquity, through a medieval "dark age", to "rebirth" in Tuscany — a period he literally calls rinascita, providing one of the fundamental early formulations of the concept of the Renaissance.

From a historiographical point of view, the Lives have long been considered "the first important book on art history" and "perhaps the most famous, and still the most read, work of early art literature". Modern scholarship has, of course, identified many factual errors, biases and hagiographic distortions, especially in biographies of artists before Vasari's own time or outside his Florentine milieu. Yet the work remains an indispensable primary source for fourteenth- to sixteenth-century Italian art, not only for its anecdotes and data but also as a key text in constructing the very narrative of artistic "progress" that dominated European art history for centuries. Vasari is cited throughout this database as an early written witness for works and attributions that must be weighed critically against documentary and material evidence.

Vasari's position at the Medici court made him a central cultural mediator between artists, patrons and institutions. In 1563, together with Cosimo I and with Michelangelo as symbolic head, he helped found the Florentine Accademia e Compagnia delle Arti del Disegno, one of the earliest artist academies in Europe, which codified the status of painting, sculpture and architecture as liberal arts based on disegno and theory rather than mere manual trades. This institutionalization of the profession is inseparable from the broader program of the Lives to present artists as heroic, intellectual figures comparable to poets and philosophers.

In his last decade Vasari continued to work for the Medici, supervising the decoration of spaces such as the interior of Brunelleschi's dome, where he designed the immense fresco cycle of the Last Judgment (begun 1572), completed after his death by Federico Zuccari. He also produced late paintings such as Perseus and Andromeda for Francesco I de' Medici, showing his continued engagement with mythological allegory and courtly themes. Giorgio Vasari died in Florence on 27 June 1574 and was buried in Santissima Annunziata; by then he was widely admired as a successful "Renaissance man" who united artistic practice, writing and architectural invention.

From the seventeenth century onward, scholars, collectors and artists repeatedly returned to the Lives for information and inspiration, and modern editions and translations have made it a constant presence in art-historical education. For this reason later historians have aptly called Vasari the "father of art history", not because he was neutral or "objective", but because he provided the first sustained, internally coherent narrative of the development of the visual arts, one that continues to structure debates about Giotto, Cimabue, Masaccio and their successors even when we argue against him.

Venturi, Adolfo

1856 – 1941

Adolfo Venturi was an Italian art historian and connoisseur who fundamentally shaped the modern study of medieval Italian painting. Through his monumental nine-volume Storia dell'arte italiana and meticulous connoisseurship, Venturi established the foundational protocols for attribution and chronological sequencing of Giunta Pisano's works. His detailed study of signed crucifix panels demonstrated that thirteenth-century panel paintings were products of extraordinary technical sophistication and profound theological intention rather than primitive precursors to the Renaissance.

Venturi's comparative analysis of surviving works established the chronological development of Giunta's stylistic evolution and positioned him not as a marginal medieval craftsman but as a revolutionary figure whose innovations fundamentally reoriented medieval art scholarship and inaugurated the artistic transformations culminating in the Renaissance.

Venturi, Lionello

Modena, 25 April 1885 – Rome, 14 August 1961

Lionello Venturi was one of the greatest Italian art historians and critics of the twentieth century, distinguished both by the breadth and originality of his scholarship and by the civic courage with which he opposed fascism, refusing allegiance to the regime even at the cost of exile. His work spans medieval, Renaissance, and modern art, and remains foundational for the international historiography of art criticism.

Born in Modena to Adolfo Venturi and Giovanna Zanni, he grew up in an intellectually formative environment and moved early to Rome with his family. He graduated in Literature and Philosophy at La Sapienza in 1907 and in 1911 obtained his qualification to teach medieval and modern art history at the University of Padua. After graduation, he held a rapid sequence of museum and administrative posts: inspector of the Venice Galleries (1909-1910), inspector of the Galleria Borghese in Rome (1911-1912), and, at only twenty-eight, Director and Superintendent of the National Gallery of Urbino (1913-1914).

Between 1914 and 1915 he entered university teaching at Turin, where he was soon appointed associate professor. With Italy's entry into World War I he volunteered for military service; wounded in combat in 1917, he was discharged with a decoration for military valor. From 1915 to 1931 he held the chair of Art History at the University of Turin, where he trained a major generation of students including Giulio Carlo Argan and Mario Soldati. His teaching combined methodological rigor with sustained attention to both the aesthetic quality of the work and its historical context.

In October 1931, Venturi was among the very small group of Italian university professors who refused the oath of allegiance imposed by the fascist regime. The decision led to his immediate exclusion from university teaching in Italy. In 1932 he left for Paris, where he remained until 1939, developing contacts with the anti-fascist movement Giustizia e Libertà and teaching and lecturing in Paris, Lyon, London, and Cambridge. After the introduction of racial laws, he promoted the Italian section of the International League Against Racism and Anti-Semitism. With the outbreak of World War II he moved to New York (1939), taught at American universities, took leadership roles in the Mazzini Society, and chaired the Italian Emergency Rescue Committee.

His years in exile were also among his most productive intellectually. Major works include Histoire de la critique d'art (1936; expanded Italian edition, 1948), still a central text in the history of art criticism; the two-volume Cezanne (1936), which established him as a leading interpreter of modern painting; Archives de l'Impressionisme (2 vols., 1939), a key documentary corpus for Impressionist studies; and Come si guarda un quadro, one of the most widely read twentieth-century Italian books on aesthetic education. For medieval and Renaissance studies, Il gusto dei primitivi (1926) remains especially significant for the reassessment of early Italian painting.

After the Liberation, Venturi returned to Italy in 1945 and resumed teaching at La Sapienza, remaining there until 1955 and contributing decisively to the reconstruction of postwar Italian art-historical culture. He was elected to the Accademia dei Lincei, the Accademia di San Luca, and the Accademia delle Scienze di Torino, and became a corresponding member of the Institut de France. He died in Rome on 14 August 1961.

Walram of Naumburg

died April 12, 1111

Walram of Naumburg was one of the more intellectually active German bishops of the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries, positioned at the intersection of the great political and theological controversies of his age — the Investiture Controversy between the papacy and the Empire — and remembered also for his role in the documentation of the cult of Saint Leonard of Noblac.

Walram was appointed to the Diocese of Naumburg-Zeitz, in Saxony, around 1089, succeeding Bishop Günther I von Wettin, and was formally consecrated bishop around 1091 by Hartwig of Spanheim. He held the see until his death in 1111, when he was succeeded by Dietrich I von Naumburg. His episcopate was dominated by the bitter conflict known as the Investiture Controversy, the struggle between Pope Gregory VII (and his successors) and Emperor Henry IV over the right to appoint — or "invest" — bishops and abbots with the symbols of their office. Walram was a committed imperialist, firmly siding with Henry IV against the reforming papacy, and this political alignment shaped his entire career and literary output.

Walram's most significant intellectual contribution is the treatise Liber de unitate ecclesiae conservanda ("Book on the Preservation of the Unity of the Church"), attributed to him and published in a critical edition in 1883 by the Monumenta Germaniae Historica (Impensis bibliopolii Hahniani). This polemical work defended the imperial position in the Investiture Controversy, arguing that the unity of the Church required cooperation between the sacerdotium and the imperium — pope and emperor — rather than papal supremacy over temporal appointments. It is a document of considerable importance for understanding the ideological arguments marshalled in defence of Henry IV and stands as one of the more sophisticated pro-imperial theological texts of the period.

A preserved letter from Walram to Anselm of Canterbury — held in the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF, ark:/12148/btv1b10544536g) — reveals that he was engaged in theological correspondence with one of the greatest minds of the age. Anselm of Canterbury, though himself a reformist sympathetic to Gregorian ideals, maintained contacts across the theological spectrum, and Walram's letter to him testifies to the intellectual climate in which German bishops of the period operated, exchanging arguments and seeking legitimacy from renowned ecclesiastical figures even across political divides.

In 1103, Walram was present at the Abbey of Noblac in the Limousin (modern Saint-Léonard-de-Noblat, France) during the celebrated pilgrimage of Bohemond I of Antioch, the Norman crusader prince who had been captured by the Danishmend Turks and attributed his release to the intercession of Saint Leonard. This event was a moment of extraordinary publicity for Leonard's cult, drawing European-wide attention to the abbey. Walram seized the occasion to compose or commission a new hagiographic vita of Leonard, incorporating the miraculous liberation of Bohemond and other contemporary miracle accounts. The resulting text — whose authorship was later critically examined by the Bollandist Albert Poncelet, who questioned whether it was genuinely Walram's own composition or attributed to him posthumously — marked the moment when Leonard's veneration expanded from a regional Limousin devotion into a major European pilgrimage cult.

Walram died on April 12, 1111. His legacy is twofold: as a political theologian, he left the Liber de unitate ecclesiae conservanda as a testament to imperialist ecclesiology in the age of the Investiture Controversy; as a hagiographer, his presence at Noblac in 1103 and the vita associated with his name played a decisive role in amplifying the cult of Saint Leonard across Latin Christendom, making him an unexpectedly important figure in the history of medieval devotion to one of the most widely venerated saints of the central Middle Ages.

Ward, Benedicta (Florence Margaret Ward)

Durham, 4 February 1933 - 23 May 2022

Sister Benedicta Ward was one of the most significant medievalists of her generation: an Anglican contemplative nun who combined a life of strict monastic enclosure with a prodigiously productive career as a scholar of early Christian spirituality, hagiography, and medieval theology.

Benedicta Ward's intellectual formation began with undergraduate studies in history at Manchester University in the early 1950s. This period proved decisive in two respects: she discovered her vocation as a historian, and she converted from the Methodism of her northern English upbringing to Anglicanism and to the contemplative religious life. In 1955 she entered the Community of the Sisters of the Love of God (SLG), an Anglican religious order based at Fairacres, near Oxford, which at the time observed a rule of very strict enclosure. Far from stifling her intellectual energies, the community valued scholarly engagement with the breadth of inherited monastic wisdom, Eastern and Western, and eventually permitted select members to pursue academic work outside the convent walls.

By the early 1970s Sister Benedicta was allowed to pursue doctoral research at Oxford, and in 1972 she began her DPhil under Sir Richard Southern, then the foremost British authority on Saint Anselm of Canterbury and medieval intellectual culture. Her thesis, completed in 1978, examined miracles and miracle collections between 1015 and 1215, a characteristically bold topic that interrogated how ordinary medieval Christians, monks, pilgrims, and lay people alike, actually experienced and understood miraculous events. This study was published in 1982 under the title Miracles and the Medieval Mind and has remained in print.

Her association with Harris Manchester College, Oxford, where she served for many years as a Fellow and tutor, brought her into sustained contact with generations of theology undergraduates. She eventually held the title of Reader in the History of Christian Spirituality at the University of Oxford, a formal recognition of her standing among the most authoritative voices in the field in Britain. She lectured across the full sweep of pre-Reformation Christianity: medieval mysticism, Anglo-Saxon hagiography, the Desert Fathers, Anselm of Canterbury, Bede, Julian of Norwich, and Teresa of Avila.

Her most widely read and enduring single volume remains her 1975 translation of The Sayings of the Desert Fathers (Apophthegmata Patrum), a landmark rendering of the accumulated wisdom of the early Egyptian and Syrian hermits of the third and fourth centuries. Clear, lively, and scholarly, it became and remains one of the most accessible and dependable introductions to the subject in English, consulted by monastics and academics alike.

Her contributions to Bede studies were equally substantial. She produced several works on Bede and the psalter, a study of the Synod of Whitby, and a monograph on Bede as theologian and historian that illuminated the spiritual dimensions of his work beyond the familiar surface of his narrative chronicle. Her work on Anselm of Canterbury began almost simultaneously with her academic career: her translation of The Prayers and Meditations of Saint Anselm appeared as a Penguin Classic in 1973, the year after she began her DPhil, and introduced Anselm's interior devotional writing to a vast general readership.

Her approach to Julian of Norwich was particularly illuminating and exemplifies how her own lived experience as a cloistered nun sharpened her scholarly judgement. She challenged the prevailing assumption that Julian had been a nun, pointing out that Julian's writings contain no trace of the specifically cloistered life that Ward herself inhabited daily, a methodological insight that reshaped subsequent scholarship on the Norwich mystic.

What distinguished Sister Benedicta Ward from many of her academic contemporaries was the quality of inhabited understanding she brought to her subjects. She was not merely a historian writing about monasticism from the outside; she had lived under the rule for nearly seven decades when she died, and the rhythms of communal prayer, the experience of silence, and the discipline of lectio divina formed the living context within which she read the Desert Fathers, Bede, and Anselm. Along with the great French Benedictine scholar Jean Leclercq, she held that the intellectual revolution of 1150-1250 had displaced a complex, coherent pre-scholastic monastic culture, and as a contemplative she regarded that earlier culture as her own authentic home territory.

A commemorative volume, Prayer and Thought in Monastic Tradition: Essays in Honour of Benedicta Ward SLG, published in 2023, brought together twenty-two essays from international scholars spanning the full range of her interests, from Boniface's missionary zeal to the intercessory prayer traditions of both East and West, as a fitting testament to a life of learning lived entirely in service to understanding the deep roots of Christian spirituality.

Zappasodi, Emanuele

active 21st century

Emanuele Zappasodi is an Italian art historian based in Florence who specialises in medieval painting and visual culture in central Italy, particularly in Umbria and Tuscany. He completed his doctoral dissertation in 2015 at the Università degli Studi di Siena, with a thesis entitled Sorores reclusae: spazi di clausura e immagini dipinte in Umbria fra XIII e XIV secolo — a study of enclosed female religious spaces and the devotional images painted for them in Umbria between the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. This dissertation immediately signals the two axes that define his scholarship: the intersection of religious institutional life and visual art, and the geography of medieval Umbria. He obtained the Abilitazione Scientifica Nazionale (national qualification to teach as Professore Associato) in the sector 10/B1 (Storia dell'Arte) in October 2018.

Zappasodi's university career has unfolded entirely at the Università per Stranieri di Siena. He taught there as Professore a contratto from October 2020 to September 2021, before being appointed Ricercatore a tempo determinato di tipo B (the Italian equivalent of a tenure-track researcher) in the sector L-ART/01 — Storia dell'Arte Medievale — from December 2021. He was promoted to Professore Associato di Storia dell'Arte Medievale in August 2024, the post he currently holds in the Dipartimento di Studi Umanistici. Since July 2022 he has served as Vice-Coordinator of the doctoral programme in Art History of the Università degli Studi di Siena, demonstrating his early commitment to the formation of the next generation of medieval art historians. He is also a member of the governing council of CADMO, the interdisciplinary research centre for archaeology, diversity, and pre-Roman mobility at the Università per Stranieri di Siena.

Zappasodi's central scholarly contribution is a sustained, rigorous investigation into the painting of Umbria between the Duecento and the Quattrocento. His essay Il lungo percorso della pittura umbra del Duecento, noted on his Academia.edu profile, reflects his deep engagement with the slow, complex evolution of stylistic currents in medieval Umbria — a territory that has often been overshadowed by the more celebrated Florentine and Sienese traditions but which, as Zappasodi demonstrates, produced works of remarkable originality. His article Sulla prima maturità di Allegretto Nuzi, published in the prestigious journal Prospettiva (no. 188, October 2022), exemplifies his connoisseurship: Allegretto Nuzi was a Fabriano-born painter active in the mid-Trecento whose early career had received insufficient scholarly attention, and Zappasodi's study advances new proposals on the formation and chronology of his mature style.

A further dimension of Zappasodi's scholarship is his interest in the fortuna — the persistence and afterlife — of Romanesque forms in later medieval painting, as signalled by his essay Persistenze romaniche, published by the Università degli Studi di Firenze. This kind of study, which traces how archaic visual formulae were consciously preserved or revived in later centuries, places him in a productive dialogue with the historiography of stylistic continuity and rupture in Italian art. He has also engaged with technical art history: his article Descrivere la tradizione, codificare l'avanguardia: Cennino e la lavorazione dell'oro in tavola tra Trecento e Quattrocento, published in the journal Predella, examines how Cennino Cennini's famous Libro dell'arte codified goldwork techniques in panel painting, placing the treatise in the wider context of workshop practice at the turn of the fifteenth century.

Beyond his specialist research, Zappasodi is visibly committed to the public communication of medieval art history. In December 2025 he gave a lecture on the Beato Angelico at the Istituto Sangalli in Florence alongside Marco Campigli, an event that attracted considerable attention. His teaching at the Università per Stranieri di Siena — an institution whose mission is explicitly the promotion of Italian language and culture to international audiences — further reflects this orientation toward wide accessibility.

It is worth noting the remarkable institutional and scholarly parallel between Zappasodi and Elvio Lunghi. Both hold the rank of Professore Associato in L-ART/01 at one of Italy's two Università per Stranieri — Lunghi at Perugia, Zappasodi at Siena. Both focus on medieval painting in Umbria and central Italy, and both have contributed to understanding the same geographical and chronological territory — the painting of Umbria from the Duecento to the early Renaissance. Zappasodi represents, in many ways, the next generation of the tradition of which Lunghi has been the dominant figure for four decades.

Zimmermann, Max Georg

1861 – 1919

Max Georg Zimmermann was a German art historian whose scholarly career spanned the Wilhelmine period and whose engagement with Italian medieval and Romanesque art places him among the generation of German-speaking scholars who, in the decades around 1900, produced the first systematic monographic studies of northern Italian Romanesque sculpture and architectural decoration. Born on 1 June 1861 in Elbing (now Elbląg, Poland), he studied art history and classical archaeology in Berlin and Munich, completing his doctorate in Munich in 1885. From 1888 he taught as a lecturer at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, where he received the title of Professor in 1892. In 1893 and 1894 he undertook an extended research journey to Italy — the formative period for his direct engagement with Italian medieval monuments — before settling first in Bonn and then, in 1896, in Berlin. In 1900 he was appointed Privatdozent at the University of Berlin and, in the same year, full professor of art history at the Technische Hochschule Berlin (now the Technische Universität Berlin), the chair he held until his death on 10 July 1919. In 1912 he received the honorary title of Geheimer Regierungsrat and was appointed director of the Schinkelmuseum und Beuthsammlung in Berlin.

Zimmermann's scholarly output belongs to the tradition of German art-historical Denkmalkunde — the close descriptive and typological study of monuments — that flourished in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries alongside the more theoretical approaches of Alois Riegl and Heinrich Wölfflin. His Italian journey of 1893–94 gave him direct access to the Romanesque sculptural programmes of the Po valley, including the portals of Ferrara, Piacenza, and Verona, and it is from this engagement that his observations on the Ferrara Cathedral lintel derive. Among his published works is the topographical study Sizilien (Leipzig: E.A. Seemann, 1905), which demonstrates his broader interest in the art and architecture of the Italian peninsula. His critical observations on the lintel reliefs of Ferrara Cathedral — in particular his remark on the rendering of the infant Jesus in the Nativity scene, which he described as conveying a quality of deep, natural sleep with rare sculptural conviction — are characteristic of his method: close formal description attentive to the human and naturalistic registers within an otherwise hieratic Romanesque programme. Such observations anticipate the later interest of German and Italian scholarship in the specifically naturalistic strand within Nicholaus's œuvre, a strand that would be more fully theorized by subsequent historians of Romanesque sculpture.

Zimmermann worked at a moment when the systematic attribution of northern Italian Romanesque sculpture to named masters was still in its early stages, and his contributions to the study of Nicholaus and the Ferrara programme form part of the larger German-language effort — shared by contemporaries such as Arthur Kingsley Porter and, slightly later, Wilhelm Pinder — to map the geography of Romanesque sculptural workshops across Europe. Though his work has been largely superseded by twentieth-century monographic studies and the documentary reassessments of scholars such as Geza de Francovich and Roberto Salvini, it retains historical value as an early formulation of the critical vocabulary used to describe Nicholaus's formal language.
